MORPHOLOGICAL FEATURES:Body stout and fairly deep, its depth 3 to 3.7 times in standard length. Head relatively small, its length 3.5 to 4 times in standard length and shorter than (rarely equal to) body depth. Eyes visible from underside of head. Mouth small; lips fleshy. Barbels two pairs;maxillary barbels equal to eye-diameter, rostral pair shorter. Scales large.

 

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY: Tor tor is heterosexual. Sexual differentiation is made by secondary sexual characters during breeding season. Pathani (1978) recorded the following characters by which the male and the female can be distinguished. Size/Body Males are smaller and lighter than females of identical age. Body is narrower in males in comparison to that in female. Head length-body width relation in male is higher than in female during breeding season. Bulkiness of the abdomen for enlarged roes gives rise to an arched ventral profile in female. In male the abdomen is almost straight and the milt oozes on slight pressure on the abdomen. Fins Pectoral fins in male are rough in touch and comparatively longer reaching up to 6th lateral line scale. In female it is smooth and it reaches to the 5th scale. Anal fin in male isthinne transparent and larger with light orange tinge at the tip. In female of about same age it is thicker, opaque and smaller. According to Chaturvedi (1976) the pectoral reaches to 7th L.I. scale in males, and to 5th or 6th in female. Base of the anal fin projects out of profile line in female, whereas the profile is comparatively less arched and the base of the anal fin do not as much project out of profile line in the males. Tubercles Present on either side of the head of male, but absent in females (Desai, 1982- in case of Narmada R. tor) Body colour This is not a consistent character, though in some the body colour of male appears to be brighter than female' Sometimes small black spots are seen on the lateral sides of the mouth of male.

 

Breeding season: Day(1873), Beavan(1877), Thomas (1 897), Dunsford(1911), Nevill(1915), Sken-Dhu(1918), Spence and Prater (1933), Khan (1939a,b) and MacDonald (1948) recorded the spawning of mahseer in general and with a mention that this fauna breeds several times in a year. Beevan (1877) reported that when the rain begins tor commences moving up the streams for spawning purpose and breeds all at once in small batches during a period of several months, say from May to August. Thomas (1897), whereas, stated that mahseer lays a batch of eggs at a time, and repeats the process several times in a season, and probably there are three batches. Dunsford (1911) asserts that in the Kumaun lakes it most certainly breeds at the commencement of rains in July. While makillg commellt upon the breeding of mahseer Spence and Prater (1933) remarked that how many batches of eggs the mahseer lay in one season is not know. Nevill (1915) recovered female mahseer with well developed roes during several months of the year and observed the fish working out the gravel of eggs. Khan (1939 a , b) on the basis of his scientific studies comments that mahseer from hill streams of Kangra, Hoshirpur, Jhelum and Rawalpindi Districts of the Himalayan range seems to spawn firstly in winter (January-February); secondly, in May-June, when the snow melts and the rivers are swollen; and thirdly, July-September, when the rivers are flooded with monsoon rains; and thus mahseer fry of all sizes are available in these waters throughout the year. Codrington (1946) has spoken about two breeding season of mahseer, a minor season in January-February and a major season in August. Kaushik (1985) described that mahseer breeds throughout the year with three peak periods during January-February, May, June and July-September in the upper reaches of Jamuna in Ambala District. As regards the breeding of Tor tor a few references are only available. Horn (1940a) recorded the breeding of this species sometimes in August-September in the Himalayan rivers. Hora and Nair (1944 b) reported the breeding of tor mahseer in the Riyana belt stream in the Darjeeling Himalayas during March-April. According to Karamchandani et al. (1967) the Narmada tor mahseer has a prolonged breeding season commencing in July-August and continues up to December with peak breeding from July to September; and the individual fish breeds in three acts of spawning probably with an interval of2-3 weeks in between two acts. Chaturvedi (1976) stated that tor mahseer from Udaipur lake and its connecting streams breeds only once in a year during a short spawning period that extends from July to September, with its peak in August. Desai (1982) from the gross microscopic examination of gonads; availability of 57.7% ripe females during July-September; estimation of monthly gonadosomatic index as 11.105 (June), 29.131 (July), 22.460 (August), 11.311 (September), 14.088 (October) and 4.563 (November); and the ova diameter measurements as 0.808 mm (June), 0.942 mm (July), 1.211 mm (August), 1.253 mm (September), 0.646 mm (October) and 0.633 mm (November) inferred that the Narmada tor has a prolonged breeding season commencing in July-August and continuing up to February-March, with the peak from July-September, and that individual fish spawans more than once in each spawning season. The above data reveals that tor mahseer has post-monsoon spawning (July/ August-November/December) at least in the rivers non- fed by snow-meltwater, whereas it has both post-monsoon and pre-monsoon (February-April/May) spawning in the streams which receive also slow-melt water during the certain part of the year. The flood of clean water in the upland streams accompanied by drop in the temperature is essential' prerequisite for spawning of this species. The optimum condition of temperature for spawning is reported to have a great variability in the Himalayan waters from4-28°C (Kaushik, 1985) and in the Narmada river from 19.9-28.4°C (Desai, 1982). Tor tor do not breed in confined waters of ponds, though it spawns in lakes and its connecting streams, where the fluvetile conditins prevail.

 



Breeding techinique: The successful artificial breeding has been achieved by stripping method and seed are produced (Kulkarni, 1980).

 


Fishing methods:From the Himalayan waters mahseer fishing is done by "Kadh" (using bagnet in conjugation with stakenet); "Chip" (specially designed traps), gillnet, castnet and long lines. Chip is a method of trapping mahseer during September-October when spent fish after spawning migrates down-streams (Jhingran and Sehgal, 1978b). Dragnets in conjugation with stake and castnets are effectively used during winter months when water level in deep pools of the streams are lowest. In Narmada, tor mahseer forms the major fishery of castnet and gillnet throughout the year, except in monsoon. The long lines fishery is insignificant in the Decan plateau.

 



BIOLOGY: The mahseer of Kumaun Himalayas (Tor tor and Tor putitora) are famous throughout the world, specially for sports to the anglers on the rod and lines. The bait-fishing, spoon-fishing and spinning-fishing (combination of spoon and fly-fishing) are commonly employed. Both the live and dead baits of fly and insects nymph, which accepts nicely by the fish, are commonly used for sports fishing. Tor mahseer is also regarded as an important coldwater food fish. T. tor  possesses a stream-lined body with very large and powerful fins. The size of the fins together is greater in area than the total superficial area of the rest of their body, which gives a strong power in locomotion through the torrential current of hilly streams. Their suctorial mouth with modified lips are suitable for removing periphytic organisms and algal slime off the submerged/emergent rocks and boulders. The shape of the pharyngeal teeth is well adopted for tearing and masticating grasses, aquatic plants, molluscan shells, etc. For their extensive feeding habits on underwater rooted vegetation and algae in natural habitat, they are considered as a very promising agent for biological control of submerged weeds. Mahseer builds nest by scooping gravel in rocky terrain. They perform migration in a limited range for the perpose of breeding, feeding or wintering. The limit of such migration is determined by them by water temperature and floods. Unlike other coldwater fishes tor  mahseer has normal sized gill chambers, and thus they can also able to adopt easily in the normal moderately low land waters. They can not tolerate least salinity. They can stand long distance transport for large yolk-sac, long hatching period and big size of hatchling.

The auditory sacs were seen with otoliths. Eyes were fully developed with minute melanic dots on the peripherial ring. The pectoral fin buds were discernible. Within 76-85 hours after fertilisation most of the eggs were hatched, though a few were logging behind even up to next day. Economic Importance

Uses: This species has afforded a lucrative source of sport for the anglers in the northern Indian region since long. It is also often called the red-finned mahseer and deep-bodied mahseer. It is a powerful mahseer and is one of the important food-fishes in our region.The voracious feeding habit of this fish (particularly higher submerged plants) can be utilised in biological control of water weeds (Tilak and Sharma, 1982).

Miscellaneous Information

In Madhya Pradesh, particularly in the Narmada river near Hoshangabad and in Tapti near Barhanpur, mahseer figured prominently in the commercial catches about 20 years ago but the landings are reported to have dwindled remarkably in recent years. In the lakes in Rajasthan as well as in the Chambal river between the Ganga-Sagar and Rana Pratab Sagar Reservoirs, the species occurs in fairly good numbers. In Assam the populations are fast depleting and are presently localised to certain major river systems. In Punjab this species ascends rivers during March-April. As a general rule, T. tor travels towards headwaters at the beginning of rains and downstream when rain cease.
