MORPHOLOGICAL FEATURES:Body oblong and strongly compressed. Head compressed, its length about 4.2 times in standard length; preorbital smooth. Mouth large, maxilla exten considerably beyond hind edge of eye. Dorsal fin inserted nearer to base of caud fin than to snout-tip. Pectoral fins moderate, extend to anal fin. Scales on opercles, of equal size as on body.

Breeding season:. Chital is a seasonal spawner and is known to breed during summer (April-May) and monsoon months (~une-August). They have rather a prolonged period of breeding in riverine conditions, commencing from April- May. In Assam, the spawning season starts from April-May where monsoon breaks rather earlier. While in the river Ganges the spawning extends from the end of May to the middle of July at Bauxar of Bihar (Southwell and Prasad, 1918). In the Mahanadi, based on the availability of72 mm sized fingerlings during September-October, the breeding is anticipated in July (Job et a/., 1955). Chital breeds also in lentic environment. In pond, its spawning commences immediately after the early monsoon rains following dilution of pond water to some extent at least. Singh et a/. ( 1980) recorded its spawning in ponds at Cuttack (Orissa) in June-July after the early rains when the pond water was only slightly diluted and still contained a dense of Microcystis bloom. It breeds in the shallow or moderately deep water near the banks where some hard submerged objects or masonry structures are available to deposit eggs. The breeding habits of chital in the river Ganga (Southwell and Prasad, 1918) and in ponds (Singh et al 1980) are described in detail. In the river, while shedding eggs, female lies close to the object on which the ova are to be deposited first, the body of the fish being inclined at a certain angle to the vertical. The ova, being glutinous, adhere firmly to the object. The male, later on, emits the milt over the deposited eggs. 300 to 500 eggs are usually-laid at a time and the nest is very carefully protected by the parent fish(es). It is not known whether the parental care continues to be on their fry (Southwell and Prasad, 1918). According to Singh et a/. (1980) its natural spawning in the pond is a common factor. It occurs only after rains. A large quantity of eggs are found sticking to stones, brick-walls, tin-carriers, wooden box or any other hard objects lying submerged at the comer/sides of the pond. The spawning appears to be profuse inside tin carrier with stone blocks. The hard substratum of the stone as well as the room inside the tin carrier provide better environment and protection to the eggs as nest. No deposition of eggs is observed on the aquatic weeds. About 100 eggs/25 cm2 surface of the substratum could be obtained; 60% of the eggs being fertilised. However, the large scale raising of seed is easily possible by careful rearing of brood stock, natural breeding in confined waters, collection of egg mass along with artificial hard objects provided for attachment of eggs, and incubating eggs and rearing larvae under controlled laboratory or yard conditions.



Breeding techinique: The hypophysation technique can also be applied in chital for production of pure seed, following the method adopted by Mobarak (1980) in induced breeding of N. notopterus.

BIOLOGY: The posterior part of the body of chital and pholui looks feather-like and hence they are commonly known as 'Feather-backs' and occassionly as 'Rajor Fish' or 'Knife fish'  The air-bladder of chital is long and thick and sub-divided internally at the centre by a partition wall. Posteriorly it is prolonged on either side of the haemal spine half the way to the caudal fin (Day, 1889). Chital is called as facultative air-breather for accessory respiratory function. Peculiarities are found in the nervous system of chital. The eurydendroid cells also exist in the metencephalon. These cells comprise a large axon and several short dendrites. Axon enters the granular layer neurons. The dendrites synapse with those of Purkinje cell and also with axon of the neurons of molecular layer (Goel, 1983).The digestive system of chital is suitably modified as predacious feeding habits. The specialised type of dentition (such as, numerous small teeth on maxillary, mandibular, vomarine and palatine bones; backwardly directed large teeth on both the jaws; and pharyngeal teeth on the fifth branchial arch) and highly developed teeth-like gill-rakers are rightly fit for holding fast and giving good grip to prey. The opening of the large mouth through the short oesophagus  into a highly developed pear-shaped stomach enables to ingest easily larger food materials like small fish, insects, etc and digest quickly. The stomach is without any blind sac and it leads to a short single-coiled intestine. Two very long pyloric appendages with transverse rugae internally and one rectal caecum are the characteristic features. The wall of the alimentary canal from oesophagus to rectum consists of mucosa, sub-mucosa, muscularis and serosa. Stomach possesses a single layer of columnar epithelium and a broader muscular coat. In the columnar epithelium of the intestine, a number of mucous cells are present, while the rectum is devoid of such cells. The buccal cavity lining consists of stratified epithelium, sub-epithelial connective tissue and muscularies. The high rate of mucous lubrication from the abundant mucous cells present in the buccul cavity, pharynx and oesophagus helps to soften the prey before entering the food into the stomach. Presence of a major part of cardiac stomach suggests a greater need for digestive activity of the animal food ( Shanna and Chandy, 1961).

Stages of development:  The embryonic development and hatching of eggs are slow and prolonged in chital. Egg takes about two weeks to hatch out (South well and Prasad, 1919). Under natural condition in the pond at 30-33°C water temperature the hatching is found nearly 24 hours earlier than that in the laboratory. The history of embryonic and larval development of chital given here is based on the descriptions of South well and Prasad (1919) and Singh et al. (1980). Embryonic development The fully swollen fertilised eggs measuring about 5.0-5.2 mm diameter with surface projections at its one part possess developing embryo inside the capsule. Almost spherical massive yolk-sac gradually takes somewhat oblong shape in advanced stage and a fully developed embryo looks like a piece of red thread with large yolk-mass. The embryo shows an occassionaly and very slow movement at an interval of about 30 seconds. In a fairly advanced stage, the embryo (7.1 mm) ,grown in length lies in a slightly coiled position over the yolk-sac and possesses a continuous embryonic fin-fold. The head is differentiated but still attached to the yolk-sac ventrally. The pigmentation on the eye is yet to appear. The ear is formed. In ventral and posterior to the eye, the heart is appeared, its demarcation into chambers has already commenced but has not yet sufficiently marked for the various divisions to be identified. The straight notochord, slit-like opening of the mouth, straight tube-like alimentary canal with rudiment of liver mass, small somewhat oval-shaped air-sac and the rudimentary branchial arches are visible. The myotomes are 57 in number (Fig. la). In the next advanced stage (Fig. I b), the developing embryo do not have anything special to mark, except the lobes of the brain being better marked,myotomes better developed, myosepta with more wavy outline, and eye and air-bladder more distinct. In the stage of hatching (Fig. I c), the head and the rest of the body, with large yolk-sac, are wriggled out of the egg capsule by quick violent movements of the tail (tail to come out first). Miscellaneous Information

 This species may be distinguished from its congener, its notopterus by its strongly humped dorsal profile which declines gradually fro the front to the tail. This species attains a length of 122 cm, but mark, specimens are about 26 cm long. Swamps yield considerable quantities of the species in Indian area where it is captured with seines.The catches of this species are fast declining in India. Though bionomics and breeding N. chitala have been investigated by several workers, no worthwhile atteni] towards its culture has been made.
