MORPHOLOGICAL FEATURES: Body elongate and fairly rounded in cross-section. Eyes moderate, its diameter 7 to 8 times in head length (eyes larger in young). Mouth large; lower jaw with 7 to 18 canines behind a single row of villiform teeth which deepens to 5 or 6 rows at symphysis; teeth on vomer, absent on palatines. Pectoral fins about half head length; pelvic fin about 75% of pectoral fin length. Caudal fin rounded. Scales on summit of head of moderate-size, rosette of head-scales lies between orbits, frontal head-scale occupying central portion of rosette; two scales between rosette and basal head-scale; 10 scale-rows between preopercular angle and hind border of orbit; predorsal scales 16; scales 60 to 70 in lateral series.

 

 

 

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY:. C. marulius is heterosexual. Sexual dimorphism is noticeable, female is much larger than male (Alikunhi, 1947). Otherwise the sexes can be separated during the breeding season when the secondary sexual characters are developed. The slit-like vent appears pale in male. The vent is round, prominent and reddish on the slightly bulging abdomen of female. The abdomen of mature male is not bulging, but milt is extruded on slight pressure on it near the vent.

 

Breeding season:Diversified views are present about the frequency and season of spawning of C. marulius. The ova diameter frequency polygons of mature ovary of the giant murrel from U.P. exhibiting distinct groups of ova indicate the fractional spawning (Qasim and Qayyum, 1961), and each breeder may spawn repeatedly during the single spawning season in northern India. This species normally breeds in rivers, reservoirs, lakes, swamps, jheels, bee Is and large tanks during monsoon months, but Hora and Pillay (1962) said about the breeding of saal in ponds also without particular measures. Its breeding in the northern Indian waters coinciding the premonsoon and monsoon months of the south-west rain~ are reported to be during April to July in Punjab (Khan, 1924), April to June (Alikunhi, 1957; Hora and Pillay, 1962), and June to October in Uttar Pradesh (Qasim and Qayyum, 1961). In some states of south India its spawning season appears twice, coinciding the south-west and north-east rains. Chacko (1956) recorded spawning of the giant murre I in south India rivers twice in a year, once during March-June and the next during October-December, when the normal temperature of the breeding grounds ranges between 28°C and 31°C. Arumugam (1966) stated that saal breeds also without monsoon rains, twice in a year during March-June and October-December in Tamil Nadu waters. Devaraj (1973) recovered only one distinct group of maturing ova from the specimens in swamps and reservoir of Bhabanisagar, showing a likelyhood single spawning in a season, when all mature ova is shed out in single act of spawning. Parameswaran (1975) explained that the spawning season seems to be determined by the type of monsoon rain that influences it. With the south-west monsoon break when the river and reservoir begin to be flooded by rains in the catchment during May-July the Bhabanisagar river and reservoir population of C. marulius do spawn. On the other hand, the spawning in the swamp takes place during October- February when it swells up due to local south-west rain. The progression of ova through successive stages of maturity and is an intermittant breeder and spawns at least twice during the breeding season in this area. In Sri Lanka, the spawning season of C. marulius is from April to June (Deraniyagala, 1929).

Fishing methods: Long lines and hooks and lines with live baits as fish and frogs are mainly used for fishing. Gillnetting also gives considerable catch from lotic environment. Fairly good catch is obtained by castnetting over the broods while guarding their youngs. Bailing out of shallow water of the tank during extreme summer months and hand picking ofmurrels is a common method practised in Bengal, Assam, North Bihar and peninsular India and in Bangladesh. A traditional method of fishing murrels is of spreading a net over a shoal and keeping it for a while to make the fishes initially inactive for want of atmospheric respiration, and then the entire lot is rather netted out easily. Specially deviced 'Murrel Brood Trap' (Arumugam, 1966) is used for catching murrel fry or brood from deeper river sections, paddy fields, etc.

 



BIOLOGY:Murrel is the popular name of Ophiocephalids. The latin nomenclature'Ophiocephalus' means snake-headed fish. Ophiocephalids are exclusively freshwater origin, perhaps in the region of Yunan in south China where the marshy waters of Siwalik gulf extends during the Pliocene or earlier (Chandy, 1955). Of the 21 species under the genus Ophiocephalus India possesses II species, and O. gachua is the most generalised species with a hypothetical ancestor. Other bigger-sized murrels might have been evolved with some morpho-taxonomic;al changes, including the development of the complexed supra-branchial organ. The accessory respiratory organs of the pharyngeal region are well developed in C. marulius and these serve as air- sacs to breath by atmospheric oxygen. It is a simple cavity, without an accessory branchial organ, the opening of which is partly closed by a fold of the mucous membrane (Gunther, 1963). The manner of participation of the fish in the respiratory function is unknown. For amphibious mode of respiration, the murrel can live for lengthened period out of water. During draught they get buried in the slush and hibernate, and come out again on the onset of rain. In adverse conditions, they crawl over the moist land to a certain distance and reach to an adjacent water body to live. As for peculiar habit murrels periodically stir up the bottom sediments and secreate a quantity of mucus from the body when they appear to be delighted, their colours become vivid and then they take to other favourite resort just beneath the surface of the water amongst the weedy margins. When the water is settled and clear they become excited and again change their resort (Arumugam, 1966). They often secreate a good amount of slime over their body, specially when excited.

Economic Importance

Uses: A favourite sporting fish, it can be be caught with ladle or spoon but is apt to retire to the bottom after surface fight.The fish is esteemed as food. Predaceous; eggs are laid in nests guarded by parents; breeding almost throughout the year.It is avoided in culturable waters because of its carnivorous habit.

Miscellaneous Information

It attains a length of 122 cm; common about 46 cm. The fish is cultured in irrigation wells in parts of South India. Hatchlings are collected from cup-like nets; they are stocked in shallow irrigation wells and fed on kitchen refuse, frogs and dead animals and thus attain a length of 30 cm in one year. Only about 10% survive and hence annual stocking is necessary.
