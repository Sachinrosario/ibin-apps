MORPHOLOGICAL FEATURES:Body streamlined, oblong and somewhat compressed, with both dorsal and ventral profiles greatly arched, its depth 1.1 to 1.4 times in standard length. Head broadly pointed, its length more than depth of body and 3 to 3.6 times in standard length.eyes not visible from underside of head. Mouth small; lips fleshy and continuous at corners of mouth.

 

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY: Putitor mahseer has sexual dimorphism. Male and female sex can be separated by the following secondary sexual characters developed during breeding season (Pathani, 1978) : Size/Body Males are smaller and lighter than females of the same age. Depth of body in ripe females is about equal to the length of head, whereas in ripe males the head length is smaller than the depth of body. Fins. # Pectoral fins are rough in males and smooth in females. The length of pectoral fin in both the sex is found to be largely variable, and thus, this character is not taken as a dependable one. Anal fin is smaller than pectoral and more opaque in females; whereas in male the anal is equal or sub equal to pectoral and more transparent. Body streak A conspicuous growing body streak appears in males during breeding season but it is absent in females. The streak originates in front of the lips of the maxillary bone and extend posteriorly along and below the eye-ball up to opercular bone on both the sides of the head. Body colour Males are brighter than females in general appearance. Anal fin of female is brighter than that of male. However, the colour characters are not much reliable since these are variable due to different conditions of the eco-system.

 

Breeding season: Conflicting views have  been expressed regarding  the spowning period of T. putitora.. MacDonald (1948) expressed his opinion about three times breeding of mahseer in a year. On the basis of several batches of eggs at all stages of maturity in the ripe ovary of T. putitora, Qasim and Qayyum (1961) remarked that this species seems to be a non-seasonal breeder breeding over a greater part of the year in the river Ganges, Yamuna and Kali. From the frequency polygons of ova diameter Bhatnagar (1964) expressed that T. putitora of Bhakra reservoir release eggs in several batches during a greater part of the year resulting in prolongation of the spawning season. Basing on observtions on the natural collection offertilised eggs and hatchlings, Sehgal et al. (1971) reported two breeding seasons: (i) first during May-June and (ii) second during August-September in Baner and Jogal tributaries of the river Beas. Sunder and Joshi (1976, 1977) observed spawning of 7:putitora in the streams of Yamuna during August-September, and in Anji stream of Jammu in August at a distance of 3-8 m above the confluence of the Anji with R. Chenab. The requisite conditions for spawning, as stated by them, are cloudy day with occassional showers and preceeding day with heavy rainfall. The physio-chemical conditions of the spawning ground were noted as : water temp. 23.5-26.5°C, depth 44-53 cm, transparency 4.7-6.0 cm, pH 6.8-6.9, DO 8.0-8.4 ppm, free CO2 nil, total alkalinity 58-64 ppm and silicates 0.81-1.25 ppm. Pathani and Das (1980) described intermittent spawning behaviour of put it or mahseer in Bhimatallake during may to September. Tandon (1981) by scale studies and personal negociation with local fishermen concluded that there appears to be two breeding seasons (May-June and July-October) of this species in Himachal Pradesh, the peak season falls between the first week of August and the second week of September. Putitor mahseer performs the spawning migration from the main rivers to the tributaries and breeds in the flood phase. They lay eggs amidst stones, pebbles, gravels, etc., in the shallow areas of the streams (Shaw and Shebbeare, 1.937); in the water depth 30-40 cm (Sehgal et al., 1971). Sehgal and Jhingran (1978b) recorded the physico-chemical conditions of the water of the spawning grounds of7: putitora as : depth, 41-73 cm; temperature, 19.7 -20.8°C; transparency, 10-15 cm;..pH, 8.3-8.4; dissolved oxygen, 9.6-9.9 ppm; and total alkalinity, 61-69 ppm during May-June; and depth, 10- 12 cm; temperature, 26.0-27.5°C; transparency, 3.0-5.0 cm; pH, 8.4-8.6; dissolved oxygen, 13.0-16.8ppm; and total alkalinity, 58.0-66.2 ppm during September. The average number of eggs and fry obtained per square meter area of the stream were 30-37 and 110-115'during the above two breeding periods, respectively.



Breeding techinique:The artificial breeding of T. putitora has been successfully made in Bhimtal by 'wet stripping method' in August 3-4, 1976 (Tripathi, 1977) and 'dry stripping method' in July 11, 1982 (Joshi, 1981). The size range of the breeders used were 360-470 mm/365-1500g. The rate offertilisation of eggs was 50- 80% and 80-100% in the first and the second occassion, respectively. The hatching of eggs was made in the laboratory in trays with provision of mild flow of water having temperature 16-25°C

 


Fishing methods:The experimental fishing for T. putitora  by using gillnets in Bhimtallake constituted 44.6% of the total catch of the lake in 1970-71, against 27% in 1971-72, which by weight estimated as 385 kgand415 kg respectively for the two years (Sehgal and Vass, 1979). 'Chip' and 'Kadh' fishing in the Baner stream of Himachal Pradesh during 1964-67 (Jhingran and Sehgal, 1978b) shared putitor mahseer 5.2% to 39.2% of the total landings. The sport fishing of T. putitora is made by rod and lines. The bait fishing, spoon fishing and spinning are commonly employed for its sport as detailed in the Chapter Tor tor. The method of put it or seed collection is same as that of tor mahseer.

 



BIOLOGY:Putitor mahseer is the premier sporting fish of India, but little is known of its general biology. It is a very strong and hardy fish, and it can stand long-distant transport. Its strongly-built stream-lined body and powerfull ocomotary organs provides quicker movement against the torrential current of the hilly streams. It is a local migrant fish. It performs normally seasonal migration for breeding, feeding or wintering. The limit of such migrations are dertermined by water temperature and floods. Putitor has nest-making habit, which they do in1he form of depression on the river bottom before their spawning. It is a gaint size fish and attains as big as 2710 mm (Hamilton, 1922). Pathani (1980) recorded some important morphological and anatomical characters of a 18.50 kg female putitor as TL, 1260 rom; FL, 1170mm; SL, 1100 rom; body length, 783 mm; HL, 317 rom; eye dia., 31 mm; body depth, 310 mm; and length of ovary, 355 mm. The alimentary canal is not much long and coiled. The length of gut is about 2 times of the length of the fish. The intestinal bulb is long, about 1/6th of the alimentary canal. The pharyngeal teeth is well developed and strong enough to crush fish bones and molluscan shell and chew hard vegetations. Putitor has suctorial  mouth like T. tor. Its modified lips are suitable for browsing periphyta and algal slimes of the submerged substrata.

Stages of development: The descriptions given here on different larval stages of put it or mahseer are based on the samples of natural collections from Narmada river (Desai, 1972), Himalayan waters (Sehgal, 1978) and tray-bred ones under laboratory conditions (Tripathi, 1977; Joshi, 1981). Th~ incubation period of fertilised eggs is 80-98 hours at 21°C water temperature (Tripathi, 1977) and 58-192 hours at l6-25°C water temperature in the hatching trays under laboratory condition with a mild flow of water (Joshi, 1981). In the process of hatching, the tail comes out first and gradually the hatchlings wriggle out of the egg- shells. Miscellaneous Information

 It is the most common mahseer of the Himalaya and is also sometimes known as the golden, yellow-finned, grey-hound or the thick- lipped mahseer. The commercial fishery of Putitor mahseer in Jammu, Himachal Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh consists largely of individuals either ascending streams for breeding or the spent ones returning to perennial ponds in the plains. In Assam the population of Tor are fast depleting and at present are chiefly localised to certain major river systems only.It is fast approaching extinction in the streams and lakes of northern India. Large fishes are only found in some of the perennial pools.
