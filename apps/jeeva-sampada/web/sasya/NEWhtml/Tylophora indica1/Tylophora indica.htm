HABIT & HABITAT : 
A branching climber, 1.5 m. high, found in Assam, West Bengal, Orissa and the peninsular India, ascending to an altitude of 900 m.; also occurs in the Car Nicobar islands.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES : 
A branching climber, 1.5 m. high, Rootstocks short, knotty, 3.2 mm. thick; roots numerous, fine, 15 cm. long, very brittle, consisting of fibers or fibrils; leaves ovate to orbicular, cordate-based 3-10 cm. long, thick: flowers greenish yellow out purplish within, in many-flowered umbels; follicles fusiform, divaricate, 5 cm. ><10 cm., striate; seeds ovate, elongated into 2.0-2.5 cm. long coma.

Other species:
T. mollissima Wight :
Is a herb, with tomentose stem, oblong or linear-oblong leaves and few-flowered umbels, found in the Kodaikanal, Nilgiri, Paini and Sirumalai hills, ascending to an altitude of 2,100 m. The herb is eaten.
T. rotundifolia Buch-Ham. ex Wight:
Is a trailing herb found in the upper Gangetic plains, Bihar, Assam and in some parts of the peninsular India, up to an altitude of 1,500 m., and in Nepal. The plant is reported to possess medicinal properties.

Cultivation:
Tyiophora can be propagated by seeds and vegetatively through cuttings. However, as the seeds have poor germination capacity, it is preferred to propagate it by cuttings for commercial cultivation. To propagate it vegetatively, cuttings of finger thickness with 6" length stem having two nodes are used. The cuttings are planted in raised beds or in polybags to get greater success. The rooting of cutting takes about 8-10 weeks and cuttings will be ready for planting in the main field after about 10-12 weeks. Planting Before planting, the land should be ploughed 2 to 3 times and levelled properly. The usual planting time is during the onset of mansoon in May-June. Rooted cuttings are planted in main field in 30 cm3 pits at a spacing of 1 m x 1m. Tyiophora can be propagated by seeds and vegetatively through cuttings.  Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used : 
Plant, Leaves & Roots.

Uses

  1. The roots have a sweetish taste turning acrid, pleasant aromatic odour, and a brittle fracture. 
    They possess stimulant, emetic, cathartic, expectorant, stomachic and diaphoretic properties and are used for the treatme asthma, bronchitis, whooping-cough, dysentery and,diarrhoea. 
  2. They are reported to be given in rheuinatic and gouty pains. 
  3. The roots are said to possess bacteriostatic properties and have been suggested to be a good natural preservative of food. 
  4. The climber is reported to yield a fine, silky and strong fibre. It may be useful in the manufacture of extra-fine fabrics.

Harvesting Practices
The leaf is the economic part. The first harvesting can be done after 3-4 months of planting and subsequently at two monihs interval. While harvesting, care should be taken in pluck the leaves without damaging the vines. These leaves are then spread on a clean floor for drying. In about a weeks time, when they are fully dried they are packed in polythene lined bags for further marketing. An average yield of about 5000 kg of dried leaves can be obtained from one hectare in an year.

Chemistry
The powdered leaves, stem and root contain 0.2-0.3 per cent (up to 0.42-0.46%) of alkaloids. The alkaloidal content is not affected by seasonal variations. The aIkaloids present are tyiophorine (C24H2704N; m.p.286-87° decomp.) and tyiophorinine (C23H25O4N; m.p.248-49°). Both the alkaloids are pharmacologically active, and are reported to have caused dermatitis, producing itching, redness, swelling and eruptions on the skin. Tyiophorine is toxic to Paramoecium caudatum in concentrations of 1 in 50,000 or more. The MLD for frogs is 0.4 mg./g. body-wt. Its toxicity for mice and guineapigs is very low. It has no irritant action locally or when injected subcutaneously or intramuscularly. It has a marked stimulating effect on both the striped and unstriped muscles, and a distinct depressing effect on the musculature of heart. The blood-pressure is lowered when a dose is administered but is raised soon after and is maintained at a level higher than the normal for a fairly long time.
A new potential antitumour alkaloid, tyiophorinidine (C22 H 23O4; m.p. 213-14° decomp.), has recently been isolated from the roots of three-year old plants in 0.009 per cent yield. Three minor alkaloids, designated alkaloid. A (C2aH230gN; m.p. 250-52° decomp.), alkaloid B CaHsgOiN; m.p. 235-37° decomp.), and alkaloid C ( CliHg^N; m.p. 253-55° decomp.), have also been reported from the plant. Alkaloid C showed significant activity in murine leukemia (L 1210 system) at a dose of 6-8 mg./kg. In a more recent investigation, tyiopho- rinidine and two other bases, identified as (+)-septicine (CMHggOiN; m.p. 136°) and (+)-isotylocrebrine (C^HayOiN; m.p. 212-14°), have been isolated from the plant. Extracts of leaves and stem showed significant anti-cancer activity against two standard transplantable tumours lymphoid leukemia L 1210 and lymphocytic leukemia P 388 in mice.
Other constituents reported in the plant (leaves, stem and root) include ceryl alcohol, a phytosterol (m.p. 192-93°), a substance of an alcoholic nature (m.p. 89- 90°), wax., resin, caoutchouc, tannin, glucose, calcium salts, and potassium chloride. The leaves also contain c-amyrin, quercetin, kaempferol, and an unidentified flavonoid. Steam distillation of an alcoholic extract of the air-dried root powder gave 0.26 per centof R-methoxy salicylaldehyde, combined with a small amount of oily matter.

Other Uses
  1. The roots have a sweetish taste turning acrid, pleasant aromatic odour, and a brittle fracture. They possess stimulant, emetic, cathartic, expectorant, stomachic and diaphoretic properties and are used for the treatment of asthma, bronchitis, whooping-cough, dysentery and diarhoea; they are reported to be given in rheumatic and gouty pains. The roots are said to possess bacteriostatic properties and have been suggested to be a good naturalpreservative of food. 
  2. The roots and leaves are also reported to be used in hydrophobia. Theleaves are employed to destroy vermin.
  3. The leaves and roots have often been employed as a substitute for ipecacuanha; the dried leaves are reported to be more uniform and certain in their action than the roots. In large doses, the dried leaves may cause fatal poisoning.
  4. Recently, the leaves of the plant have attracted attention in the treatment of bronchial asthma. Preli-minary clinical trials on patients of bronchial asthma and allergic rhinitis, carried out at the Vallabhbhai Patel Chest Institute, Delhi, have indicated marked relief in symptoms for a few weeks in 40-50 per cent of the cases, after a small dose of 3-6 leaves only. A high incidence of side-effects (sore mouth, loss of taste, vomiting, etc.) was observed in 75 per cent of the cases. There appeared to be a direct relationship in several patients between the high incidence iof side-effects and high degree of relief in symptoms. The LD50 of the crude alcoholic extract of the drug was found to be 2 mg./kg. The extract was also reported to suppress histamine response.
