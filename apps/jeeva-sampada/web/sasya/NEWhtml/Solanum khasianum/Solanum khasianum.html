HABIT & HABITAT
The plant is a stout, much- branched, under shrub, up to 1.5 m. tall. Found in the Khasi, Jaintia and Naga hills of Assam and Manipur, Naga land, Sikkim, west Bengal, Orissa, the upper Gangetic plains up to an altitude of 1600 m. Common in Himachal Pradesh in the waste places, ravines and near habitations up to an elevation of 1500m.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:
 Leaves are ovate, lobed and the lobes are lanceolate or triangular, hirsute and prickly on the both the surfaces. Flowers are white in colour and are in lateral, 1-4 flowered racemes. Berries are yellowish or greenish globose and 2-5 cm. in diameter. Seeds are smooth, brown and compressed.

Economic Potential and Uses
UTILIZATION:
Uses:
 Berry is used in the synthesis of corticosteroidal hormones. It is much valued for its solasodine used in various formulations including   birth control pills. Alcoholic extracts of the plant have been tested for biological activity. They affect the contraction of the isolated ileum of guinea-pig and also influence the central nervous system.


Chemistry:
Berries contain glycoalkaloids-solasonine (Which on hydrolysis yields solasodine), solamargine and khasianine. Roots contain nautigenin and iso- nautigenin. The latter two compounds are also present in aerial parts. Aerial parts also contain solasodine, diosgenin and b-sitosterol. An alkaloid saponin-solakhasianin has been obtained from berries.

·        Solasonime, mp. 2760, isolated. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1960-1969).

·        Solasodine and solakhasianin, mp. 2510, isolated berries. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1960-1969).

·        Plant extract possessed mild sedative, anticonvulsant and anticonvulsant and antiovulatory activities; it contained some biogenic amines like histamine and acetylcholine; it provided protection against electroshock seizures in rats and mice and produced transient fall in blood pressure. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Solasodine isolated from ripe berries. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Solasodine identified in ripe fruits collected in Guatemala. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        b-sitosterol, lanosterol, diosgenin and solasodine isolated from mature fruits. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Hydrolytic studies on glucosides showed that hydrochloric acid produced appreciable amount of solasodiene whereas sulphuric acid produced only very small amount. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Solasodine isolated from fruits. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Solasonine, solamargine and khasianine isolated from berries. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Solasodine content in leaves and fruits of Pakistani plants. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Aculeatiside A (0.1) and aculeatiside B (3.0%) isolated from roots and their structures determined. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Solasonine (0.70%) isolated from fresh berries of Pakistani plant; seeds contained fixed oil (4.47%). (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).

·        A process developed for isolation and purification of solasodine. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).

·        In elicitor-treated photomixotrophic Solanum khasianum cell cultures, nine phenolic compounds accumulated in the cell culture medium. They were isolated and structurally identified as the cis- and trans-isomers of N-p-coumaroyloctopamine, N-ferutoyloctopamine, N-p-coumaroyltyramine and N-feruloyltyramine as well as a-hydroxyacetovanillone. The cis-isomers of the hydroxycinnamoyl moieties were only formed during illumination of the cultures. The hydroxycinnamoylamides showed a transient accumulation with maximum values between 6 and 24 hr after elicitation, depending on the compound. The decrease of the compounds is very likely a peroxidase-mediated reaction. (Muehlenbeck et al., 1996)

·        The effect of some phytohormones on the yield of berries and salasodine content of S. khasianum plants was studied. Results indicate that there is the possibility to increase solasodine level by the application of phytohormones. (Barua et al., 1997)

·        Solakhasoside , a novel steroidal saponin, was isolated from the fruit of Solanum khasianum. Its structure was determined as (23S, 25S)-spirot-5-en-3beta,17alpha, 23-triol-3-O-[alpha-L-rhamnopyranosyl-(1-->2)[beta-D-xylopyranosyl-(1 -->3)]-beta-D-galactopyranoside] by spectroscopic analysis. (Putalun et al., 1999)

·        Solasodine glycosides were analyzed for the selection of higher-producing natural resources in the fruits of Solanum species by western blotting and ELISA using anti-solamargine monoclonal antibody (MAb). Western blotting of Solanum khasianum showed a correlation with the analysis by ELISA. The results indicated that the fruits of S. khasianum contained the highest level of solasodine glycosides, compared to other species having 53.43 ± 3.90 microg/mg dry wt. (Putalun et al., 2000)

·        Distribution of solasodine glycosides in Solanum khasianum fruiting stage determined by ELISA and western blotting. (Putalun et al., 2001)

·        Pure Solanum glycoalkaloids were also prepared and hydrolysed accordingly. The process has analytical applications too, and could be extended in a general way to glycosides in other series for preparation of their related aglycones. (Weissenberg, 2001)

Pharmacology:

·        Solamargine (4.0 mg/ml) in vitro killed (100%) adults and microfilaria (mf) of Setaria cervi in 60 and 88 min respectively. When administered orally (0.1 g/kg) to rats, in which S. cervi was implanted i.p., it reduced blood mf count by more than 30% after first phase of treatment of 10 days. Mf count showed increase of 72% after second phase of treatment. Following third and fourth phases of treatments mf d. was reduced by more than 90%; 100% reduction was obtained 15 day after fourth and final phase of treatment which was of 5 days duration. At 0.1 g/kg x 4 phases, it killed 100% of adult worms without incident toxicity. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).


Ethnobotany:

·          Medicine for abartleaf (fruit, Rao and Jamir, 1982); asthma, bronchitis, cold, cough (fruit, Goel, et al., 1984); insect-bite (seed, Tiwari, et al., 1979); toothache and decay (fruit, Rao and Jamir, 1982, Megoneitso and Rao, 1983, Borthakur, 1976, Borthakur, 1981, Kharkongor and Joseph, 1981).
