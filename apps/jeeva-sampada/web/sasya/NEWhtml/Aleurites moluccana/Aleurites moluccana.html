Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Aleurites moluccana is evergreen tree native to Malaysia, Polynesia, Malay Peninsula, Philippines and South Seas Islands, now widely distributed in tropics. Naturalized or cultivated in Malagasy, Sri Lanka, southern India, Bangladesh, Brazil, West Indies, and Gulf Coast of United States. It is found wild in South India (Wynaad), Assam and Burma and is grown in some parts, but it is not abundant anywhere. Candlenut trees thrive in moist tropical regions, up to 1,200 m altitude. Ranging from Subtropical dry to wet through Tropical very dry to Wet Forest zones, Aleurites moluccana is reported to tolerate annual precipitation of 6.4 to 42.9 dm, annual temperature of 18.7 to 27.4°C and pH of 5.0 to 8. Fruit drupaceous, laterally compressed ovoid-globose or bi-globose with 4 low longitudinal ridges, very slightly acuminate at the apex , 1-2-seeded, olivaceous, glabrescent, 4-6 cm long, indehiscent, with bony endocarp and oily seeds.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A medium or large sized tree with widespreading or pendulous branches, 10-40 m tall. With heavy, irregular, large-leafed spreading crown, it appears as whitish or frosted from a distance due to a cover of white stellate hairs especially on young parts. Bark gray-brown to blackish. Leaves alternate, simple dark green with a silvery gloss, ovate or trilobed or rarely 5-lobed, or sublanceolate, or subdeltoid, acute at apex, rounded, truncate, or subcordate at base, 3-5-nerved from the base, midrib thereafter pinnately branches, blades mostly 10-20 cm long, on an equally long slender petiole, 2 glands at junction of blade and petiole. Stipules 1 mm long, leaves petiole 3.5-16 cm long, glands 0.5-2 mm in diameter.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Flowers unisexual with female flowers terminating the ultimate branches of the cymes, male flowers much more numerous, smaller arranged around the female flowers. Flowers small in rusty-pubescent panicled cymes 10–15 cm long, greenish-white, male and female on same plant, panicles 10-15 cm long, many-flowered, petals pubescent, elliptic-spathulate, petals 5, dingy white or creamy, oblong, up to 1.3 cm long. Staminate flowers 5-10 mm in diameter, pedicel 4-12 mm long, calyx lobes 1.5-3.0 mm, stamens 17-26, stamens of unequal lengths, outermost filaments 0.5-1 mm long, inner most 2-3.5 mm long, anthers 0.3-0.8 mm. Pistillate flowers 7-10 mm in diameter, pedicels stout, 2-3.5 mm long, calyx lobes 3-6 mm, ovary subglobose, pubescent, laterally compressed, 2 locular, 1.5-3 mm broad, stigmas deeply bifid, 1-2.6 mm long.

Breeding System :
Flowers are monoecious in nature, male and female flowers in same inflorescence, usually with the pistillate flowers surrounded by several staminate flowers. Both self and cross pollinating. Flowers are protogynous.

Phenology :
Flowering stars from April-May and fruits are produced from late September- November.

 

Back to Top

Pollinators:
Entamophillous in nature. Bees are needed to transfer pollen from anthers to pistil. When staminate and pistillate flowers are on separate trees.

Seed Biology :
Seeds broadly obovoid, cordate in dorsal view, dorsiventrally compressed, truncate or rarely minutely tipped at the micropylar end, always tapering towards the obtusely acuminate chalazal end, 23—32 mm, greyish, mottled brownish.

Seedling Germination :
Seeds may be planted dry, or soaked in water for 2 to 5 days before sowing. Soaking of seeds in water enhances the rate of emergence. Seeds typically germinate in 4 to 5 weeks.

Cultivation:

Usually propagated from seed. They grow very well in tropical climates with ample rainfall and also adapts to dry climates. Seedlings planted 300/ha. Candle nut trees once established, require little to no attention.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES
Candlenut-oil trees are relatively free from insects and diseases and control measures are not often justified by the losses.

Pests:

Insect pests are not a serious problem, since fruit and leaves of Candlenut-oil trees are toxic to most animal life.

Diseases:

Candlenut-oil tree is attacked by following pathogens. Cephalosporium sp., Clitocybe tabescens, Fomes hawaiensis, Gloeosporium aleuriticum, Physalospora rhodina, Polyporus gilvus, Pythium ultimum, Sclerotium rolfsii, Sphaeronema reinkingii, Trametes corrugata, Xylaria curta, Ustulina deusta. Nematodes include Meloidogyne sp.

Economic Potential and Uses

Oil is refered by so many names China wood oil, candle-nut oil, lumbang oil, walnut-oil or artist's oil. Seed yields 57–80% of inedible, semi-drying oil, liquid at ordinary temperatures, solidifying at -15°C, containing oleostearic acid. Oil, quicker drying than linseed oil, is used as a wood preservative, for varnishes and paint oil, as an illuminant, for soap making, waterproofing paper, rubber substitutes and insulating material. Seeds are moderately poisonous and press cake is used as fertilizer. Kernels when roasted and cooked are considered edible, may be strung as candlenuts. Oil is painted on bottoms of small crafts to protect against marine borers. Tung oil, applied to cotton bolls, stops boil weevils from eating them. Also prevents feeding by striped cucumber beetle.

The oil is also used as rubbing oil for rheumatism. Usually the nut is pressed for its oil, which is used for a variety of industrial purposes like soapmaking, varnishes, and fuel. The oil is sometimes used medicinally similar to castor oil, as well as a laxative. In Southeast Asia, the oil is sometimes applied topically to treat headaches, fevers and swollen joints. Because of the high oil content, the nuts will burn with a smoky flame.

Uses

The nuts can be poisonous when raw, causing violent vomiting. Roasting destroys the toxin in the oil, which causes these effects. The roasted nuts are delicious, and are reported to be nutritious and high in energy from the fat they contain. They can be used to tenderise meat and used in the preparation of curries. It is used in Indonesian cooking, usually ground to a paste.

Wood is not durable, used for tea boxes, fuel, matches and canoes. The seeds are eaten raw or after roasting together with rice or used as purgative. The seeds are used as torches by wrapping them in leaves or stringing them to the midribs of palm leaves. The latex is chewed as gum in the Pacific and is also used medicinally or as glue. Juice from the husk is used as a black dye, similarly from the bark of the root, the latter is used to paint canoes. Chinese in Hawaii let the wood of felled trees rot to grow the fungus Auricularia cornea for local consumption or for export to China. The tree is also used as an ornamental.

Harvesting Practices
Fruits are gathered by hand into baskets or sacks. In USA, Clean cultivation and mechanized harvesting is practiced. The harvesting machine sweeps or rolls fallen fruits into windrows, and then collects them by a pick up mechanism which includes suction device. Bears two heavy crops each year, harvested when mature. Kernels adhere to sides of shell and are difficult to separate. Nut yields at 80 kg/tree. Would suggest 16 MT/ha/yr, about 20% of which (3 MT) would be oil. Fruit yields may range from 4–20 MT/ha/yr. Commercial production of oil yields 12–18% of the weight of the dry unhulled fruits. Oil yields as high as 3, 100 kg/ha have been reported.


Produce and Products

Oil of Industrial importance is obtained from the seeds. It is widely used as a dryer in varnishes and paints as tung oil polymerizes to a hard, waterproof gel that is highly resistant to acids and alkalies. It is also used as a component of insulating compounds and in the manufacture of linoleum and oilcloth.

Chemistry

Oil contains oleostearic acid. It also contains glycerides of linolenic, oleic and various linoleic acids. The oil cake, containing ca 46.2% protein, 4.4% P2O5, and 2.0% K2O, is said to be poisonous. Bark contains ca 4–6% tannin. 100 g seed contain 626 calories, 7.0 g H2O, 19.0 g protein, 63.0 g fat, 8.0 g total carbohydrate, 3.0 g ash, 80 mg Ca, 200 mg P, 2.0 mg Fe, and 0.06 mg thiamine.

Processing Techniques

Pretreatment: Fruit is dried without delay after harvesting; artificial drying is sometimes necessary. The nuts should not contain more than 5% moisture when processed. Decortication of the seed to remove the thin shell. (80 % of the shell removed). The kernels are then ground and preheated to 180oF. (820C.).

Extraction: Ground kernels then enter a screw type expeller press. Solvent extraction can also be used. The oil has a low free fatty acid content and no further refining is necessary, except to filter off impurities through a press on its way to the storage tanks. The oil is extracted on a large scale in China and Philippines, small quantities in India.

Other Uses
Bark used on tumors in Japan. The oil is purgative and sometimes used like castor oil. Kernels are laxative stimulant, and sudorific. The irritant oil is rubbed on scalp as a hair stimulant. In Sumatra, pounded seeds, burned with charcoal,are applied around the navel forcositiveness. In Malaya, the pulped kernel enters poultices for headache, fevers, ulcers, and swollen joints. In Java, the bark is used for bloody diarrhea or dysentery. Bark juice with coconut milk is used for sprue. Malayans apply boiled leaves to the temples for headache and to the pubes for gonnorhea .

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

Tung oil has wide range of industrial significance. In India it has not attained much importance and is grown only in limited area. It can be alternatively used with edible oils so as to reduce the pressure on edible oils. Most oil produced in India, Sri Lanka and other tropical regions is used locally and does not figure into international trade.
