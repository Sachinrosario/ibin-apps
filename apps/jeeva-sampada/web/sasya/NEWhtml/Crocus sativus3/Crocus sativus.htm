Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
A small bulbous perennial corm growing to 0.1m by 0.1m, cultivated in India and is mostly confined to the table-land of Pampur (5300 ft above sea level) in Kashmir and Kishtwar in Jammu.

The saffron crocus is a sterile triploid cultivated variety possibly developed from the wild Crocus cartwrightianus of Greece. Its origins, like those of so many plants that have been in cultivation since antiquity, are lost to history. The genus Crocus has about 80 species that are native to regions from the Mediterranean to western China. Crocuses are in the Iris family.

The plant prefers light (sandy) and medium (loamy) soils, requires well-drained soil and can grow in nutritionally poor soil. The plant prefers acid, neutral and basic (alkaline) soils and can grow in very alkaline soil. It can grow in semi-shade (light woodland) or no shade. It requires dry or moist soil.

Found in Cultivated areas and plains.

The spice's name derives, in fact, from the Arabic za'faran ("yellow") and sahafarn ("thread"). Even the Greek word croci, forerunner of crocus, means "weft".


Availability :  
 The origins of saffron are obscure, but the plant is believed to have originated in the eastern Mediterranean, probably Greece, Asia Minor and Persia. It has been cultivated in this area for 35 centuries, and murals depicting saffron have been found in the ancient city of Knossos in Crete. Its use soon spread to the Romans, who may have been involved with its introduction into Britain. The Arabs are sometimes credited with the introduction of saffron into Spain around the tenth century. The movement of saffron into India occurred much earlier. There is evidence of its medicinal use in Kashmir in 500 BC.

The plant is a native of south Europe and is cultivated in Spain, France, Italy, Greece, Turkey, Persia, India and China. Cultivation in India is mostly confined to the table-land of Pampur (5,300 ft. above sea level) in Kashmir and Kishtwar in Jammu..

Status : 
Cultivated in crop lands
Formerly valued as a medicinal herb and a dye, saffron is now principally used for flavoring and coloring foods. It supplies the characteristic flavor and color in Spanish paella, Italian risotto, French bouillabaisse, Arabic lamb and chicken dishes, and Indian dessert sauces, as well as Swedish, Cornish and Pennsylvania Dutch holiday breads.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

Saffron is a bulbous perennial plant, ranging from 10 to 30 centimeters in height. The bulb is meaty and round, although somewhat flat at the base. It is white coloured on the inside, covered with a fibrous and rough membrane, and brown in colour. Flowers surge from the bulb in a stem of about 3 millimeters in diameter, which develop in two purple to violet coloured membranes.

The flower is called the "Rose of the Saffron" and made up of six elliptical pieces. The stem is also a violet colour when it initially emerges, becoming more opaque, and finally turning white

The flower contains three large stamen with orange coloured anthers. Inside the flower tube is the style, a long white filament whose apex is orange coloured. This divides into three red threads, the threads or cloves of the saffron, which correspond to the stigmas.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Breeding System :

The scented flowers are hermaphrodite (have both male and female organs)

The saffron crocus flowers in autumn and is dormant during the summer months. Flowers are lilac to mauve, but the outstanding feature of the flower is its three bright red stigmas 25-30 mm long, which droop over the petals. A white-flowered form of saffron is known, but the stigmas are not as large.

Saffron crocus flowers are sterile, therefore the plant is not able to set viable seed and must be propagated vegetatively. The cultivated form is thought to have originated as a naturally occurring hybrid that was selected for its extra-long stigmas, and has been maintained ever since.

Phenology :
Flowering during October-December.

Pollinators:
pollinated by Bees and butterflies.

Seed Biology :

Seedling Germination :

Seed - according to some reports this species is a sterile triploid and so does not produce fertile seed. However, if seed is obtained then it is best sown as soon as it is ripe in a cold frame. Stored seed can be sown in the spring in a cold frame. Germination can take 1 - 6 months at 18°c. Unless the seed has been sown too thickly, do not transplant the seedlings in their first year of growth, but give them regular liquid feeds to make sure they do not become deficient. 

Other means: Divide the small bulbs once the plants have died down, planting 2 - 3 bulbs per 8cm pot. Grow them on for another 2 years in a greenhouse or frame and plant them out into their permanent positions when dormant in late summer. It takes 3 years for plants to flower from seed. 


Cultivation:

In the scheme of saffron-growing regions, Kashmir is considered to produce the world's finest. Here, the ideal growing conditions of hot days, cool nights, and fertile soils allow the Crocus sativus plant to thrive

Most commercial production of saffron occurs in Spain and India. In Spain, saffron is grown in dry temperate conditions with a rainfall of around 400 mm per year, but the crop is usually irrigated. Saffron is grown successfully under rain-fed conditions in Kashmir, India (annual rainfall 1000-1500 mm). Spring rain is favourable for corm production, while rain immediately before flowering encourages high flower yield.

Soils should be sandy or loamy in texture, and very well drained. Incorporation of organic matter is beneficial as it improves soil texture and water- holding capacity. Saffron is normally grown in soils of high pH, around 7.0. However, a crop grown in Central Otago at a soil pH of 5.2 still grew and flowered very well.

Saffron prefers a cool, sunny situation, and can withstand substantial frosts. In parts of India, saffron fields are covered with snow in the winter. Flowering is generally regarded as more reliable in the Mediterranean region than in northern Europe. In New Zealand, flowering of saffron has been reported to be erratic, but crops grown in Central Otago have grown and flowered consistently for five years.

Since saffron does not set seed, it must be propagated vegetatively. Each season, new corms are formed above the old ones, which wither and eventually rot away, in the same manner as Gladiolus corms. Each corm produces 4-10 daughter corms, so the crop rapidly increases in density. Therefore, the crop needs to be dug up and replanted periodically. This occurs about every four years in Spain, but beds may last up to 12 years under the rain-fed conditions in Kashmir. Replanting should be carried out when yields of saffron spice begin to fall due to overcrowding, and competition for nutrients or water.

Extremely well-drained soils must be used for the cultivation of saffron. Raised beds are often used to improve drainage and minimize disease risks. A planting density of around 50 corms/m2 (20 cm between rows, 10 cm between corms in row) is recommended. Higher density plantings, although giving increased yields in the first year or two, soon become overcrowded and will need to be dug up and replanted earlier.

In traditional saffron culture, large amounts of organic manure are incorporated into the soil before planting the crop. This material improves the moisture-holding capacity of the soil as well as supplying some nutrients. Usually no further fertilizer applications are made during the life of the crop. However, recent overseas trials indicate that an annual fertilizer application is beneficial, increasing flower and saffron spice yields.

Corms are planted out during their dormant period in summer. Depending on plant density and size of corms planted, around 8 t of corms are needed to plant out 1 ha. Generally, the larger the corm, the more flowers it will produce. Corm s smaller than 1.5 cm in diameter should be planted in a nursery bed for one year to grow to plantable size. Corms should be planted 10 cm deep. A mulch such as sawdust, spread over the beds, helps control weeds and conserves soil moisture.

No herbicides have been tested for weed control in actively growing saffron crops. However, when the crop is dormant during the summer, herbicides such as Roundup can be safely used to clean up beds before the crop begins to grow again in autumn.

In Central Otago, leaves begin to appear around the beginning of March and the crop starts flowering in April. Flowering usually lasts four to five weeks. The traditional method of hand picking is still the only method for harvesting the crop. Flowers should be picked every fine day after any dew has evaporated and, later, stigmas can be separated by hand from the flowers for drying.

The bright red stigmas and coloured portion of the style constitute the saffron of commercial importance. Pale-coloured parts of the style should be discarded as they are of inferior quality. Only fully open flowers where the stigmas are clearly visible should be collected. Harvesting and separating stigmas is very time consuming. In Central Otago trials it took 45-55 minutes to pick 1000 flowers and 100-130 minutes to remove stigmas for drying. This equates to around 370-470 hours of work to produce 1 kg of dried saffron.

Saffron can suffer from a range of diseases - the most important ones attack the corms. Dipping corms in fungicide before planting, and using raised beds to improve drainage help minimize these problems. A range of viruses has been identified in saffron crocus, but their effect on flower production is not known. Rabbits like the leaves and can dig up corms, so rabbit-proof fencing may be required in areas where these pests are found.

Saffron's short blooming season--about three weeks in the fall--and its labor-intensive harvest make it the most expensive of the herbs and spices on the market. The saffron sold in the tiny quantities you find in the grocery store is the dried stigmas of the fall-blooming crocus, Crocus sativus. [It has occasionally appeared in print that the part used is the stamen--the yellow pollen-producing organ. This is an error.] Each crocus bulb produces two to nine flowers per season, and each flower has three long red-orange stigma branches, attached together at the base. It takes the dried stigmas of a hundred flowers to make a gram of saffron. Fortunately a little saffron goes a long way: about 1/2 teaspoon of powdered threads will flavor a cup (raw measure) of rice.

UTILIZATION:

Parts used
Stigmas

Uses

  • Dye:  The yellow dye obtained from the stigmas has been used for many centuries to colour cloth, 4000flowers are required to produce an ounce of saffron. Flattened stigmas have characteristic aromatic odour and bitter taste. Dye is readily soluble in water and alcohol. Saffron used to be used as a dye to give a bright yellow colour. However, this use has now been superseded by the use of synthetics because of the high price of saffron. 
  • It is the favoured colouring for the cloth of Indian swamis who have renounced the material world. 
  • It is chiefly used in Europe as a dye and to colour cheese, puddings and very little as a medicine.  
  • In India it is too expensive to be used as a dye-stuff.  It is, however, held in high esteem as a medicine.  Used for colouring butter, cheese, puddings, pastry and confectionery. 
  • A blue or green dye is also obtained from the petals
  • It acts as Nerve sedative and emmenagogue. occasionally used in fever, melancholia and enlargement of liver. Stimulant and stomachic properties and remedy for catarrhal affections of children.

Harvesting Practices

In Kashmir, saffron is hand-harvested, primarily by women, during the months of September and October. On our family fields, only the dark red stigmas are harvested — NEVER the yellow stamens of the flower. The practice of including the yellow stamens, commonly done to push the yields, only serves to reduce the quality, in terms of flavor and aroma, of the saffron. Our saffron is ALWAYS 100% pure dark red threads.

For preparing saffron, flowers are collected daily in the morning after  the dew disappear. Styles and stigmas are separated and dried either in the sun  or over artificial heat. The yield is very low and over 100 flowers furnish one grain of the dye.

Yields per plant are extremely low, about 4000 stigmas yield 25g of saffron. Saffron is the world's most expensive spice, it takes 150,000 flowers and 400 hours work to produce 1 kilo of dried saffron. About 25 kilos of styles can be harvested from a hectare of the plant. Fortunately, only very small quantities of the herb are required to impart their colour and flavour to dishes. Because of the cost, saffron is frequently adulterated with cheaper substitutes such as marigold flowers and safflower.

In the first season after establishing a saffron field, only small yields can be expected. Yields increase up to about the third season and can be maintained for a time until overcrowding occurs and yields then decline.

Average yields of saffron in Spain, a major world producer, are around 10-12.5 kg/ha. Yields under unfertilised and rain-fed conditions in Kashmir only reach 1.5-3.0 kg/ha on average. Higher yields have been found in experimental trials in Italy, India and Spain, up to 15.5 kg/ha.

The size of individual stigmas and the amount of style collected from each flower influence total yield and quality of saffron. Between 70,000 and 200,000 flowers are needed to produce 1 kg of dried saffron threads.

The Redbank Research Station in Central Otago established a trial in September 1990 using corms imported from Japan. Production of saffron increased dramatically from the first to the second season, but dropped in the third season. The crop was becoming very dense. In the summer of 1992-93 while the corms were dormant, small samples were dug to assess corm multiplication rates. The crop was originally planted at a density of 50 corms/m2. Two years later, density of corms heavier than 1.0 g had increased to 786 corms/m2, and 358 of these were more than 10.0 g in weight. It is not known just how big a corm should be before it will flower. However, using only corms heavier than 10.0 g, there was more than a 700% increase in corm numbers in two years.

Chemistry:

Floated on water the stigmas are seen to be about 25 millimeters long, and of a characteristic tubular shape, irregularly notched at the top. Saffron is flexible and unctuous to the touch when fresh, becoming brittle when quite dry. It has a characteristic odour and bitterish taste. It should not lose more than 12.5 per cent. of moisture when dried at 100°. It contains about 13 per cent. of water-soluble matter, and yields about 6 per cent. of ash.

An orange-red coloring matter, which gives to saffron its chief value; a glucoside, usually called crocin, C44H70O28, but formerly called polychroit, because of the many different colors it gives with acids; crocetin, C34H46O9, and a volatile oil, C10H16, upon which its medicinal virtues depend.

Saffron contains about 1 per cent. of volatile oil, an amorphous red colouring matter, crocin or polychroit (resembling and probably identical with carotin), and a colourless, crystalline, bitter principle, picrocrocin. Crocin, like carotin, is coloured deep blue by concentrated sulphuric acid, and green by nitric acid.

Processing Techniques
                                       

Once stigmas have been separated from the flowers, careful drying is needed to produce a good quality product. The traditional method in Spain involves carefully toasting the stigmas in a silk sieve over the embers of a charcoal fire. This product is known as saffron "hay" and takes 30-45 minutes to dry. "Gucci" saffron is prepared by removing the entire style with the stigmas, binding these together in bunches and drying in the sun. This method is sometimes used in Iran. 

In Central Otago, saffron was dried in an airflow oven at 30øC for 24 hours. A domestic dehydrator set at a low temperature would be suitable for drying saffron.

Saffron can be ground or pressed together to form "cake" saffron but, as this material is easily adulterated, it is less liked on the world market.

Because saffron is so expensive, it has always been a subject for adulteration. A wide range of adulterants have been used, from dyed corn silk, petals of safflower and other flowers, to wax or fat to make the product heavier, and even red lead. In the Middle Ages, there were saffron inspectors in Nuremberg, Germany, with authority to burn or bury alive those who adulterated saffron! In these more reasonable times, International Standards are available detailingtests that can be carried out to determine saffron quality.

Good quality saffron has a strong odour, is glossy and greasy to the touch when freshly dried, but turns dull and brittle with age. It is best stored in a cool, dry place, and is easily bleached if exposed to light.

Other Uses

 

  1. The flower styles are commonly used as a flavouring and yellow colouring for various foods such as bread, soups, sauces, rice and puddings. 
  2. They are an essential ingredient of many traditional dishes such as paella, bouillabaisse, risotto milanese and various other Italian dishes. 
  3. The styles are extremely rich in riboflavin. Water soluble.
  4. The flower styles are used as a tea substitute.
  5. Root - cooked. The corms are toxic to young animals, so this report of edibility should be treated with some caution.
  6. Medicinal Uses: The styles and stigmas are anodyne, antispasmodic, aphrodisiac, appetizer, carminative, diaphoretic, emmenagogue, expectorant, sedative and stimulant. They are used as a diaphoretic for children, to treat chronic haemorrhages in the uterus of adults, to induce menstruation, treat period pains and calm indigestion and colic. A dental analgesic is obtained from the stigmas. The styles are harvested in the autumn when the plant is in flower and are dried for later use, they do not store well and should be used within 12 months. This remedy should be used with caution, large doses can be narcotic and quantities of 10g or more can cause an abortion. 
  7. It has an essential oil, the active ingredient of which is aphrodisiac in nature. 
  8. An alcoholic tincture of saffron is sometimes used as a fragrance ingredient, particularly in oriental-type fragrances. The spice is also used in some types of incense.

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

Total production of saffron in Spain has fluctuated in recent years from 25 to 60 t, while Indian production increased from 5 t in 1974 to about 10 t in 1983. Iran is another major producer growing 50 t of the spice in 1989. Iran uses 10-15 t in its domestic market and the rest is exported to Spain. Spain re-exports this product together with its own. Trade statistics, therefore, give an inflated impression of the size of the Spanish industry. Overall, Spanish production is in decline, due mainly to increasing labour costs and the unwillingness of young people to enter the industry.

Saudi Arabia imports substantial quantities of saffron - 39 t in 1987. France, Italy, Switzerland, Finland and Hong Kong imported 5-11 t each in 1987, but most of these countries also have a re-export trade. The total world trade in saffron was estimated to be around 90 t in 1987, equivalent to at least 10 billion hand-picked flowers.

Spain, Iran and India have low labour costs compared with New Zealand, and this is reflected in the wholesale prices for saffron on the world market. One German company wholesales saffron for approximately NZ$1600-3200/kg depending on quality. These prices are typical for higher quality saffron worldwide. Trade statistics for saffron can be misleading, for instance, the average value of saffron imported into Australia for the year ending June 1990 was NZ$335/kg. It is likely that other "saffron-flavoured" products were included in this category, therefore decreasing the average value of the category. 

Retail prices, naturally, are rather higher than wholesale prices. Local New Zealand prices for the spice vary, but an informal survey of selected retailers in the South Island showed prices varied from $8 to $14/g on average ($8000-$14,000/kg). However, one Christchurch source has sold saffron at $8.00 for one tenth of one gram, or $80 000/kg.

Saffron is a most attractive garden plant, and as it becomes more widely known, is being sought by home gardeners. A small market will develop for the sale of saffron corms as they become available.

The crop is expensive to establish and requires a high labour input for harvesting and stigma separation, so returns must be very high to make growing the crop economic. There is a large disparity between the wholesale price and the retail mark-up. If returns to growers were higher and the retail margin lower, there would be good potential for small-scale economic production in New Zealand.

Miscellaneous Information

The plant is poisonous. The plant is perfectly safe in normal usage but consumption of 5 - 10 grams of saffron at a time has been known to cause death.

Saffron bulbs are toxic to young animals, and the stigmas in overdoses are narcotic.

Because saffron is so expensive there is always the risk of unknowingly buying a cheaper substitute, like safflower petals, (which produce the color but not the flavor of saffron), or of being cheated by an unscrupulous supplier who adulterates his product. For this reason it is best to buy it in the form of dried threads rather than a powder.

Since it takes about 4,000 crocuses to produce one ounce of saffron, the spice was once literally worth its weight in gold. Its hue is actually an orange-ish gold, considered, in Asia, to be the ultimate color. Kings and other ancient VIPs strutted in robes dyed with saffron.
