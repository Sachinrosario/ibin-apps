HABIT & HABITAT
Trunk 10-25 m high, 20-50 cm in diameter, found to occur in Wild in Andaman & Nicobar Islands. Cultivated throughout India. Myanmar, Srilanka all tropical shores.


Availability :  
Plant is commercially cultivated.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:
Trunk 10-25 m high, 20-50 cm in diam., annulate, thickened at base with mass of rootlets. Leaves 1.5-4.5 m long; leaflets equidistant, linear-lanceolate, 60-90 cm long, coriaceous; petioles 0.8-1.5 m long, stout. Spadix 1.2-1.8 m long, stout, androgynous, simple panicled; branches bearing scattered female flowers towards their bases and numerous male above. Lower spathes short, hard, splitting lengthwise; upper fusiform or clavate. Drupes green or yellowish, obovoid or subglobose, 20-30 cm long, 1-celled; pericarp fibrous; endocarp stony.


REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:   
Inflorescence: Spadix androgynous at the length drooping, stout, simply panicled, branches bearing scattered female flowers towards the base and numerous male above, lower spathes oblong, hard, splitting lengthwise ; Male Flower:  Unsymmetric; sepals small valvate; petals oblong, acute, valvate; stamens six, filaments subulate, anthers linear, erect, pistillode minute; Female Flower: Larger than males, ovoid, supported by broad bracteoles, perianth accresent; sepals round, concave, imbricate; petals shorter than sepals convolute,  with imbricate tips ; Fruit: 3-gonously, obovoid or subglobose, green or yellowish, pericarp thick and fibrous, endocarp bony with 3 basal pores indicating three cells of ovary, filled with sweet somewhat milky fluid. 
Floral and seed biology:
It flowers throughout the year.
Mode of pollination and seed dispersal:
Flowers are pollinated by insects. Seeds are dispersed by wind, birds or animals.
 
Genetic variability:
The reported chromosome number - 32.

AGRO TECHNOLOGY:

Propagation: Plant is propagated through seeds.

Soil type: Plant requires well drained soil with sandy or sandy-loam.

Nutrient requirements: Wood ash, cattle manure and green manure are useful for plant.

Water management:  Plant requires well distributed rainfall and also good irrigation services.

Harvesting period / Method: Fruits are harvested when they are fully mature.

Economic Potential and Uses
UTILIZATION:
Uses:
 Coconut tree as a whole has got great commercial value. Leaves are used for making mats or buskets. The endosperm and oil are edible and copera obtained from the fruit is used in confectionaries (Dixit & Prasad, 2001). Root is anthelmintic. Fruit is sweet, cooling, fattening, tonic, laxative, aphrodisiac, cardiotonic. It is useful in leprosy, thrist, biliousness, diseases of blood, burning sensation and tuberculosis. Flowers is cooling and useful in diabetes, dysentery, leprosy, urinary discharges and constipation. Dried fruit improves taste. It has aphrodisiac, fattening and constipating properties. Milk is cooling, oleaginous, appetizer, aphrodisiac, laxative. It is useful in bronchitis, biliousness and tumours. Oil is indigestible, aphrodisiac, fattening and useful in urinary complaints, asthma, bronchitis and ulcers (Ayurveda) (Kirtikar & Basu, 1933).


Chemistry:
CIMAP:
The leaves of coconut were analyzed monthly for a period covering 3 years in 3 different areas for 8 inorganic elements. The N content was directly correlated with the water supply. The K content was correlated with a Ca and Mg content of the leaves. Na was neg. correlated with K.P was correlated with N. Cl was correlated with the area. N, P, K, Na and S varied with the area. N, P, K, Na and Cl decreased with increased age of leaf while Ca, Mg and S increased1.

  • Albumin, globulin and prolamine fractions of coconut separated, hydrolysed and amino acids analysed. Albumin fraction composed of aspartic and glutamic acids, alanine, serine, threonine, valine, leucine, isoleucine, methionine, cystine, proline and hydroxyproline; globulin fraction contained lysine, arginine and protamine fraction contained aspartic and glutamic acids, serine, threonine, alanine and valine. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1960-1969)a.
  • Detection of phenol, p-cresol, caproic acid and p-hydroxybenzoic acid by TLC in shell fibres; in addition, tar from shells contained crotonaldehyde, furfural and acetic acid. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

 

Pharmacology:

  • The neutral detergent fiber (NDF) isolated from coconut (Cocos nucifera) kernel was digested with cellulose and hemicellulase and the residual fiber rich in hemicellulose and cellulose were fed to rats and compared with a fiber free group. Hemicellulose rich fiber showed decreased concentration of total cholesterol LDL+ VLDL cholesterol and increased HDL cholesterol, while cellulose rich fiber showed no insignificant alteration. Rats fed hemicellulose rich coconut fiber produced lower concentration of triglycerides and phospholipids and lower release of lipoproteins into circulation. There was increased concentration of hepatic bile acids and increased excretion of faecal sterols and bile acids. These results indicate that the hemicellulose copinents of coconut fiber was responsible for hypolipidaemic effect. (Sindhurani and Rajmohan, 1998).
  •  The effect of coconut (Cocos nucifera) water pretreatment on the activity of some centrally acting drugs was investigated. The results showed that in rats coconut water progressively reduced amphetamine induced spontaneous motor activity, and prolonged pentobarbitone sleeping time.  Coconut water pre-treatment also protected mice against pentylenetetrazole-induced convulsions. These effects have been linked to the ability of coconut water to depress the central nervous system. (Akah et al., 1998).  
  • Feeding kernel protein (globulin) resulted in lower levels of cholesterol, phospholipids and triglycerides in the serum and most tissues when compared to casein fed animals. Rats fed kernel protein had (I) increased hepatic degradation of cholesterol to bile acids, (2) increased hepatic cholesterol biosynthesis and (3) decreased esterification of free cholesterol. In the intestine, however, cholesterogenesis was decreased. The kernel protein also caused decreased lipogenesis in the liver and intestine. This beneficial effect of the kernel protein is attributed to its very low lysine/arginine ratio 2.13 % lysine and 24.5 percent arginine. (Nair  et al., 1998).
