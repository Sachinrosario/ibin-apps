Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
A genus of trees or shrubs are distributed chiefly in tropical Asia. Only one species is found in India. Mesua ferrea is a large evergreen tree of tropical wet or semi evergreen forests occurring either scattered or in pure patches in Burma, India and The Western Ghats in the Andamans. Found in Himalayas from Nepal eastwards, in northeastern India and Deccan peninsula. In cultivated conditions, it is found chiefly as an ornamental tree under various local names. The plant is more valued as timber, than as oil seed. In its natural habitat the climate is moist, warm and euqable, the rainfall varying from 80-200 inches or more, the obsolute maximum shade temperature from 950-1030 F., and the obsolute minimum from 400-600 F. It requires well drained deep fertile soil, stiff clay and low lying situations are undesirable. It is susceptible to frost and drought (2, 3, 4).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
It is a medium sized to large evergreen tree with short trunk, often buttressed at the base. Bark greyish or reddish brown, exfoliating in large thin flakes, leaves lanceolate, coriaceous, generally covered with waxy bloom underneath, red when young. The sapwood is creamy white or pinkish brown, rather broad; heartwood dark red or deep reddish brown, medium to coarse textured (2, 3, 5, 6).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Flowers are large, fragrant, cream coloured, ebracteate, pedicellate, pedicel short, axillary or terminal, solitary or in pairs of 2-3 and 2.5 to 7.5 cm in diameter. Buds are subglobose. Some of the distinguishing microscopic characters include presence of cortical fibres, numerous resin canals and calcium oxalate crystals in cortex and pith of pedicel, anamocytic, anisocytic or paracytic stomata in sepals and petals and 3-4 zonocolporate pollengrains with reticulate exine surface. Stamens are numerous with short filaments and somewhat thick elongated anther lobes. Anthers are tetrasporangiate. Some fused stamens alongwith their respective vascular supplies have also been observed. Occasionally the fusion may involve antherlobes as well. When the tree is in flower it is a gorgeous sight with its masses of white blossoms (2, 3, 5, 6).

Breeding System :
Cross-pollinated

Phenology :
The old leaves fall during the cold season and the new flush of red or pink leaves appears towards the end of that season. Flowers appear from the end of February to April (3).

Pollinators:
Flowers are visited by innumerable bees and other insects, which are known to cause poliination.

Seed Biology :
The fruit is ovoid, pointed, somewhat woody, about 1-1.5 inch long, two-valved, containing 1-4 seeds. The seeds are 0.8-1.1 inch. long, ovoid or irregularly hemispherical, pointed, with angular sides smooth, reddish brown, testa hard but somewhat brittle. The seeds are very oily and soon lose their vitality (2, 7).

Seedling Germination :
Germination is Hypogeous. The testa of the seed splits and the radicle emerges from the blunter end and descends. Meanwhile the cotyledonary petioles elongate rapidly, enabling the plumule to emerge and ascend. The fleshy cotyledons remain below the ground within the testa, remaining attached to the seedling for several months and supplying it with nutriment (3).

Cultivation:

Natural reproduction is generally profuse on account of abundant seeding. Artificial propagation may be done by direct sowing or by transplanting nursery raised seedlings. The seed should be sown as soon after collection as possible and well covered with earth in the nursery beds, the later should be in a shady situation and should be kept moist, care being taken to avoid caking of the soil. Transplanting is preferable under top canopy shade and may be carried out from the first to the third rainy season after sowing. Watering should be carried out in the dry season for the least two years after transplanting. A spacing of 6 ft. is recommended. The rate of growth is slow. The exploitable girth of 6 ft. is attained in 90 years or more, indicating a mean annual girth increment of 1 in. It is 1ikely that the tree grows faster in plantations than in natural forests (2, 3, 6).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

The tree is attacked by beetles like Xyleboris discolor, X. interjectus. Also attacked by a big buprestid borer, Chrysochroa sp (2).

Diseases:

The tree is affected by brown cubicle rot ( Fomes dochmius) and white sap and heart rot (Ganoderma lucidum) (2).

Economic Potential and Uses

The seed is about 2.5-3.0 cm in length and contains a kernel of nearly the same size. The kernel contains 35 to 50% or 70% of dark yellow or brown oil with an unpleasant pungent odour. The oil exhibits a very high acid value and is rich in oleic acid, followed by stearic and linoleic acids.The oil from the seed kernel is used mostly for lighting purposes. The oil can also be used for soap making. The unacceptable flavour and odour of the oil limits its use in soap making. Oil from the seeds exhibited antifungal activity against a number of pathogenic fungi. Phenolic extract of seed oil of M. ferrea revealed potent antiasthmatic effect. The seed oil is used for treating rheumatism and skin diseases. The cake is rich in nitrogen and can only serve as good manure. The volatile oil from the flowers shows antibacterial, antifungal and anthelmintic activities. It exhibits significant anti-inflammatory and styptic activity. The essential oil from the stamen showed antifungal activity (2, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9).

Parts used

Seeds, flowers, fruit, bark, bud, oil and wood


Uses

Fruit is sometimes eaten. The fragrant stamens of flowers are used for stuffing the pillows and cushions. The phenolic substances in the seeds are anti-bacterial activity. Dried flowers, along with other aromatics are used for preparing perfumed ointments. A reddish brown volatile oil obtained from the flowers is used for perfuming soaps, particularly in conjunction with sandalwood oil. Oleo-resin exuded from the base of the fruit, bark and root is used as varnish. The wood is used for timber for railway sleepers, bridges and posts, agricultural implements etc (2, 7).

Harvesting Practices
Seeds are collected by climbing the tree with the help of long ladders or fallen seeds on the grounds are manually collected. For tree species there is organised method of seed collection.

Produce and Products

Fruits are eaten. Wood is used for the timber purpose. Oil is extracted from the seeds. Seeds have antibacterial activity. Volatile is obtained from the leaves, which is used for perfuming soaps.

Processing Techniques

The seeds are cleaned, depulped, dried and decorticated to extract the oil by solvent. The oil can be refined by treatment with caustic soda followed by bleaching with activated carbon, or treatment with potassium chlorate and, hydrochloric acid. The non-glyceridic components may be extracted with alcohol or other solvent, and the oil treated with dilute alkali, followed by bleaching and deodorization. An elegant refining method consists in dissolving the oil in 5 times its weight of carbon tetrachloride and passing the solution through a column of alumina, the column is subsequently washed with carbon tetrachloride and the solution and washings when worked up yield a refined product which is colourless, odour less and tasteless (2, 4).

Other Uses
The flowers are astringent, bitter, acrid, mildly heating, anodyne, sudorific, digestive, carminative, constipating, anthelmintic, diuretic, alexipharmic, expectorant, stomachic, haemostatic, aphrodisiac, febrifuge and cardiotonic. They are useful in asthma, cough, hiccough, leprosy, scabies, skin diseases pruritus, phatyngodynia, vomiting, dysentery, haemorrhoids, ulcers, burning of feet, dipsia, impotency, leucorrhoea, haemoptysis, strangury, halalgia, fever and cardiac debility. The seed oil is used in skin diseases. Stem bark is used as astringent, in combination with ginger as sudorific. Leaves are applied to the head in severe cold. Root is aromatic and demulcent. Pericarp of the fruit is astringent and stomachic (2, 5, 6, 10, 11).

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

As an oil seed, it is of minor importance. Plant has assumed its value more as a madicinal plant. The commercial sample of Nagakeshara consists of loose fragrant stamens of Mesua ferrea which form a golden brown powder, coarse to tough in texture and consisting of filaments, pellate stigma and style, pieces from fleshy sepals and paper thin pieces of petiole, last two being in much lesser proportion. The stamens which yield the drug Nagakeshara contain mesuferrone-A and B, mesuaferrol, mesuanic acid, a- and ß-amyrin, and ß-sitosterol. The seeds and heartwood contain a number of xanthones and coumarins (5, 6).
