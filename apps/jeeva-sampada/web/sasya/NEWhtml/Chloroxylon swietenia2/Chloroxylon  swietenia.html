 Other Biological Details
HABIT & HABITAT
A moderate-sized tree, common in dry deciduous forests as far north as the Satpura hills and chota Nagpur, on areas receiving an annual rainfall of 750-1500 mm. It grows on bare rocky grounds and on poor soils that are well-drained with a large proportion of sand or gravel. In many localities it is typically associated with Soymida febrigugg (Meliaceae).

Availability :  
Its availability is in Central and Southern India and Ceylon.  Common in the forests of the Konkan, Deccan and Coromandel.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A medium-sized deciduous tree, up to 15m tall, with a short straight, clear bole and spreading crown. Bark thick, corky rough, furrowed, pale yellow or light gray, aromatic; leaves alternate pinnate, 12-24 cm long, aromatic; leaflets 10-20 pairs, oblong, unequal-sided, gland-dotted, 1.5-3.0 cm long and 0.6 –1.5 cm wide. Flowers white or cream –coloured, borne in terminal or axillary, pyramidal, panicles up to 15 cm long. Capsules oblong, glabrous, 2.5 cm long, dark brown when ripe, dehiscent, seeds oblong, brown, winged. The tree is usually leafless from February-April. 

Heartwood light yellow or golden yellow; not distinctly demarcated from the sapwood. Texture fine and even; highly lustrous; grain narrowly interlocked, often with attractive mottle figure; fragrant but without characteristic taste.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Timber, Bark, Leaves

Uses

  • The gum occurs in irregular, fractured and agglutinated tears, very variable in size, brittle with a shining resinous fracture, translucent, brown, somewhat resembling babul gum, but more brittle, soluble in water, tasteless or slightly sweet. Mucilage rather turbid, dark mahogany colour with an odour as a fusil oil.  It has a peculiar and remarkable phenomenon which the mucilage of this sample exhibited is that its surface was in an hour or two covered by a thick pellicle of gum, the upper surface of which became quite dry, as if, by rapid evaporation of the water in which it was dissolved, it was returned to the solid state.  Although this pellicle was broken up, it continued daily to re-form on the surface of the solution.
  • Dye:Yields a yellow dye.
  • Bark contains 17% tannins, astringent.

Other Uses
  1. Timber - furniture, agriculural implements, railway sleepers, poles, cart shafts, axles, bridges, well-pipes, cabinet-work, picture-frames, brush-backs, carving and turnery.
  2. Small branches - fuel.
  3. Wood yields an essential oil.
  4.   Leaves are applied to wounds and also prescribed in rhematism. A paste of leaves and roots in equal parts is taken internally to relieve headache or applied externally to the forehead as a balm by tribal inhabitants of Bastar district.
  5.  A decoction of stem bark is astringent and is used externally to treat painful joints. Also given to alleviate fever  and to treat chest pain among the tribal inhabitants of southern Bihar. Among the Gonds of U.P. a decoction of stem bark, together with that of Mangifera indica, Madhuca indica and the leaves of Haloptelea integrifolia  and Dendrocalamus strictus is used as a bath to treat jaundice.
