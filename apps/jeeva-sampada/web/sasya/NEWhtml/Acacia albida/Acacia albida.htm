Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Slow growing. Native of Africa’s dry Savannas and riverine basins, widespread below 600 m in wooden grasslands and along streams, often in pure stand where water table is high. Naturally occurs in wetter area of Sahelian savannas from Senegal to Sudan, Ethiopia and also along the rivers in East Africa as far south as northern Transvaal, Bostwana, and Losotho. Stands occur in coastal areas of Namibia and Angola, Israel and Lebanon.

Multipurpose exploitation in the agronomic system of West Africa. Tribals of Senegal and Bambras of Mali protect the tree and have a good system for intensive management of parks.

Prefers deep soil and an annual rainfall of 650 mm, if deep underground water is available, also thrives where rainfall is 300 mm. Occurs in diverse habitats from alluvial soils on perennial or seasonal stream-banks to woody open savannas and cultivated areas.

Floral and seed biology:

Seed production is sufficient, natural regeneration by seed is limited because of consumption by predators and high rate of seed mortality.

Sowing in polypots is considered best to produce the maximum number of healthy and vigourously growing plantable seedlings. Broadcast sowing in boxes is also good. For better germination, soil media should contain only soil and sand in the ratio of 2:1 and no FYM. Soon after completion of germination after pricking out in the bed, FYM in 2:1:1 is added to the growing medium to obtain vigourously growing healthy seedlings to plant in the field.

Other uses:

During dry season dense foliage gives fresh shade, foliage for live stock, protection against soil erosion and excessive dryness. It allows sunrays to reach the cultivation under the tree.

Leaves and fruits valuable food for stock and game animals. Seeds if boiled repeatedly can be eaten by man.

Pods dried and stored for forage.

The bark yields tannin and gum.

Gum used for treatment of diarrhea.

Miscellaneous Information

 
Nitrogen Fixation Because the trees are leafless during rainy season they do not inhibit growth of crop. In fact being a leguminous plant, it enriches the soil. Nitrogen fixing bacteria on their root nodules fix atmospheric nitrogen. Tree is a nitrogen fixer.

Peanuts grown under the tree reportedly yield twice as much as when grown farther away. Millet yield is reported to have increased by five times.

Effect on Environment It has beneficial effect on physical, chemical and microbiological properties of the soil.

It has been estimated that dry agricultural land may feed 10 bovines per sq. km in the Acacia albida zone and capability can be increased to 20.

Apart from this A. albida has played an important role in the religion and myths of several African tribes much as khejri, also known shami during the harvest festival. It is a sacred tree in Mali for the Bambara and far to the east in Chad for the Zaghawa. Offerings of millets, butter and occasionally chickens and animals are made to the albida trees which are identified as ancestral spirits, amenable to requests for rain, children and other blessings.

Bambara believe that albida was the first tree created by God and it played a major role in the early days of humanity, granting immortality, fertility and the secret of fire in return for human affection and gifts of blood.

According to legends, human ingratitude and abuse were responsible for mankind losing the gift of immortality and experiencing sickness, hatred and death. Bambara believe that albida’s red pods symbolize blood formerly shed for the tree by humanity and the white trunk and limbs represent bleached bones of ancestors. As a result, albida wood is scarcely used because it is the reincarnation of human flesh.

Study on 6-month old seedlings of Acacia albida and Prosopis cineraria planted at 5m X 5m, 5mX10m, 10m X10m distances with interculture of pearl millet, cluster bean and mung bean showed that growth of P. cineraria was slow and for first 3 years the saplings grew only 13-16 cm in height while A. albida growth was much faster and during the same period saplings attained a height ranging from 89-129 cm. Growth of Acacia was fastest in plants grown at 5m X 5m distance while at wider spacing tree growth was comparatively slow.

Plant shows mycorrhizal association. These associates are of VAM type. Mycorrhizal plants avoid drought stress by producing long taproots.

Plants are micro propagated.
