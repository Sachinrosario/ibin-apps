Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:

Palmyra palm is a very tall, magnificent palm, found throughout tropical India, especially along the peninsular coast and in West Bengal and Bihar. This palm probably native t India is the most striking of the palms and is a grand feature in the landscape of the tropical regions. It has run wild in many parts o India and has also been cultivated chiefly in the dry or sandy localities of Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Madya Pradesh, Kerala, Orissa, Tamil Nadu and West Bengal. It grows in isolated patches in other states such as Assam, Gujarat, Maharashtra and Uttar Pradesh.(1)

The Palmyra palm also grows wild from the Persian Gulf to the Cambodian-Vietnamese border, is commonly cultivated in South-East Asia, Malaysia and occasionally in other warm regions including Hawaii and South Florida


Availability : Plenty


Conservation/ Threat Status : Both wild and cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Palmyrea palm is 20-23 mt. In height and 1.0-2.2 mt. In girth, sometimes upto 31 mt in height (1) normally with a single trunk. It is clothed with overlapping leaf basis until it is 25-40 ft tall in dry zones. In wetter sites, the palm holds them longer (2). The spadices are very large and stout.(1)

There may be 25-40 fresh leaves present at one time, arranged in three spiral rows, some ascending to the right and some to the left. They are leathery, gray-green, fan-shaped, 3-10 ft. wide, folded along the midrib; are divided to the center into 60-80 linear-lanceolate, 2-4 ft long, marginally spiny segments. Their strong, grooved petioles, 3-4 ft long, black at the base and black-margined when young, are edged with hard spines

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

The palm blooms when about 20 years old. Male trees produce in the axil of each leaf a single, or sometimes a double complex, branched spadix, at first covered by a compound, pointed, boat like sheath, fibrous and tough, coated with soft, brown, velvety tomentum when young. The tiny flowers, intermingled with bracts, are borne in dense, slender, cylindrical spikes 12-15 inches long, one to three together under the four to seven upper sheaths; always in groups of three under the lower sheaths, and 9-12 inches long.

The female spadix is unbranched, its flowers, rotund and 1 inch wide, are uncrowded in small, sparingly branched spikes each enveloped by a series of 8-12 large bracts. Conical hermaphroditic flowers occur rarely on what have been thought to be male trees; these have proved to be fertile, resulting in fruits with viable seeds.

Each palm may bear 6-12 bunches of about 50 fruits per year. The fruits an average of 1.5 kg in weight, superficially coconut like and three sided when young, become rounded or more or less oval, 6-8 inches wide, and capped at the base with persistent, brittle, overlapping sepals. The outer covering is smooth, thin, leathery, and brown, turning nearly black after harvest. Polyembryogony and twin seedlings have been reported in the palm although in general only one seedling emerges . The immature endosperm is sweet and gelatinous, but it becomes hard ivory like and hollow when ripe .

Breeding Syste : Palmyra palm as we already know is dioecious. The palm palm grows naturally and no particular cultivation is necessary. It requires no artificial irrigation or manuring. It is generally propagated by direct sowing.

Sometimes the palm is reported to produce bulbil like offshoots instead of floral bunches in the crown. It is very easy to grow the palm artificially and pure forests of this tree can be formed if the seedlings are well protected. The palm can also be grown as a mixed plantation crop along with date, coconut and sago palm.

MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
The palm is attacked by insects which also infest the coconut palm (Cocos nucifera Linn. (rhinoceros beetle), Rhyncophorus ferrugineus Oliviera (red palm-weevil), Coccotrypes borassi Buson, etc. bore the softer portion of crown, stem, young growing leaves and fruits. Chiorapis dilatata Green, Parlatoxia mangiferae Marlatt, Nephants serinopa Meyrick (black headed caterpillar), Halimococcus borassi Green, etc. feed on sap, leaf sheaths, leaves and flowers
Diseases:

The palm is liable to infection by Phytophthora palmivora Butler causing a serious fungal disease called "bud-rot". It results in the withering of the central shoot or the expanded leaves and the collapse of the affected cells. The affected bud should be removed from the stem and burnt as soon as the first leaf turns white. Infection of healthy trees can be prevented by brushing or spraying the bud below the expanded leaves with ordeaun mixture, Perenox or Fytalan. Prophylactic spraying of the surrounding trees with the solutions of the above fungicides is also done.

Ceratostomella paradoxa (de Seyn) Dade causing stem bleeding; Exosporium palmivorum Sacc. Causing leaf splitting, Graphiola borassi Syd. & Butler resulting in pin head spot; and Pestalotiopsis palmarum (Cooke) Stey. Syn. Pestalotia palmarum cooke causing grey blight are other diseases found in the palm.

Other fungi reported are Merulius similes Berk. & Br. on the roots, Sphaerodothis borassi T.S. Ramakr and K. Ramakr on the leaves, Phytophthora colocasie Racib. Emend. Thomas & T.S. Ramakr on the leaves and growing points and Peziga repanda Pers. on the rotter fruits.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
fiberS

fibers from the petiole and its base are used in the manufacture of brushes of different shapes, brooms, ropes, twines, fencing, sandals, reapers, cradles, door mats, mats or packing, cushions, different type of baskets, hats, etc. and for fuel. It is also employed for lacing of cots and chairs, tying up forggots, cattle yokes, and for other agricultural purposes.

The stem or tar fiber is used for making fishtraps. The fibrous web at the base of young leaves is used for straining toddy and to make torches.

The midribs of the leaf are used in the manufacture of brooms.

PULP
The petiole, devoid of coarse and short edges, is a cheap and potential raw material for paper pulp.

MEDICINAL

Young plants relieve biliousness, dysentry, gonorrhea. Young roots are used diuretic, anthelmintic and a decoction is given in certain respiratory diseases. The ash of spadix is taken to relieve heart burn and enlarged spleen and liver. Bark decoction with salt is used as mouth wash and charcoal made of the bark serves as dentrifice.

Sap from the flower is used as a tonic, diuretic, stimulant, laxative, antiphlegmatic and amekecide. Sugar made from sap is said to counteract poisoning and is prescribed in liver disorders Candied, it is a remedy for coughs and various pulmonary complaints.

Fresh toddy, heated to promote fermentation is bandaged onto all kinds of ulcers.

The cabbage, leaf petioles and dried male flower spikes all have diuretic activity. The pulp of the mature fruit relieves dermatitis.

Due to higher content of non reducing sugars in palm-jaggery, it is used as an energy food for convalescents. It exhibits mild laxative action and is reported to be an effective therapeutic agent for anaemia

Parts Used:

Inflorescence, stem, leaves, petiole, roots, young plants, wood, endosperm, tuberous seedlings, mesocarp pulp, bark.


Harvesting Practices:
The quality of the fiber largely depends upon the age of the palm. Whereas a middle-aged palm yields strong fiber of dark clour, the fiber from younger or older palms is lighter in colour and weak. Stripping usually commences in the fifth year after planting when the palms are 3-5 m. in height, and the best fiber is obtained for 2 or 3 years thereafter.

Normally, as the palm grows, the leaf stalks fall of leaving the fasal part of the petiole attached to the tree. This portion appears grown and dried but it is, in fact, moist and juicy and yields the best fiber. This portion of the petiole should have reached a certain stage of decay before harvesting, as it would ensure that the fiber is mature, pliable and black in colour.

The palm is prepared for tapping of sap from inflorescence by cutting off all the leaves except 3 or 4 at the top, as also the young bud, the outer covering of the part of the tree from which the leaves and racemes grow is also removed. The racemes are then tied, beaten and crushed between wooden batters to wound them and stimulate the flow of the sap. The operation of beating and crushing is repeated on three successive mornings, and on each of the next four mornings a thin slice is cut from the tips of the racemes to encourage the flow of sap and keep them from bursting. On the eight morning a clear, sweet juice begins to flow from the wounded racemes into the earthen, or sometimes bamboo receiver placed for collection.

Chemistry:
The sap is mildly acidic and contains sucrose. The composition of sap varies with individual trees, localities, age of tree as well as time of tapping. Analysis of the sap from male and female trees respectively gave : purity 93.28, 90.80%; soluble solids, 13.72, 14.03; sucrose, 12.45, 12.20; reducing sugars, 0.8, 0.1; gms / pectins, 0.04, 0.04; and minerals, 0.55, 0.33%. The sap is a colloidal solution containing some whitish pectinous material. It is a nutritious supplement to diets which are deficient in iron, ascortic acid and vitamin B complex

Processing Techniques:
fiber - The leaf stalks are torn apart into strips, after softening the ends by beating with a wooden mallet. These are combined over a brush, made of steel wires, to separate the raw fibers from the connective tissue. They are dried under the sun and tied into small bundles. These bundles are further cleaned in factories and made more uniform in thickness.

Palm jaggery - It is prepared by boiling the sweet juice. The juice is directly strained into the boiling pot, leaving behind, the sediment of lime. An improved method is warming the limed juice to 40oC and ten neutralizing it with either phosphoric acid or sulphur dioxide, to a pH of 7.0. It is then filtered clear to eliminate the added lime and other impurities. The clarified juice is brighter and lighter.

Palm sugar - About light percent by weight of brown sugar can be recovered from palm sap. In the factories, raw jaggery is heaped on platforms for 2 months to drain away most of the molasses, then dissolved in water and refined to obtain brown crystalline sugar.

Candy - The sweet palm candy is directly produced from the sap. The clarified raw juice is boiled down as in the manufacture of jaggery, but not to such a high degree of concentration. It is boiled only to 107-108oC and then poured into pots, which are covered and buried under grounds for some months, when crystals of sugar candy form


Other uses:

In Tamil Nadu, the tender growing point of the Palmyra is edible. Sometimes referred to as palm cabbage, the green vegetable is extracted from many species of palms. Since, the palm must be destroyed to remove the heart, it is only available when a tree is felled for some other reason, such as when land is cleared for annual crops.

When the palmyra fruit is fully mature, the fibrous, yellowish, mesocarp pulp is sugary and dense. It can be eaten fresh but is more commonly boiled, sundried, or baked before being consumed, generally after being mixed with sugar.

By itself, the mature endosperm has no direct human use. However, upon germination a starchy, edible lutex is produced. This is in reality an underground first leaf, a rarity among palms.

In Tamil Nadu by slicing the top of the fruit, Nungu (or the immature endosperm) is removed with spoon or sucked out. The endosperm may also be sliced and made into a dessert with the addition of rose water and sugar. It can also be preserved is palmyra palm syrup.

Tiny fruits the size of hen eggs are pickled in vinegar.

Green leaves, mesocarp pulp, and the less palatable portions of the palm hearts are eaten by livestock.

Green leaves are made into drinking cups. Petioles have utility in fece making.

Wood is used for making pillars, posts, poles, food-bridges, fencing, well-sweep. Also used in building houses in villages, as water channels after splitting the wood. The bark mature wood is used in making umbrella handles, walking sticks, paper-rulers, fancy-boxes, picture frames, furniture and ornamental articles.

Soft pitty inner portion of the young trunk is a good feed for animals
