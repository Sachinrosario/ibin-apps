Other Biological Details
HABIT & HABITAT
It is a moderate or large deciduous tree, attaining 25-30 m in height found in abundance in Northern India from Kumaon to Bengal and southward to the Deccan table lands at 1000 to 3000 feet, also found in Burma, Ceylon and the Malay Peninsula.

A short day plant species, but is generally a light demander and is fairly a frost hardy, drought hardy and fire resistant.  It grows on a wide range of soils with a pH range of 5 to 7.5 but prefers soils with low salinity and with moderate level of fertility.  Best growth is seen in deep, moist, sandy loam with good subsoil drainage.  It grows in areas with rainfall ranging from 750 to 3300mm.  It can tolerate temperature up to 47°C.

Availability :  
It is  common all over the forests in Madras, Coimbatore; it is of large size; occurs in Godavari tracts.  In Bombay, it is common on the higher forests on and near the Ghats, and is very abundant in the forests of highlands of the Satpurass and above the Ghats in Belgaum and Kanara.  In southern region it is found in Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu and Karnataka.

In Punjab, it is occasionally cultivated on the Sub-Himalyan tracts up to the Indus, in the Siwalik and Outer Himalaya upto 5,000ft and extending west to the Sutlej; It is extensively grown in Kangra District.

Several other species yield commercial myrobalans, but the fruit is almost unknown in Western commerce. In India and China, where the species are indigenous, the fruit is highly valued for almost every ill that flesh is heir to. The hard wood takes a fine polish and is useful in cabinet work. The creamy, fragrant juice of the T. angustifolia, Wight, when dried, is used in Indian temple worship as an incense. The tree is regarded sacred, and has interesting historical and mythological connections. The celebrated India Ink is the product of the bark and leaves of T. cattapa, Linné. All the species yield a tanning bark. The leaves, bark, and fruit yield a dye, which with iron gives a rich black, and with alum a fine yellow color.

Status : Not threatened

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

A tree 15-24 m in height and 1.5-2.4 m in girth, with a cylindrical bole of 4-9m, a rounded crown and spreading branches. Bark dark-brown, often longitudinally cracked, exfoliating in woody scales, leaves ovate or elliptic with a pair of large glands at the top of the petiole; flowers yellow to orange-brown, sometimes tinged with red or black, and hard when ripe, 3-5 cm long, become 5-ribbed on drying; seeds hard, pale yellow.

Seed Biology :


Seeds are dried in shade and stored in gunny bags for a year.  

Chebula myrobalans are ovoid or oblong, about the size of the prune, yellow-brown, marked with 5 or 6 obtuse angles, and ribbed. The light-brown endocarp is resin-dotted. The single seed is white.

Seedling Germination :

It can be propagated both by seeds as well as vegetative means.  As a pretreatment, freshly fallen ripe fruits are collected and allowed to ferment in cow dung slurry for two weeks or more, until the seeds show signs of germination.  seeds may also be de pulped as soon as the fruits are collected and soaked in tepid water for 4 to 6 days.  Seeds are either sown in nursery beds in March-April or directly sown in the field in June-July.  Seeds are sown in nursery beds in lines 20cm apart and 5cm within lines.  For direct sowing, seeds are dibbled at 1 m apart and covered with soil about 1 to 1.5cm thick.  Seeds take 10 to 30 days to germinate and about 60 percent seeds germinate.  3 to 5cm tall seedlings are transplanted into polybags.

Vegetative propagation is through stumps.  The plant can be propagated through stump planting for which 12 to 15 month old plants provide satisfactory planting material.

Back to Top

Cultivation:
Transplanting in case of nursery raised seedlings or saplings is done in June-July when the plants are about one year old.  Transplanting of seedlings or planting stumps is done in pits of 45cm cube or crow-bar holes at a spacing of 10 x 10m after the start of rains.  About 18 percent of the transplants establish.  Regular weeding and watering is necessary.

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Dried fruits

Uses

Myrobalans are one of the most important tanning materials of the pyrogellol class.     Roots, barks, heartwood, sapwood and leaves contain tannin. Maximum concentration of tannins occur in the fruits followed by the roots, barks, heartwood and leaves.  

  • These tans produce a brownish coloured deposit on leather called bloom, as opposed to the catechol class of tans which do not produce this bloom.  
  • The minimum tanning content worked out on dry basis for fresh picked selected, leftover and crushed nuts is 35, 27 and 40percent respectively by the total weight.  The maximum non-tannin content studied on dry basis in fresh picked selected, leftover and crushed nuts is 17, 20 and 20 percent by weight respectively. 
  • Myrobalan tan is not very astringent and penetrates the hide very slowly.  When used alone, it produces a soft, mellow and rather spongy leather which lacks good wearing properties.  
  • Myrobalans in India are largely used in conjunction with babul, avaram and mangrove barks.  They are not as a class, considered a good weight giving tan stuff and the proportion of tannin combining with the hide substance is small compared with other tanning material.
    The dried fruits are used as dye, tan and also in medicine.  It is also used in manufacture of ink
  • Galls are also found on the leaves which in conjuction with alum yield a good permanent yellow dye.
  • Myrobalan  is one of the principal bloom-yielding tans due to its high ellagitannic acid content, and is useful especially in the production of sole-leather.  
  • In India, it is occasionally used as a dye by itself, the rind of the fruit being powdered and steeped in water.  The cloth steeped in this infusion acquirers a dirty grey colour.  With alum both the fruit, and the Galls produced in quantity on the Leaves, are said to give with alum a good permanent yellow.  It is commonly employed throughout the country as a mordant, or accessory, to concentrate the colour in dyeing with safflower, manjit, huldi and tesu.  With iron-salts it is employed in making country ink, and mixed with ferruginous mud it makes a black paste employed by harness and shoe-makers as well as by dyers.
  • The Bark is also occasionally used for dyeing khaki gray and black, and in Bengal and Manipur for dyeing bamboos.  
  • The extract has been successfully tried for pre-tanning cow and buffalo-hides.  
  •    Myrobalan is widely employed in tanning and to fix fine colour in leather  resistant to light and ageing.  It contains nearly 20% tannins.
  • Myrobalan pulp or spent-tan is used for making activated carbon, filler in cardboard manufacture, in adhesive resins as a fuel or for paper-making  
  • Tree  yields a gum, largely collected in Maharashtra. This gum is collected mixed with those of Acacia arabica, Anogeissus latifolia, Bassia longifolia, and Melia azadirachta.  This gum is sold either for medicinal purposes or to dyers to mix with their colours.     

 
Harvesting Practices
Best time for collection of fruits for optimal tannin content is the month of January in the country.  The collection after this will yield slightly inferior quality.  Collection done too early also show similar quality.  A good sample contains tannin to the amount of 32 percent.  The tannin content varies from 12 to 49%. According to the Indian standards, nuts of this tree are classified into three categories.  The first category includes good quality fresh handpicked nuts i.e., the nuts which are free from sand, dust, insect-pest or worm infections.  Second category includes the left over ie., those left after the selection of the fresh picked.  Third category nuts are the crushed nuts.

Myrobalans freshly collected and dried immediately have yellowish colour and fetch a better price.  If the fruits are allowed to lie on the ground have darker colour and sometimes mould attack which reduces tannin content.  Sometimes it would attack on the trees itself, causing major cause of poor quality of myrobalan.

Produce and Products
Tannins, timber, gums

Chemistry

The fruit, as well as all other parts of the tree, contains tannin. According to E. Mafat (Pharm. Jour. Trans., Vol. XXIII, 1892, p. 145), the amount of tannin in chebulic myrobalans varies from 18.2 to 52 per cent. Stenhouse (1843) found 45 per cent of tannin, gallic acid, mucilage, and a brown-yellow coloring matter. The black variety contains much sugar. Apéry, (1888) isolated from black myrobalans a green oleoresin soluble in alcohol, ether, petroleum spirit, and oil of turpentine. He named it myrobalanin. A. Campbell Stark (Pharm. Jour. Trans., 1892, Vol. XXIII, p. 253) making a complete analysis of myrobalans, observed the same oil and obtained only 20.6 per cent of tannin. Dr. G. Zölffel (Archiv der Pharm., 1891, p. 123) found the tannin matter of myrobalans to be identical with that of algarobilla, the astringent fruit of Caesalpinia brevifolia, Bentham, a Chilenian plant. It consists of two principles which differ in both plants only with regard to their relative proportions. Accordingly, the tannin matter of myrobalans is a mixture of predominating ellaggen-tannic acid (C14H10O10, Loewe, 1875, or C6H2.COOH.[OH]2.O.O.CO.C6H2[OH]3, Zölffel), and a smaller quantity of gallic acid glucosid, yielding, upon hydrolysis, gallic acid and dextrose. Gallic acid also preexists in part in myrobalans. Ellaggen-tannic acid, isolated by Loewe (1875) from the fruits of Caesalpinia Coriaria, Willdenow (Divi-divi), as well as from myrobalans, decomposes, upon hydrolysis, into water and ellagic acid (C14H6O8+2H2O). Fridolin's crystallizable chebulinic acid (1884), upon hydrolysis, splits into 2 molecules of gallic, and 1 molecule of tannic acid. It is no doubt closely related to the tannic principles aforenamed.

Fruits contain about 30% of astringent substances - chebulinic acid, tannic acid, gallic acid etc. Resin and a purgative principle of the nature of anthraquinone and sennoside are also present.

Myrobalans contain an oleo-resin of a green colour, soluble in alcohol, ether, petroleum spirit and oil of turpentine and is called myrobalanin.

MYRABOLAMS extract  a hydrolyzable (pyrogallic) tannin, astringent, with a good light fastness.

Myrobalan extract is a brown powder, easily soluble in water, with the following analytical values:
  • Tannins %: 70 minimum
  • Humidity %: 8 maximum
  • pH (at 6,9°Bé): 3,4 ± 0,3

Tannage with only myrobalan extract gives light-green leathers with a full and soft touch. Blended with sweet extracts (Mimosa and Quebracho) it increases the astringency of the mixture and slightly tones down the typical red colour imparted by these  extracts. It is sometimes compared to Sumach, but contrary to it  myrobalan gives less glossy and more dull dyeings, maybe due to sulphiting.

Indication of quantities for specific uses.

  • For sole leather tannage: 2-3%, on limed weight, in the final drum tannage improves the colour and helps to lower the pH of the liquor. If used in such small amounts the finished leathers will not assume the characteristic green colour of Myrobalans.
  • For the vegetable tannage of shoe upper leathers: up to 5-7% of the total amount of extracts in order to obtain a soft touch and a light colour.
  • For the retannage of chrome tanned leathers: 2-10% on shaved weight, either alone or in mixture with other vegetable extracts or syntans.

Processing Techniques
Myrobalans are marketed in the form of whole nuts,  crushed nuts and solid dried extracts which are used in tanning. For the extraction of tannin, the whole myrobalans are broken into 2-3 pieces in toothed roller-crushers and leached with hot water in a battery of six wooden vats using the counter-current principle.

Other Uses
  1. Timber - construction, posts and beams, carts, frames, axle and shafts, railway wagons, railway keys, buffers and brake-blocks. In making wrapping and printing paper. For fuel wood,  its calorific value  is high.
  2. Fruits are credited with laxative, stomachic, tonic and alterative properties.
  3.  Fruit –pulp is used as a dentifrice to cure  bleeding and ulceration of gums.  
  4. It is used in cough, asthma and urinary diseases. It is one of the ingredients of Triphala along with Amla and Behra. Used in treatment of piles. It is also used in tanning of leather.

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

In India, the total yield is about 6000-9000 tonnes including the blended extracts.

In trade, myrobalans are usually known by the place of origin.  For ex. Bimilies (Bis) exported from Bimlipatam (Andhra Pradesh), etc. The myrobalans of Salem district (Tamil Nadu) are regarded as the best in India for colour and tannin content.

Important markets for myrobalans in India are 

MP- Raipur, Durg, Mandla, Chhindwara, Jabalpur, Shahdol, Katni, Raigarh, Bastar etc

Maharashtra-Satara, Poona, Kolaba, Chandrapur etc.,

Orissa-Mayurbhanj, Kalahandi, Ganjam, Koraput, Konjhar, Belangir

Andhra Pradesh-Adilabad and Nalgonda

Bihar-South Bihar

Karnataka-Belgaum and Kanara, Mysore, Bangalore

Tamilnadu-Salem, Madurai, Coimbatore.

M.P.accouts for 75% of the total production in the country. The production has been around 200,000 quintals. During the past 7-8 years, it has established to 120,000 quintals.

Miscellaneous Information

According to Indian mythology, this plant was originated from the drops of ambrosia (Amrita) which fell on the earth when Indra was drinking it.  It is known in Ayurveda as an adaptogen and anti-ageing agent. This plant was known to early Arabian writers and through them to the Greek writer, Actuarius, who mentions five kinds.
