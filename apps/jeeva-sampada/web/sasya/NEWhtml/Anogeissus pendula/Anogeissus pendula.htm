Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

A gregarious bush or a tree, commonly occurring in dry tropical forests of Rajasthan, parts of Gujarath, Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Haryana and Bundelkhand. The tree has a decidedly limited distribution. It extends from Aravalli hills in Rajasthan, northwards to Bundelkhand and from Jhansi, Hamirpur and Banda districts of Uttar Pradesh southwards to the river Narmada in Nimar districts of Madhya Pradesh and Panch Mahal district of Gujarath. It is very common in Gwalior and Shivpuri forests and abundant in many parts of Rajasthan, especially in the Ajmer - Marwar forests.

The tree is essentially an inhabitant of dry hot regions. The normal rainfall in its habitat is 400 to 800 mm. The maximum temperature varies from 44 to 470 C and the minimum from -3 to 20  C. It grows on the lower and gentle slopes but also extends on the top of small hillocks and ridges particularly if the soil is good. Usually found on a variety of sites including organic debris over rocky outcrops. It grows on dry arid soils overlying well-drained from hill slopes.

The tree is normally frost hardy but in the years of severe frost, coppice shoots are killed. Frost does not leave a permanent injury and the affected trees give rise to new shoots in the ensuing rainy season. Drought does not affect regeneration unless it is severe. It is a light demander.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

A gregarious bush or tree, 9-15 m in height and 1 m in girth, with a bole 3 m high. Bark smooth, grey, often silvery. Leaves very small, beautiful reddish brown when the tree is in fruit, elliptic-obovate, obtuse or acute, usually mucronate, base narrowed, silky hairy on both sides.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Flower heads on slender, unbranched axillary peduncles. Fruits small, winged, orbicular.

Breeding system and varietal improvement:

Phenology:

The leaves turn reddish-brown during winter and start falling in January. By March the tree is leafless. New foliage appears in May - June. Flowering takes place at the end of the rainy season during September. Fruits ripen from December - February and fall in March.

Floral and seed biology:

Seed Germination & Propagation: It produces root suckers. It coppices well.

The tree fruits abundantly every year but the germinative capacity of the seeds is very low and the seedlings have a tendency to die back probably due to prolonged drought. Therefore, seedling regeneration is not adequate and dependable. Seedlings are slow growing in the earlier stages and require lateral shade. Later, they require a good deal of light for successful development. They also require protection from browsing in unprotected forests.

For successful regeneration, the planting of entire transplants is preferable to direct sowing. Seeds ripen from December and continue to remain on the trees till March. Seeds are sown in nurseries in June. Germination commences after a week and is complete within a fortnight. The beds are irrigated everyday.

Best time for plantation is July.



MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:

Beetles and larvae of Schistoceros anobioides, Chrysobothris beesoni Obenberger and Olenecamptus anogeissi Gardner- bore between the bark and sapwood of logs and make a shallow pupal chamber in the sapwood.

Galasma goniaria - defoliate the tree

Urapteryx picticaulata Walker - defoliate the tree

Anua triphaenoides Walker - defoliate the tree

Dendrophthoe falcata - angiospermic parasite.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Wood used as fuel. The wood is moderately good fuel. The calorific value of the sapwood is 4,837 and that of the heartwood is 4,739 calories. The wood makes excellent charcoal.

Wood: The sapwood is pale greenish grey and wide with faint yellowish cast. The heartwood is blackish purple, small, irregular. The wood is lustrous with a smooth feel, without characteristic odour or taste, heavy to very heavy (sp gr 0.96, wt 992 kg/ cu m), shallowly interlocked grained in narrow bands or sometimes wavy or curly-grained in the radial plane, very fine-textured.

It is an important source of timber. It is one of the toughest, also ideal for making handles of all sorts including heavy striking tools like forging hammers. It is used as poles and rafters in construction, for picker arms, shuttles and bobbins, cart-axles, shafts, wheel spokes and frames and in furniture making. In central India it is commonly used as round timber for agricultural implements and for huts. It is also recommended as substitute for teak.

Other uses:

The tree yields the 'ghatti' or 'Indian gum'.

Leaves - The leaves yield a dye producing a dark green colour. They also contain tannins and are widely employed in Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh for tanning sheep skin into a beautiful white colour.

The tree yields good fodder.

Seeds - The seeds possess haemagglutination properties against the human A, B and O red cells.

Miscellaneous Information

Occasionally planted as ornamental due to its graceful pendulous branches and is useful for afforestation of hilly tracts with shallow soils and an annual precipitation of 500-700 mm.
