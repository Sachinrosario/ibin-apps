Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
The tamarind is native to tropical Africa and grows wild throughout the Sudan. It was introduced into India so long ago, it has often been reported as indigenous to India also. It is extensively cultivated in tropical areas of the world, Asia is a major tamarind producing area. Tamarind trees are widely planted in India and Sri Lanka as well as southeast Asian countries, and they mainly grow in dry mountain slopes where heat is sufficient, rain is less, and the elevation is not higher than 1500m (2). It is found neutralized almost throughout the plains and subhimalayan tracts of India. Tamarind is one of the common fruit trees found grown all over India mostly under rainfed conditions, particularly in Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh and Orissa. In Bajapur, in the Deccan Plateau, the tree is famous for its fine varieties and is cultivated extensively(48). Tamarinds are slow-growing, long-lived, evergreen trees. Though they are evergreen , there is abundant leaf fall and new leaves appear during March-April. Tamarinds tolerate a great diversity of soil types but do best in deep, well drained soils which are slightly acid. Trees will not tolerate cold, wet soils but are tolerant of salt spray and can be planted fairly near the seashore (1). Tamarindus is a tree of tropical climate. In areas where it is grown, the maximum temperature touches about 46°C in South India and the minimum temperature rarely drops to 00C in North India. The summers are hot and dry and winters mild. Frost is rare. The average annual rainfall varies from about 500 to 1,500 mm and most of it is received during monsoon season (8).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
The tamarind, a slow-growing, long-lived, moderate-sized to large, evergreen tree, up to 24 m. in height and 7 m. in girth with a compact rounded crown with drooping branches, but generally smaller, Trunk stout, often becoming twisted, Bark brownish or dark grey, longitudinally and horizontally fissured; The mass of bright-green, fine, feathery foliage is composed of paripinnate leaves, up to 15 cm., long, with pulvinus at base of petiole, leaflets generally 10-20 pairs, opposite, entire, sub-sessile, oblong, 8-30 mm. long and 5-10 mm. wide, pale green beneath, with unequal rounded base and rounded or emarginate apex, which fold at night. It is highly wind-resistant, with strong, supple branches, gracefully drooping at the ends. The tree has usually a short and thick trunk, which is seldom straight. It has a handsome, dense and spreading crown of feathery foliage (3,9).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Flowers small racemes are 5-petalled (2 reduced to bristles), yellowish with pink stripes, in lax, few-flowered racemes at the ends of the branchlets. Inflorescences in small terminal drooping racemes, 5-10 cm long. Flowers 2-2.5 cm in diameter, zygomorphic, bracteoles 2, reddish, boat-shaped, about 8 mm long, enclosing bud but falling before flowers open, sepals 4, reflexed, ovate, cream-coloured, 1-1.5 cm long; petals 3, borne on top of flowers, obovate, pale yellow streaked with red, 1-1.5 cm long; fertile stamens 3 alternating with staminodes, filaments 1 cm long, united for about half their length, curling upwards under petals, anthers transverse, reddish-brown, dehiscing longitudinally, pistil oblique, curving upwards, green, sparsely hairy, longer than stamens, with small clavate stigma. The flowerbuds are distinctly pink due to the outer colour of the 4 sepals, which are shed when the flower opens (2,9,11)

Breeding System :
Studies on the floral biology of tamarind are few. It is reported that flower bud development takes about 20 days from first visible initiation. Peak anthesis takes place at 6.00 a.m and peak anther dehiscence at 10.30 a.m. Maximum stigma receptivity is on the day of flower opening.

Phenology :
Seed propagated plants start bearing 7-8 years after planting. While grafted or budded plants will start fruiting 4-5 years of planting. Flowering takes place from April to June and the pods ripen from February to April of the following year. The pod is thick, filled with dark brown acid pulp and traversed by fibers. The seeds are brown and shining. It is an evergreen tree except in very dry localities where it becomes leafless for a short period early in the hot season. The old leaves are shed in April-May and the new leaves appear in March-April. Fruits are harvested during January-April months (8).

In tamarind, foliage changes in March to April, flowering takes place in May to June and fruits are available from December to February.

Pollinators:
Very little is known about pollination and fruit set in tamarind. It is reported that tamarind is protogynous, entomophilous and highly cross-pollinated. The nectiferous flowers attract honeybees to collect nectar and pollen, so they presumably assist in cross-pollination. Self-pollination also results in seed set.

Seed Biology :
Pods 7.5-20,0 cm. long, with 2.5 cm. broad and 1.0 cm. thick, more or less constricted between the seeds, slightly curved, brownish-ash-coloured, scurfy seeds 3-12, obovate-oblong, compressed, with a shallow, oblong pit on each side of the flat faces, with. 1. 5 X 0, 8 cm. smooth, dark brown, shining. The seeds are contained in loculi, enveloped by a tough, leathery membrane, the so-called endocarp. Outside the endocarp is the light- brownish, red, sweetish acidic, edible-pulp, traversed by a number of branched, ligneous strands. The outer most covering of the pod is fragile and easily separable. Seed has a 35 % of Testa and 65 % Kernel. Testa has dark brown colour and kernel if of creamy white colour. (44)

Seed dispersal: The large, hard seeds are dispersed by both animals and man. When the fruits are eaten fresh, the seeds are often discarded.

Seedling Germination :
Tamarind seeds remain viable for months, will germinate in a week after planting. Propagation has been customarily by seed sown in position, with thorny branches protecting the young seedlings. Germination is epigeous and the cotyledons enclosed in the testa are pushed above the ground. The testa falls off as the cotyledons expand. The stem is erect, wiry and tomentose and the primary root is long, wiry and with numerous fibrous lateral roots distributed down the entire length of the main root. The seedlings exhibit a moderate rate of growth and attain a height of about 0.5 and 1.2 m by the end of first and second season of growth respectively. The root also develops fairly fast. The seedlings continue growing throughout the year though the growth is somewhat slowed down during winter. Regular weeding and watering encourage seedling growth (8).

Cultivation: (1,30,31,32,33,49)

Propagation:

Propagation by Seed: Tamarind is normally grown from seed. Seeds should be collected from high yielding trees with well-formed, rounded, fully ripe pods, although they may not come true to type due to outcrossing. Pre-packed commercially produced seed is also available in some countries.

Vegetative Propagation: Vegetative propagation is preferable to seed propagation, as seed propagation does not produce true-to-type progenies. Vegetative propagation has the advantage of producing true-to-type progeny which can be taken from selected, superior, mother trees. Selection through vegetative propagation is a rapid means of improving the quality of the produce to meet the varying demands of diverse commercial markets.

Field Establishment:

Direct Seeding: Tamarind can be established in the field by directly sowing seed at stake. Seeds may be sown directly to establish plantations, hedge rows, or home gardens. Planting holes should however have been previously prepared and filled with well-decomposed manure or compost. Depth of sowing should be about 1.5 cm. For planting on the square, on the triangle, or in lines for orchard establishment, a few seeds are dibbled in 5 cm apart at each planting station. These are based at 4-5 m apart in lines which are at 4-5 m apart. The seeds, may also be sown at random in discrete patches, at 4-5 m apart. This method is practised in the South Indian states of Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh. After germination, and when the seedlings reach a height of 10-20 cm, they should be thinned to one at each planting point, the best seedlings being selected. For seed planting in these ways approximately 20 kg of seed is required per hectare.

Transplanting:Transplanting nursery grown plants into the field can have advantages over planting seed at stake, as the plants are already established. This, however,  incurs much higher transport costs, is more costly, and the newly planted trees need more care and attention than do field grown seedlings.

Spacing: The spacing between plants varies between 5 m and 13 m apart either way. Spacing may be even wider on fertile soils where the trees grow larger. When establishing a pure stand plantation, the final spacing should be at least 13 x 13 m square or on the triangle. There can be an advantage in planting at higher density (about 5 x 5 m), followed by thinning two or three times  to the appropriate final spacing. This allows some further selection in the field and reduces the weeding costs in the early years. The final spacing should be closer when vegetatively propagated plants are used, as they do not attain the height or spread of seed propagated trees, and their management is easier. In parts of India tamarind is established at 8 m x 8 m, 8 m x 12 m or 12 m x 12 m. Sometimes the seedlings are planted at spacing of 5 x 5 m, which becomes 10-15 m x 10-15 m after thinning two or three times. Tamarind is frequently planted along land borders, canal banks, on field boundaries, in village groves or in home gardens as individual trees. In India, tamarind is used to make firebreaks, as the soil beneath is almost bare due to heavy shade. When creating firebreaks, the spacing adopted is 2.5 m x 2.5 m or 3 m x 3 m. If the spacing appears to be too close some plants can be removed later. In Mysore, where tamarind is used commonly in fire lines, seedlings are raised in tiled pots and planted in the field at a spacing of 3 m x 3 m in 1 m3 pits. Aftercare is minimal for these plantings, the only requirement being to hoe the soil around the plants once a year to remove weed growth.

Time of Planting: The best time for field planting is at the beginning of the rainy season, particularly in seasonally dry regions. This will reduce the need for frequent watering until the plants are firmly established in the soil. If irrigation is available, field establishment may be undertaken at any time of the year, even in the dry season. However, it is advisable to provide partial shade to the newly established plants, if planting is carried out during the dry periods.

Management

Growth of tamarind is normally fast in the early years. The juvenile phase generally lasts 4 to 5 years or perhaps longer. Growth slows down in later years. Mean plant height increases by about 60 cm annually. The average annual growth has been reported as 50 to 80 cm.

Pruning and Training: Tamarind requires minimal care, other than in the very early stages of growth. Tamarind is a compact tree and produces symmetrical branches. Young trees should be pruned to allow 3 to 5 well-spaced branches to develop into the main scaffold structure of the tree. Bearing trees require very little pruning other than maintenance pruning to remove dead, weak and diseased branches and water sprouts. In closely planted orchards regular pruning is needed to rejuvenate fruiting wood and control the size of the trees.

Intercropping: Intercropping may be practiced in order to obtain some income during the early stages of tree growth, and until the trees reach bearing age. The intercropping period is limited to 3 or 4 years for vegetatively propagated trees, which commence bearing at that age. It is good practice to plant short season crops which grow in the rainy season. Intercropping can be extended up to 5 or 6 years for the slower maturing seedling propagated plantations.

Nutrition:

Fertilisers and manures: Information on fertiliser application is scarce and the nutrient requirements of tamarind are not well known. In India, inorganic fertilisers are not applied to tamarind trees, but 5 kg of farmyard manure is applied to the planting hole at the time of planting. Every year thereafter 5 kg of farmyard manure and 5 kg of neem cake are applied per tree in the months of March and April.

Mycorrhizal associations: Presence of nodules was recorded(33). Tamarind seedlings inoculated with 13 vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhiza (VAM) fungi from various sources around the world have been demonstrated to exhibit increased leaf number, plant height, stem girth, biomass, phosphate and zinc content. The number of VAM spores in the soil, percentage root colonisation and external hyphae measured by soil aggregation were also higher. Tamarind responded best to inoculation with Gigaspora margarita (ICRISAT) and Glomus faciculatus. It was also observed that significant increases in growth parameters and leaf P and K contents when seedlings were planted in pots inoculated with VAM. Application of inoculum to the pits before transplanting the seedlings would therefore be an effective method of introduction of the VAM fungus.

Irrigation: Irrigation is not normally practised in tamarind cultivation, but promotes better growth during establishment and the early stages of growth, especially during the dry seasons (Yaacob and Subhadrabhandu, 1995). Where irrigation facilities are available watering should be done and repeated as the need arises in the early stages of growth. In later years as the deep tap root system develops, the need for watering becomes less. Flowering and fruiting is promoted by irrigation. In dry areas the use of water harvesting techniques during the rainy season should be considered as it encourages subsequent growth and fruiting (Chundawat, 1990). Mulching during the dry season will also help to reduce water losses due to evaporation.

Role of growth regulators and other harmones: It is necessary to improve the fruit set which is greatly influenced by environmental factors in which tamarind is grown. This problem could be solved by using the plant regulators viz. 4-CPA, NAA, GA3 and SADH. It was found that 4-CPA 15 ppm, was the most effective dose. The applications of all chemicals tended to incease the fruit set(30). Young or tender leaves of tamarind are used as a seasoning vegetable in some Indian and Thai food recipes because of its sourness and specific aroma. For this reason, foliar spray of tamarind with defoliants viz. Thiourea, urea, potassium nitrate, Ethephon and 2,4-D have been used for inducing new leaves. It was found that Ethephon at 200 ppm concentration is the best chemical substance for inducing young leaf initiation of tamarind. Other chemicals were ineffective. (32)

Varieties: There are very few well recognized tamarind varieties. However, in the recent past few seedling selections have been identified on the basis of fruit quality and yield. These are: Prathisthan from Fruit Research station, Aurangabad, Tree No 38 located at college of Agriculture, Pune, (Maharashtra) A high yielding type PKM- 1 has been developed by Coimbtore Agril. University (Tamil Nadu) (4).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests: One of the major pests of the tamarind tree in India is the Oriental yellow scale, Aonidiella orientalis. Tamarind scale, A. tamarindi, and black, or olive, scale, Saissetia oleae, are also partial to tamarind but of less importance. The mealybug, Planococcus lilacinus, is a leading pest of tamarind in India, causing leaf-fall and sometimes shedding of young fruits. Another mealybug, Nipaecoccus viridis, is less of a menace except in South India where it is common on many fruit trees and ornamental plants. Chionaspis acuminata-atricolor and Aspidiotus spp., suck the sap of twigs and branches and the latter also feeds on young fruits. White grubs of Holotrichia insularis may feed on the roots of young seedlings. The nematodes, Xiphinema citri and Longidorus elongatus may affect the roots of older trees. Other predators attacking the leaves or flowers include the caterpillars, Thosea aperiens, Thalarsodes quadraria, Stauropus alternus, and Laspeyresia palamedes; the black citrus aphid, Toxoptera aurantii, the whitefly, Acaudaleyrodes rachispora; thrips, Ramaswamia hiella subnudula, Scirtothrips dorsalis, and Haplothrips ceylonicus; and cow bugs, Oxyrhachis tarandus, Otinotus onerotus, and Laptoentrus obliquis.

Fruit borers include larvae of the cigarette beetle, Lasioderma serricorne, also of Virachola isocrates, Dichocrocis punctiferalis, Tribolium castaneum, Phycita orthoclina, Cryptophlebia (Argyroploca) illepide, Oecadarchis sp., Holocera pulverea, Assara albicostalis, Araecerus suturalis, Aephitobius laevigiatus, and Aphomia gularis. The latter infests ripening pods on the tree and persists in the stored fruits, as do the tamarind beetle, Pachymerus (Coryoborus) gonogra, and tamarind seed borer, Calandra (Sitophilus) linearis. The rice weevil, Sitophilus oryzae, the rice moth, Corcyra cepholonica, and the fig moth, Ephestia cautella, infest the fruits in storage. The lesser grain borer, Rhyzopertha dominica bores into stored seeds.


Diseases: In India, a bacterial leaf-spot may occur. Sooty mold is caused by Meliola tamarindi. Rots attacking the tree include saprot, Xylaria euglossa, brownish saprot, Polyporus calcuttensis, and white rot, Trametes floccosa. The separated pulp has good keeping quality but is subject to various molds in refrigerated storage (3).

Economic Potential and Uses

Tamarind (Tamarindus indica L.) is an economically important tree of India, which grows abundantly in the dry tracts of Central and South Indian States. Its life span is long and yields 150-500 kg fruits per tree. The pulpy portion of the fruits form the tamarind of commerce, which finds extensive use in culinary preparations. Indian production of tamarind is about 3 lakh tonnes per year. The country earns about Rs. 50 crores annually from the export of various tamarind products such as tamarind concentrate, tamarind powder, tamarind kernel powder (TKP) pickles and pastes. The fruit pulp is the richest natural source of tartaric acid (8-18%) and is the chief acidulant used in the preparation of foods in India. The major industrial use for the seeds is in the manufacture of tamarind kernel powder (TKP) or Tamarind starch, which is an important sizing material for the jute and textiles. The seeds are gaining importance as a rich source of proteins and valuable amino acids. Also, the seed kernels have been used as food in times of scarcity either alone or mixed with cereal flours. This review covers the chemical, technological and usage aspects of tamarind. (22)

Parts used

Pulp from the fruit, seeds, leaves, flowers, wood and bark.

Uses

Fruit pulp: The fruit pulp may be used as a fixative with turmeric or annatto in dyeing and has served to coagulate rubber latex. The pulp, mixed with sea water, cleans silver, copper and brass. (20)

Leaves: Tamarind leaves and flowers are useful as mordants in dyeing. A yellow dye derived from the leaves colors wool red and turns indigo-dyed silk to green. Tamarind leaves in boiling water are employed to bleach the leaves of the buri palm (Corypha elata Roxb.) to prepare them for hat-making. The foliage is a common mulch for tobacco plantings. The leaves are eaten by cattle and goats, and furnish fodder for silkworms–Anaphe sp. in India.


Flowers: Flowers are useful as mordants in dyeing. The flowers are rated as a good source of nectar for honeybees in South India. The honey is golden-yellow and slightly acid in flavor.


Seeds: The powder made from tamarind kernels has been adopted by the Indian textile industry as 300% more efficient and more economical than corn starch for sizing and finishing cotton, jute and spun viscose, as well as having other technical advantages. Seeds are roasted and eaten. It is commonly used for dressing homemade blankets. Other industrial uses include employment in color printing of textiles, paper sizing, leather treating, the manufacture of a structural plastic, a glue for wood, a stabilizer in bricks, a binder in sawdust briquettes, and a thickener in some explosives. The tannin-rich seedcoat (testa) is under investigation as having some utility as an adhesive for plywoods and in dyeing and tanning, though it is of inferior quality and gives a red hue to leather.


Wood: The sapwood of the tamarind tree is pale-yellow. The heartwood is rather small, dark purplish-brown, very hard, heavy, strong, durable and insect-resistant. It bends well and takes a good polish and, while hard to work, it is highly prized for furniture, panelling, wheels, axles, gears for mills, ploughs, planking for sides of boats, wells, mallets, knife and tool handles, rice pounders, mortars and pestles. The wood is valued for fuel, especially for brick kilns, for it gives off an intense heat, and it also yields a charcoal for the manufacture of gun-powder. The wood ashes are employed in tanning and in de-hairing goatskins. Young stems and also slender roots of the tamarind tree are fashioned into walking-sticks.

 

Twigs and barks: Tamarind twigs are sometimes used as "chewsticks" and the bark of the tree as a masticatory, alone or in place of lime with betelnut. The bark contains up to 7% tannin and is often employed in tanning hides and in dyeing, and is burned to make an ink. Bark from young trees yields a low-quality fiber used for twine and string. Galls on the young branches are used in tanning. Twigs and bark is used as a fuel in rural areas.


Harvesting Practices  
Tamarind fruits mature in late spring to early summer. Tamarinds may be left on the tree for as long as 6 months after maturity so that the moisture content will be reduced to 20% or lower. Fruits for immediate processing are often harvested by pulling the pod away from the stalk, which is left with the long, longitudinal fibers attached. In India, harvesters may merely shake the branches to cause mature fruits to fall and they leave the remainder to fall naturally when ripe. Pickers are not allowed to knock the fruits off with poles as this would damage developing leaves and flowers. To keep the fruit intact for marketing fresh, the stalks must be clipped from the branches so as not to damage the shell. A mature tree may annually produce 150-225 kg of fruits, of which the pulp may constitute 30 to 55%, the shells and fiber, 11 to 30 %, and the seeds, 33 to 40%. (1,49)

Produce and Products  (1,27,28,29,49)

Tamarind kerenel powder (TKP) or Tamarind starch: The major industrial use of the seeds is in the manufacture of Tamarind Kernel Powder (TKP). It is prepared by decorticating the seed and Kernels are crushed in impact pulveriser into pure powder which is a creamy white kernels. The TKP, when boiled in water containing boric acid and phenol as preservatives, gives a very good paper adhesive. In India, TKP is used as a source of carbohydrate for the adhesive or binding agent in paper and textile sizing, and weaving and jute products. The sizing property of TKP is due to the presence of up to 60 % of the polysaccharide. A high grade adhesive from tamarind seed kernels could be prepared by roasting the seeds at 110° C for 15 minutes, de-hulling to remove the testa, powdering and passing through a 180 mesh sieve and making a porridge by boiling with water. Then it is mixed with 200 % hot water, 5 % glucose and 12 % sodium bicarbonate and is ready for use. Both bread and biscuits, with up to 15% Tamarind Kerenal Powder in flour mix, were acceptable.

Pectins: Jellose (Pectins) is prepared on a large scale by adding TKP to 30-40 times its weight of boiling water, containing citric or tartaric acid at a concentration of 0.2 %. It is then stirred vigorously and boiled for a further 30-40 minutes. The resultant solution is kept overnight for setting and the supernatant liquid is siphoned off and concentrated under vacuum, passed through a filter press and then dried in a drum drier. The resultant product is pulverised in a ball mill. A good sample of jellose should have a relative viscosity of 5.0 % at 35º C in 0.5 % solution, which is some what higher than corn starch. Jellose is much cheaper than corn starch and is required in smaller quantities.

Tamarind Juice Concentrate: Tamarind juice concentrate (TJC) is a convenient product, as it is easy to dissolve and reconstitute in hot water. It can also be stored for long periods. TJC is prepared by extracting cleaned pulp with boiling water using the counter current principle, where dilute extracts are used for extracting fresh batches of the pulp. Using this process, an extract is obtained containing 20 % soluble solids. The extract is separated from the pulp by sieving and is concentrated under vacuum in a forced circulation evaporator. When the soluble solids reach 68 %, the pulp is placed in cans or bottles. It sets like jam on cooling. The yield of the concentrate is about 75 % of the pulp used. The quality of the product is assured by using recently harvested fruits free from insect and rodent damage. An Indian Standard is available for TJC to maintain the quality of the product.

Tamarind Pulp Powder: Tamarind pulp powder (TPP) is prepared by concentrating, drying and milling the pulp into a powder form. It is one of the convenient food products produced commercially by several manufacturers in India. Depending on the manufacturing process, wide variations in the physico-chemical characteristics are reported.

Tamarind Pickle: Pulp is used commercially to prepare tamarind pickle. The pickles are commonly used in Asia as an accompaniment to curries or other main meals. Pickles are hot, spicy and of salty-sour taste, and can be preserved for several months. Preservation is due to the presence of salt, increased acidity and spices. The preparations of pickles are simple and can be done at a household level.

Tamarind gum - Natural stabilizers to improve the foam stability and texture of starch-based frozen pudding (27). The gums (Tamarind gums) come from the seeds of the leguminous Tamarindus indicus (L.), cultivated in India. The pulp of the mature pods is used in curries and chutneys and is a good source of vitamin C. Seed coats are removed by parching. The dehulled seeds are then dry-milled to produce the PSG. The powdered gum disperses in hot and cold water; its maximum viscosity is reached when it is boiled. Viscosity of the hot paste is over 5 times that of maize starch at 5% concentration. PSG produced from the seeds can in part replace pectin in jams and jellies and acts as a stabilizer or thickener in various foods. It acts as a binder in pharmaceutical tablets, as a humectant and emulsifier in cosmetics, and as a sizing agent for jute and cotton fibres. (38)

Jam: In making tamarind jam, ripe fruits are shelled and the pulp is boiled for 10 minutes. The pulp is then drained and separated from the seeds. For every cup of pulp, two cups of brown sugar is added. The mixture is then cooked and constantly stirred while boiling until it becomes thick in consistency. The resulting jam is cooled, packed in dry, sterilised jars and sealed.

Syrup: Tamarind syrup is made by boiling immature fruit pulp until it is soft and then strained through cheesecloth. To every cup of juice, a half-teaspoon of baking soda is added. The mixture is boiled down to one-half the original quantity, removing the rising scum in the process. The juice is again strained and for every cup obtained, a quarter cup of sugar is added. The mixture is boiled again for 20 minutes. The cooled syrup is poured into sterilised bottles and sealed.

Candy: Sweetened tamarind fruit is made by peeling whole ripe fruits and pouring boiling (62° C) sugar syrup over the fruits and placing in a deep enamel basin. Boiling syrup is prepared by mixing three parts sugar with one part water. After soaking for three days, the fruits are drained of the old syrup and again covered with freshly prepared syrup. The process is repeated until the fruits are sweet enough. The sweetened fruits are arranged on bamboo racks, dried in sun and covered with a wire screen to keep off flies. Final drying is done in an oven at a low temperature. After cooling, the dry fruits are wrapped in cellophane.

Champoy: Tamarind fruits may also be processed into balls or “champoy” which is a common tamarind product in the Philippines. To one cup of pulp with seeds are added two cups of boiled and mashed sweet potato (Ipomea batatas), two cups of sugar, one-eighth cup of salt and one cup of water. The mixture is cooked in a moderate fire with constant stirring until it becomes thick and may be shaped into balls. The balls are rolled in sugar and wrapped in cellophane.

Ade: This is a refreshing tamarind drink prepared in the Philippines and some tropical American countries by mixing ripe pulp with sugar and water until the desired taste is attained. The simple home method of making Ade is to shell the fruits, place in 2-3 litres of water and allow to stand for a short time, then add a tablespoonful of sugar and shake vigorously. For a richer beverage the shelled tamarind should be covered with hot sugar syrup and allowed to stand for several days and finally diluted to the required strength with iced water and strained. Sometimes spicy seasonings, such as cloves, cinnamon, ginger, pepper or lime slices, are added to improve the taste. In Brazil, Ade is prepared by covering the shelled fruits in cold water for 10-12 days, then the seeds are removed and strained and a cup of sugar is added to every two cups of pulp. The mixture is boiled for 15-20 minutes and placed into glass jars topped with paraffin and diluted as desired. It is a popular drink in the tropics of America and it is bottled in carbonated form in Guatemala, Mexico and Puerto Rico.

Oil: Is extracted from the seeds.

TKP jelly: The gel strength remained unaffected by added citric acid. Thus, the low-cost TKP could be a good substitute for costly pectin for making jelly.

Lac: The tamarind tree is a host for the lac insect, Kerria lacca, that deposits a resin on the twigs. The lac may be harvested and sold as stick-lac for the production of lacquers and varnish. If it is not seen as a useful byproduct. (3,10,11) 

Feed: Some results indicates that tamarind wastes can replace up to 75% of maize in the diet of growing pigs without adversely affecting nutrient utilization. (20)

Processing Techniques  

Tamarind starch or Tamarind Kernel Powder: Tamarind seeds are thoroughly washed with water to free them from any adhering pulp. The seeds which float on water during washing are discarded and the submerged seeds are taken out and fumigated with sulphur dioxide gas or soaked in 25 per cent brine solution for an hour. The seeds are then dried, sorted out and stored in a dry place.

The recovery of starch from the dry seeds is done by anyone of the three methods described below.

In the first method, the dry seeds are parched for 30 minutes on hot sand or in a hot air chamber at 145-150°C to remove the testa; the parched seeds are mildly pounded with wooden hammers or passed through special decorticators. The overparched seeds are removed and the properly parched kernels are washed in cold water, soaked for 3 or 4 hours in 0.5 per cent sulphur dioxide solution, washed again in water and dried on a sand bath or in a hot air chamber, at 110°C, to a moisture content of 5 per cent. The dried kernels are powdered and packed in PVC bags. The decorticated seed is ground to the required mesh size by machines to obtain a yield of 55-60 %.


In the second method, dried seeds are gradually crushed in a slow speed disintegrator. Crushing separates the testa from the kernels; the testa are blown off. by passing a current of air through the crushed mass. The kernels are then ground in a high speed disintegrator.

In the third method, the dried seeds are soaked in cold brine of 25 per cent strength for 15 hours, washed with water and finally boiled in water for 5 minutes. The water adhering to the seeds is carefully drained, and the seeds are immersed in a bath of molten paraffin wax at 200°C for 20 seconds, cooled and decorticated. This process separates testa and radicles. The kernels thus obtained are defatted and powdered.


The powder tends to deteriorate during storage under humid conditions, hence storage in a dry place in moisture proof containers is important. Mixing with 0.5 % of sodium bisulphite before packing will prevent enzymatic deterioration. The TKP will become rancid and brown on storage and the storage ability and colour will be better if it is defatted. The general characteristics of a good tamarind seed powder is that it should have flavouring characteristics when dissolved in water and be free of any burnt or other undesirable flavours, it should have good keeping quality and be free from any insect pests, fungal growth or extraneous materials. (49,50)

Other Uses

Medicinal uses:

Pulp:Tamarind preparations are universally recognized as refrigerants in fevers and as laxatives and carminatives. Alone, or in combination with lime juice, honey, milk, dates, spices or camphor, the pulp is considered effective as a digestive, even for elephants, and as a remedy for biliousness and bile disorders, and as an antiscorbutic. In native practice, the pulp is applied on inflammations, is used in a gargle for sore throat and, mixed with salt, as a liniment for rheumatism. It is, further, administered to alleviate sunstroke, Datura poisoning, and alcoholic intoxication. Tamarind fruits can also help to kill parasites and reduce poisoning by alcohol. Because of their medical effects, some large-sized medical shops in Britain and America all have tamarind in their codex. (3,10,48,49)


Tamarind leaves and flowers, dried or boiled, are used as poultices for swollen joints, sprains and boils. Lotions and extracts made from them are used in treating conjunctivitis, as antiseptics, as vermifuges, treatments for dysentery, jaundice, erysipelas and hemorrhoids and various other ailments. (3,10,48,49)

 

The bark of the tree is regarded as an effective astringent, tonic and febrifuge. An infusion of the roots is believed to have curative value in chest complaints and is an ingredient in prescriptions for leprosy. The leaves and roots contain the glycosides: vitexin, isovitexin, orientin and isoorientin. The bark yields the alkaloid, hordenine (3,10,48,49).


Seed: The seedcoat, too, is astringent. Antioxidative activity of tamarind (Tamarindus indica L.) seeds was investigated, then 2-hydroxy-3',4'-dihydroxyacetophenone (TAO), methyl 3,4-dihydroxybenzoate (TA1), 3,4-dihydroxyphenyl acetate (TA2), and (-)-epicatechin (TA3) were isolated from the ethyl acetate extract and identified. These results suggest that tamarind seed coat, a byproduct of tamarind gum industries, can be used-as a safe and low-cost source of antioxidants. (3,10,25,26,48,49)

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country  

Tamarind fruit is an important condiment/ adjunct used as an acidic/ flavouring agent in the Indian cookery. Oil from the seeds is being extracted in minor quantities (4). Tamarind trees are evergreen growing to a height of about 20 to 80 feet. A full grown yields about 175 to 250 Kgs. of fruits. India is the major producer of sour tamarind in Asia and its annual production is in the range of 250,000 to 300,000 tonnes. Nearly 20,000 tonnes of Tamarind kernel powder (TKP) is also produced annually in India, but this could be doubled through improved post harvest and processing techniques. Annual returns from tamarind seed powder are estimated at 16,000,000-17,000,000 Indian rupees (US$ 346,400-368,050).


Economics of production: Trees will begin to bear from the fourth year with a pod yield of 40 kg/tree giving a net profit of Indian rupees 14,000 (US$ 321.69) per acre. When the trees reach maximum bearing in 10 years, a yield of about 100 kg/tree could be harvested with a profit of 38,000 Indian rupees (US$ 873.16) per acre.

 

Domestic Marketing: In most producing countries there is a domestic market for tamarind. The presence of local markets could be used as an indicator of commercial potential of any under-utilised species. However, to strengthen the local markets it will be necessary to establish the size of the market, target consumers and the type of products in demand. Therefore, efforts to find potential markets for tamarind products will be useful to exploit its potential. (35-49)

Export Potential

  • Since ancient times, India has been exporting processed tamarind pulp to western countries, mainly the European and Arab countries and more recently the United States of America.

  • Tamarind products that are exported from India are: the dried pulp, and the seed and seed powder. While, juice concentrates have a export potential in European countries.

  • India is one of the foremost exporters of tamarind in the world. Exports of fresh product from 1986-1992 was highly variable. During 1993, exports reached 11,145 tonnes valued at 10.8 million Indian rupees (US$ 240,300), and were in the form of fresh fruits, pulp, seed powder and paste. The importing countries are mainly Pakistan (3968 tonnes/Rs. 1.71 million/US$ 37,158) followed by UAE (1524 tonnes/Rs 1.95 million/US$ 43,388), Japan (1222 tonnes/Rs.1.23 million/US$ 27,368) and Yemen. The other importing countries include Germany, France, Malaysia, United Kingdom, Italy and Bangladesh. Some of these countries import tamarind for the manufacture of pharmaceuticals and some 90,000 kg (88.6 tonnes) of pulp has been imported annually for the drug trade. (49)

Miscellaneous Information

Processing methods for other products of Tamarind:

  • Volatile compouds: The volatile components from the fruit of Tamarindus indica L. were obtained by supercritical fluid extraction and analyzed by GC and GC/MS. A total of 16 compounds were identified accounting for 97.75%. Aromadendrene was the major constituent corresponding to 90% of the flavor constituents. (21)

  • Antioxidants: Supercritical carbon dioxide (SC-CO2) extraction was investigated for extraction of antioxidants from tamarind (Tamarindus indica L.) seed coat. Different combinations of pressure and temperatures were used with and without ethanol as modifier. As temperature and pressure were increased, more antioxidants were extracted. Using ethanol as the modifier gave more extraction yield of antioxidant compounds. The SC-CO2 extract with ethanol (30 MPa, 80 degrees C) was also a strong antioxidant for lard as well as linoleic acid autoxidation. These results suggest that the antioxidants included in tamarind seed coat can be extracted using SC-CO2 and that they may be used for increasing the shelf life of foods by preventing lipid peroxidation. (24)

  • Oil: Extraction of oil from the seeds can be done by one of the three processes by screw pressing, by direct solvent extraction or by prepress solvent extraction. Prepress solvent extraction utilizes a combination of two processes and is the most common and probably the most economical processes.

Other Usages: (49)

Fungicidal properties: The fruits are reported to have anti-fungal and anti-bacterial properties When the bitter principle is extracted with benzene and subsequently digested with petrol, it yields 0.67 % of a brown, odourless liquid named “tamarindienal,” identified as 5-hydroxy 2-oxo-hexa-3,5-dienal. It is a potent fungicidal agent to cultures of Aspergillus niger and Candida albicans and possesses a strong bactericidal activity towards cultures of Bacillus subtilis, Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Extracts from tamarind fruit pulps have also shown molluscicidal activity against Bulinus trancatus snails. This activity is believed to be due to the presence of saponins in the fruit. Tamarind plant extracts have been used to purify drinking water in Burkina Faso and Vietnam.

The seed husk has also been found to be an effective fish poison. It is reported that powdered seed husk added to water, even at low dosages of 5-10 mg/litre, killed several fish species, Labeo rohita, Catla catla, Cirrhinus mrigala, Cyprinus carpio, Oreochromis mossabicus and Channa marlius within two hours of its application.

Tamarind extract has also been reported to have an inhibitory effect on plant virus diseases. In India, extracts obtained from tamarind plant parts have completely inhibited the activity of both cowpea mosaic and the mung bean mosaic viruses. The unfolded leaves of tamarind, containing lupeol, are said to be effective in inhibiting viral and fungal diseases in plants.

Physical Properties: (14,49)

The bulk density of raw, whole seed was slightly greater than that of roasted kernels. The surface area of seeds was linearly related to weight per seed (r greater than or equal to 0.96, p less than or equal to 0.01). The angle of friction of the whole seeds was determined on rough, smooth, and very smooth surfaces. The angle of repose for roasted kernel (34-39 degrees) was higher than that of the raw whole seeds (31-35 degrees).

Swelling power, solubility and tolerance to organic solvents of the derivatives increased with increasing carboxymethylation. The interaction properties of TKP with calcium chloride and sodium tetraborate were found to be reversed upon carboxymethylation. (28)

Pasting and flow properties of cooked solutions of tamarind kernel powder (TKP), its carboxymethyl and hydroxypropyl derivatives, exhibited shear dependent and pseudoplastic flow behaviour characteristics. Chemical modification decreased the shear dependence of the solution viscosity as the degree of substitution increased. (29)
