Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Moderate sized tree found in subtropical regions like Himalayan foothills and Central Indian hills. Distributed from Punjab to Bengal, ascending to an altitude of about 7,000 ft. in the Himalayas from Jammu & Kashmir State to Nepal. Sparingly found in the forest area and mostly cultivated along agricultural fields on terrace risers.

The average rainfall in its distributional range is 1200-2500 mm. The absolute maximum shade temperature exceeds 380 C and minimum rarely drops to -20 C. Frost is common during the autumn and winter months. Grows on a variety of soils, ranging from sandy loam to shallow dry where growth is poor. The tree is grown on terrace risers and under irrigated conditions growth is much better than under rainfed conditions.

Strong light demander requiring complete overhead light for optimum growth. Adequate soil moisture and weed competition affect the establishment and growth of plants, as can be seen from seedlings putting up little growth when grown on dry hill slopes. The tree is frost hardy but severe frost kills the seedling which regrows during spring.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

A moderate sized tree with short bole and spreading crown. Bark dark gray and smooth. Leaves ovate, acuminate, serrate, rough, typical of the family. Fruit fleshy, 2-4 lobed. Seeds have hard testa and each fruit contain 2-4 seeds.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Breeding system and varietal improvement:

Phenology:

Flowers appear with the new flush of leaves and soon the fruits are formed. By September they attain full size and ripen during October-December.

Old leaves are shed during the months of March and April and new leaves appear during April and Amy. March-April is a leafless period.

Floral and seed biology:


Seed Germination & Propagation: 

Grown by entire transplanting. Seed coat is hard and therefore germination takes place after soaking the seed in water for more than 12 hrs. Seedling growth is fast, attaining a height of 15-20 cm in three months. A long taproot is formed during the first season and is longer than the shoot length; in dry locations seedling growth is slow and the shoot dies back with frost. Sapling growth is moderate, though exact data are not available.

Since the plant is mostly cultivated it is seldom found in the forest. Birds feeding on fruits assist in seed dispersal. Germination occurs when seeds settle on favourable niches; hence natural regeneration is scanty. The tree coppices and pollards well but is susceptible to fire and browsing by animals.

It can be propagated by seeds and transplantation of saplings from two months of age to one year old and also by cuttings and/or stumps. The seeds are collected from ripened fruits which are rubbed and washed to remove the flesh. About 12,000 to 15,000 seeds weigh a kg. They have a hard testa and can be stored well with no appreciable drop in vitality. A substantial quantity of fruit is eaten by birds if collection is delayed. For good seeds elite trees may have to be marked and no lopping allowed, either traditional or partial.

Of the different presoaking treatments, cooling boiled water for 12 hours is recommended to give best results. The seeds are sown in sunken nursery beds about 2 cm deep in lines 15 cm apart. Germination is reported to start in about 10 days and takes about a month to complete. The seedlings are spaced 10 cm apart in lines as they grow fast and attain plantable height during July with regular weeding and irrigation. They can also be raised in containers. For stump planting seedlings of 15 months of age are normally used.

Planting of seedlings is done during July in pits prepared in advance (of 30 cu) at a spacing of 3m X 3 m for block planting and 4 to 5 m for single row planting along the fields. However, protection of seedlings against attack by defoliator and browsing is recommended.

The plants firmly establish in one year, attaining a height of 2.4 m on best locations and start supplying fodder in about 2 years. After the rains the plants need irrigation once a month during the first year. Cuttings are put in nursery beds during the spring and are ready for transplanting during the rainy season.

Best time for planting is July.



MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:


Larvae of Diacrisa sp. var. Indica and Chasmina tibialis defoliate the plant while larvae of the family Cerambycidae bore the dead and dry wood.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts Used: Bark, twigs, branches,leaves and fruits.

Uses:

Wood is used as fuelwood. But because of its unpleasant odour, given the other alternative this is the last preference. It is used for oar shafts, shoulder poles, cot frames, bows, paddles, tool and axe handles, bows and several other purposes where elasticity and toughness are required. Wood specially suited for ploughs.

Other uses:

Fodder - This is planted widely for fodder. Most common fodder in Himalayan region. Leaves rich in protein and other nutrients. The leaves are not relished by cattle till they are fully mature. Young twigs are also lopped for fodder.

Bark - Inner bark yields a fiber used for rope making and clothing. It is also suitable for paper.

Branches used for basket making.

Fruit edible.

Wood reported to be suitable for paper making.

Suitable for field edges.

Twigs and branches - The bark of tree twigs on being crushed yields a mucilaginous substance which works as an effective detergent to cleanse the human body. Women wash their hair with this detergent substance. Dry twigs left in water ponds for some months and later beaten with a wooden hammer yield a fibrous material that makes good rope.

Miscellaneous Information

Soil stabilizer. Growth upto 1000 m from sea level.
Wood: Wood is yellowish white or grey with an unpleasant odour, heavy (sp. gr. 0.75; wt. 48 lb./cu. ft.), even and narrowly grained and fine-textured. It is hard, tough and elastic. Not durable in exposed position, but is fairly durable under cover.
