Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

An exotic leafy evergreen shrub, cultivated for its pleasantly fragrant leaves. Most sources interpret the Latin name as ros marinus "dew of the sea". Often rosemary is used as a ground cover along roads and on embankments because of it's beauty and deep root system, which helps stabilize the soil and allows the plant to withstand hot, dry periods. The plant is considered a good source of nectar for bees, having blossoms that both attract bees and appear when few other plants are blooming (5).
                                                                                                                                                  
Climate and soil
Rosemary requires a temperate humid zone (mean annual temperature of 6-12˚C; mean annual rain fall of 1000-2000 mm) to the subtropical semi-arid to humid zones (18-24˚C; 500-2000 mm). It’s pH tolerance ranges from 4.5-8.3, but preferably 6-7.5. In the Mediterranean region, rosemary thrives best on calcareous soils, on dry sunny mountain slopes and near the coast where it is frequently exposed to fog and salt spray (Rosmarinus means ‘dew of the sea’). Rosemary can survive in areas with mild winters, about not in localities where the temperature frequently falls below -3˚C. Ones established rosemary roots deeply and is drought-resistant (4). Cultivated in gardens in cool places, temperate and hilly regions with dry to moderately moist climate (1).

Availability :

It is a native of southern Europe and grows wildly on dry rocky hills in Mediterranean and Himalayan regions (1). It is occasionally cultivated as an ornamental in the mountainous areas of Java. It is occasionally cultivated as an ornamental plant in mountainous areas of Java (4). In the Philippines it is currently grown in small scale.

Conservation/ Threat Status : Wild and cultivated.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

An evergreen, usually erect, brushy shrub up to 2 m tall and wide. Stem indistinctly quadrangular, finely gray pubescent. Leaves opposite, tufted on the branches, sessile to short petiolate; blade linear, 1-5 cm x 1-2 mm, base attenuate, margin entire but revolute, apex obtuse, leathery, dark glossy sea-green and subglabrous above, white-felted tomentose beneath, aromatically fragrant when crushed. Under favourable growing condition and optimal cultural management, rosemary can remain productive for up to 30 years.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Inflorescence racemose, axillary, 5-10 flowered, 0.5-2.5 cm long, terminating short lateral branches; pedicel 2-5 mm long; calyx campanulate, 2-lipped, 5-6 mm long, densely stellate tomentose, upper lip small and 3-dentatate, lower lip 2-lobed; corolla tubular, 2-lipped, 10-13 mm long, pale blue or blue (seldom white), upper lip erect or recurved, 2-lobed, ovate, about 4 mm long, lower lip 3-lobed, about 7 mm long, with large concave middle lobe; 2-anterior stamens perfect, 7-8 mm long, ascending under the base of the upper lip, 2 posterior stamens reduced to hardly visible staminodes; pistil with deeply 4-partite ovary, style incurved, 1.5 cm long ending into 2 short, unequal branches with stigma (4).  

Fruit
Fruit composed of 4 subglobose to obovoid nutlets, about 2 mm long, glabrous and smooth.

Breeding system and varietal improvement: Propagated by stem cuttings, division, layers and seeds.

Phenology:
Flowering in April and May (5). Flowering is initiated when plants are 2 or more years old (4). Flowers during mid spring – early summer, but may also flower intermittently most of the year in sheltered sites (3).

Pollinators:

Floral and seed biology: Seeds tiny, black, smooth, ovoid, and sub-globose.


CUTLIVATION ASPECT:
Seed germination:
Seeds of rosemary are slow to germinate taking about 3-4 weeks before emerging from the soil. To enhance germination the temperature should remain below 18 ˚C. Seedlings are likewise slow to develop, becoming a dense shrub with a diameter of 60 cm and a height of 90 cm only by the end of the second growing season.

Local cultivars: 
There are many varieties and forms of rosemary available and the species is grown as an ornamental and hedgerow plant. Bog rosemary, Andromeda species, and wild or marsh rosemary, Ledum palustre, are members of the Ericaceae family and not related (5).

Chromosome number:
2n = 20, 24

Selection of planting material:
Stem cuttings are used as planting material.

Planting:
Rosemary is commonly propagated by cuttings, division or by air-layering. Seeds are sometimes used, but they are produced only under very favourable growing conditions and often only 10-20% of the seeds germinate. Transplanting to the field is done at a spacing of 45 cm between plants in rows 1.2 m apart. It is also common to produce in containers in greenhouses (4). Cuttings 15 cm long should be planted in a specially prepared bed of sandy soil to a depth of c. 10 cm (Krumbiegel, 33; Gopalaswamiengar, 561; Chopra et al., J. Sci. Industr. Res. 1949, 8, 20; Muenscher & Rice, 122). For commercial plantation, seedlings or rooted cuttings are planted in the field 45 cm apart in rows 120 cm apart (1).

Growth of rosemary raised in containers strongly depends on the type of growing medium and the fertilizer regime used. The use of peat and perlite as growing medium results in taller plants yielding higher amount of essential oil compared with those raised in a medium of peat, perlite and soil. Plant fresh weight, however, is not affected. Growth in both media is also satisfactory when plants are provided with an adequate supply of controlled-release or liquid fertilizers (4).

Management after planting:
Rosemary will benefit from balanced fertilizer applications, with K being particularly critical for high essential-oil yield. In cold areas, the crop should be given heavy mulching to prevent winter injury. It should only be moderately watered to prevent root rot.


MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:Rosemary is attacked by Phytocoris rosmarini (4).  

Diseases
Attacked by Orthotylus ribesi in Spain and by Sclerotinia sclerotiorum in India (4).  

Nematode Induced Diseases 
Susceptible to the root-knot nematode Meloidogyne incognita (4).

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Rosemary is one of the most effective spice, widely used in food processing. Fresh or dried leaf in all kinds of meat, marinades, bouquet garni, with baked fish, rice, salads, sparingly with egg dishes, apples, summer wing cups and fruit cordials, in vinegar & oil. Leaf & flower calyx strongly aromatic, used in potpourri, and tussie – mussies, herb pillows (3). It the only spice commercially available for use as an antioxidant in Europe and US. One of the main potential uses is the suppression of warmed over flavour (WOF) and its prime use is as flavouring agents (2). Cultivated for ornamentals and hedges. Leaves yield 1-2% of volatile oil (Oil of Rosemary) used in perfumery, scenting soaps and hair lotions, room sprays, inhalants, denaturing alcohol and medicine. Superior grades are employed for blending in eau-de-Cologne for flavouring of meats, sausages, fruit salads, jams, biscuits, breads, soups, table sauces and other food products. It finds application in denaturing alcohol and is popular in aromatherapy. Rosemary oleoresin is used in the food industry as a natural antioxidant, for instance in cooking meat products (4). Fresh tender tops are used for garnishing and for flavouring cold drinks, pickles, soups and other foods. Leaves as condiment, dried and powdered, and added to cooked meats, fish, poultry, stews, sauces, dressings, preserves and jams. Sometimes added to biscuits. Flowers are excellent source of honey (Muenscher & Rice, 124) (1). In cosmetic, used in shampoo for deepening the colour of dark hair, in washing water, for astringent skin lotions. Domestically as insect repellent, for strewing, and in house hold linen and clothing, to perfume furniture polish & candles (3).  
The fresh or dried leaves are excellent flavouring agents in vegetables, meat (particularly lamb, veal and roasted chicken), sauces, stews, herbal butters, cream soups, fruit salads, jams, biscuits and bread.

Rosemary is very popular as an ornamental plant used as a ground cover, hedge or shrub and is even transformed by hobbyists into bonsai or planted in hanging baskets. The leaves and flowers can be carefully dried and sold in elegant sachets and potpourris. For the last 1000 years in Europe, rosemary has been a symbol of happiness, fidelity and love, and a wedding and funeral flower (4). Rosemary vinegar is a powerful disinfectant (5).

Parts Used: Fresh and dried leaves.


Harvesting Practices: Once established, but before rosemary becomes woody, the terminal shoots (25-30 cm from the tip) are harvested once or twice each season. The shoots are either dried or, if for sale in the fresh-herb market, bundled together in bunches of 8-12, tied with rubber bands. When the essential oil is to be extracted the plants are cut 2-3 years after planting at the onset of flowering, when the oil content is at its maximum.
Yield: Yields of fresh leaves of rosemary amount to 3 t/ha, and with a content of 0.1%, essential-oil yield is approximately 3 kg/ha (4).

Post Harvest Handling 
Bunched fresh shoots of rosemary are put in Styrofoam containers and delivered as soon as possible to fresh-market outlets. The whole fresh leaves can be frozen, preserving the flavour best. For the dried herb market, the shoots should be dried immediately on screen trays in a dark, sheltered area with good ventilation. The dried leaves are simply stripped by hand from the stems and subsequently stored in closed containers (4).


Produce and Products:


Chemistry:

Antioxidant activity of rosemary extracts is due to two phenolic diterpenes – carnosol and carnosic acid (Bracco et al. 1981). Several other diterpenes contributing to the antioxidant activity are: rosmanol, epirosmanol, isorosmanol, rosmaridiphenol and rosemariquinone (Nakatani and Inatani 1981; Houlihan et al. 1984; 1985). The other components are rosmarinic acid (2nd most frequently occurring caffeic acid ester), chlorogenic acid. Rosmanol and carnosic acid have more antioxidant potential than carnosol (Nakatani and Inatani 1984; Schwartz and Ternes 1992; Richheimer et al. 1996). Carnosol and carnosic acid are powerful inhibitors of lipid peroxidation in microsomal and liposomal systems (Aruoma et al. 1992) (2). Leaves contain 1-2% volatile oil, a saponin, tannin, ursolic acid, amyrins, betulin and rosmarinic acid. The chief constituents of oil of rosemary are: pinene, camphene, cineol, verbenone (character-impact compound), camphor, borneol (responsible for pungent, camphoraceous odour and burning taste) and bornyl acetate (1).  

Carnosic acid, the major phenolic diterpene present in the leaves, has been found to be several times affective than the commercial food preservatives butylated hydroxyanisol (BHA) and butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) in controlling oxidation in soya-bean oil. Rosemary also possesses some antibacterial and antifungal properties. Extracts of the plant have been found to inhibit skin tumerigenesis in test animals. The volatile oil of rosemary has been reported to induce hyperglycenia and to inhibit insulin release in test rabbits. 

Dried leaves contain 1-2% volatile oil. Verbenone is the character-impact compound in rosemary; the pungent, camphoraceous odour and burning taste is attributed to borneol: the cooling and minty note to camphor; the fresh aroma to cineole; and the warm, piny scent to α-pinene. The phenols thymol, carvacrol and eugenol also play an important role in the flavouring properties of the oil. 

Three types of rosemary oils are some times recognized: a camphor type (produced mainly in Spain), a 1,8-cineole type (produced mainly in Morocco) and a verbenone + bornyl acetate type that is a cottage-industry product from Corsica. In herbal medicine rosemary oil is used to treat acute middle ear infection and to stimulate liver gallbladder. It is said to be very beneficial for dry skin in skin-care products. Verbenone is also an insect pheromone. Synthetic verbenone is used in programmes to control several bark betle spices in pine trees. Rosemary is an excellent source of nectar for honeybees. Rosemary oil has in the past been adulterated with turpentine, oil of pine, certain fractions of camphor oil (i.e., camphor) and eucalyptus oil (i.e., 1,8-cineole).

Per 100 g edible portion of rosemary contain approx.: water – 9.3 g, protein – 4.9 g, total lipids – 15.2 g, carbohydrates – 46.5 g, fibre – 17.6 g, ash – 6.5 g, (Ca – 1.3 g, Fe – 29 mg, Mg – 220 mg, P – 70 mg, K – 955 mg, Na – 50 mg, Zn – 3.2 mg), ascorbic acid – 61.2 mg, thiamine – 0.51 mg, niacin – 1.0 mg, vitamin A – 3128 IU, and phytosterols – 58 mg. Energy value is 1387 kJ/100 g. 

In the United States the regulatory status ‘generally recognized as safe’ has been accorded to rosemary (GRAS 2991), rosemary oil (GRAS 2992) and rosemary oleoresin (GRAS 3001). The maximum permitted level of rosemary oil in food products is about 0.003% (4).

Processing Techniques:

Rosemary oleoresin, rosmariquinone, Oil of Rosemary – obtained by steam distillation of leaves, flowering tops and twigs. The oil is pale yellow or almost colourless liquid with camphoraceous taste and characteristic odour, and is sometimes adulterated with eucalyptus, sage and turpentine oils (Guenther, III 697; Arctander 558-59; Poucher, I, 361; Youngken, 719; IS: 3249-1965) (1). 

Other uses: Medicinal uses
In traditional medicine rosemary is thought to fortify the brain and refresh the memory. Flowering tops and leaves are considered carminative, diaphoretic, diuretic, aperient, emmenagogue, stimulant, stomachic and astringent. Rosemary also serves as a household remedy for headaches, bruises, colds, nervous tension and baldness. Rosemary leaves are therapeutically allowed internally for dyspeptic complaints, and externally for rheumatic diseases and circulatory problems (4). All parts of the plant are astringent, serve as nervine tonic and stomachic. Useful in atonic dyspepsia. Vapour bath gives relief of rheumatism, paralysis and incipient catarrhs. Dried leaves are smoked for relief of asthma. A decoction of leaves is employed as an abortifacient. Oil is used medicinally in rubefacient liniments. It is a mild irritant, carminative, diaphoretic, antibacterial, protistocidal and stimulant. 5% tincture is used as a circulatory and cardiac stimulant. Useful in tardy menstruation. An emulsion of oil is useful as a gargle in curing sore-throat (Guenther, III, 709-10; Poucher, I, 361; U.S. D., 1955, 1200; B.P.C., 1963, 711; Chem. Abstr., 1964, 61, 1703; 1959, 53, 11511; 1962, 56, 13310; Hall, Acta phytother., Amst., 1955, 2,(5), 14). An infusion of the plant with borax is employed as hair wash to prevent premature baldness. Tea made of rosemary is used in Central America to cause temporary sterility (Wren, 261; Quisumbing, 832; Watt & Breywe-Brandwijk. 525; Hall, loc. cit., Laszlo & Henshaw, Science, 1954, 119, 626) (1). Rosemary wine when taken in small quantities acts as a quieting cordial to a weak heart subject to palpitation, and relieves accompanying dropsy by stimulating the kidneys. It is made by chopping up sprigs of green rosemary and pouring on them white wine, which is strained off after a few days and is then ready for use. By stimulating the brain and nervous system, it is a good remedy for headaches caused by feeble circulation. The young tops, leaves and flowers can be made into an infusion, called rosemary tea, which, taken warm, is a good remedy for removing headache, colic, colds and nervous diseases, care being taken to prevent the escape of steam during its preparation. It will relieve nervous depression. A conserve, made by beating up the freshly gathered tops with three times their weight of sugar, is said to have the same effect (5).
