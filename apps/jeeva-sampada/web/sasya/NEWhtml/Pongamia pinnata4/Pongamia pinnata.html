Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
It is a medium-sized evergreen tree of minor economic importance in India. The tree is considered to be a native of western ghats and is mainly found along the banks of streams and rivers or near sea coast in beach and tidal forests (1, 6). The natural distribution of pongamia is along coasts and riverbanks in India and Burma. This species has also been introduced to humid tropical lowlands in the Philippines, Malaysia, Australia, the Seychelles, the United States and Indonesia (2). Grows in dry places far in the interior, up to an elevation of 1000 mt. It is often grown as a road side avenue tree nearly all over India. It resists drought well, moderately frost hardy and highly tolerant to salinity. In localities in which it is known to have been cultivated successfully the obsolute maximum shade temperature varies from 1000 to 1200 F., the obsolute minimum from under 300 to over 600 F., and the rainfall from 20 to over 100 inches (3). Pongamia can grow on most soil types ranging from stony to sandy to clayey, including Vertisoils. It does not do well on dry sands. It is highly tolerant of salinity. It is common along waterways or seashores, with its roots in fresh or salt water (2, 12).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A medium sized glabrous tree, with a short bole and spreading crown, up to 8 mt height, 1.5 mt in girth. The trunk is generally short with thick branches spreading into a dense hemispherical crown of dark green leaves. The bark is thin gray to greyish- brown, smooth or covered with tubercles and yellow on the inside. The taproot is thick and long; lateral roots are numerous and well developed. The leaves imparipinnate, alternate, compound pinnate leaves consist of 5 or 7 leaflets which are arranged in 2 or 3 pairs, and a single terminal leaflet. Leaflets are 5-10 cm long, 4-6 cm wide, ovate or ellliptic and pointed at the tip (2).

Flowers lilac or white tinged wit pink or violet in axillary racemes. Flowers fragrant, white to pinkish, paired along rachis in axillary, pendent, long racemes or panicles; calyx campanulate or cup-shaped, truncate, short-dentate, lowermost lobe sometimes longer; standard suborbicular, broad, usually with 2 inflexed, basal ears, thinly silky-haired outside; wings oblique, long, somewhat adherent to the obtuse keel; keel petals coherent at apex; stamens monadelphous, vexillary stamen free at the base but joined with others into a closed tube; ovary subsessile to short-stalked, pubescent; ovules 2, rarely 3; style filiform, upper half incurved, glabrous; stigma small, terminal (4).

Pods compressed, woody, indehiscent, yellowish grey when dry, varying in size and shape, elliptic to obliquely oblong, 4-7.5 cm long and 1.7-3.2 cm broad with a short curved beak, seeds usually one, rarely two, elliptical or reniform, 1.7-2 cm long and 1.2-1.8 cm broad, wrinkled with reddish brown, leathery testa. The testa contains brown pigments while the cotyledons possess oil drops and aleurone grains. The seed contains about 27 to 39% oil.(5)

Phenology :
Pongamia pinnata is almost an evergreen tree except in dry localities where it becomes leafless for a short period in May. The racemes of lilac flowers appear from April- June. The pods ripen from March to May the following year (3, 12).

Pollinators:

Seed Biology :


Seedling Germination :
Seed germinates within two weeks of sowing. Seedlings attain a height of 25-30 cm in their first growing season. Germination is hypogeous. The thick radicle emerges from one end of the seed, and the primary root attains some length before the plumule appears, the young shoot arching to extricate itself, and then strengthening. Seeds retain viability for atleast one year, if carefully stored (2).

Back to Top

 

Cultivation:

Natural reproduction is through seed or by root suckers. The seeds are set to retain viability for a year. Artificial regeneration is carried out through direct sowing or transplanting one year old seedlings raised in nursery. Root or shoot cuttings can also be used for this purpose. It is a good coppicer and is regarded as a fast growing species. Pongamia is easily established by direct seeding or by planting nursery-raised seedlings or stump cuttings of 1-2 cm root-collar diameter. In peninsular India, the seeding season is April to June. Seed germinates within two weeks of sowing. Seedlings attain a height of 25-30 cm in their first growing season. Transplanting to the field should occur at the beginning of the next rainy season when seedlings are 60 cm in height. Seedlings have large root systems. Soil should be retained around the roots during transplanting. Seedling survival and growth benefit from annual weed control for the first three years after transplanting.

The spacing adopted in avenue plantings is about 8 m between plants. In block plantings, the spacing can range from 2 x 2 to 5 x 5 m. Pongamia seedlings withstand shade very well and can be interplanted in existing tree stands. This species can be regenerated by coppice management. Because the tree tolerates moderate levels of salinity, pongamia is an ideal candidate for saline soil reclamation (2).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

Pongamia attracts many pests. Among the insect pests of living trees, defoliators, leaf miners and sap suckers are important. The larvae of some insects belonging to Lycaenidae, Hesperiidae, Eucosmidae, Nymphalidae, Sphingidae defoliate the tree. The leaf miners are Acrocercops anthracuris Meyrick and Lilthocolletis virgulata Meyrick. The insects feeding on the sap of foliage and branches of this tree belong to Aleyrodidae, Coccidae and Pentatomidae families. Some of the important pests are Parnara mathias, Gracillaria sp., Indarbela quadrinotata, Myllocerus curvicornis, and Acrocercops sp. Attacks by these insects cause whitish streaks and the formation of galls on affected leaves.


Diseases:

Some of the disease causing organisms are: Viruses, Sandal Spike Virus. Several fungi attack the seedlings and the trees. Ganoderma lucidum causes root rot and Fomes merillii (Murrill) Sacco & Trott attacks the base of living trees. Sphaceloma pongamiae Wani & Thirum. attacks the tender shoots and leaves and causes early defoliation in the seedlings and trees.Fungi. Fusicladium pongamiae, Phyllachora pongamiae, Ravenelia hobsoni, Ravenelia stictica.

Economic Potential and Uses

The pongamia oil, also called as Karanj oil is acrid reddish brown nondrying oil that is rich in unsaponifiable matter and oleic acid. The oil is chiefly used for leather tanning, lighting and to a smaller extent in soap making. The oil can be used as a lubricant for heavy lathes, chains, small gas engines and heavy engines. It has also been tried as fuel in diesel engines. The oil is used in the villages for illumination purposes. The cake is mostly used as manure. Some places it is also used as food ingredient. The oil is highly esteemed for medicinal purposes. It is applied in scabies, leucoderma and other cutaneous diseases. The oil and its active component 'Karanjin' possesses insecticidal and anti-bacterial properties (1). Pongamia oil showed inhibitory effects on Bacillus anthracis, Bacillus mycoides, Bacillus pulilus, Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas mangiferae, Salmonella typhi, Sarcina lutea, Staphylococcus albus, Staphylococcus aureus, and Xanthomonas campestris (4).

Uses

Soap made from crude oil tends to darken due to a component, Isolonchocarpin, which gives a wine red colour in the presence of alkali. In rural areas the leaves are used to prevent infestation of grains. The cake after oil extraction may be used as a manure. The presence of a hypotensive principle and a substance producing uterine contraction has been reported. Powdered seed and rind is valued as a febrifuge and tonic and is also used in bronchits and whooping cough. The wood is used for yokes of bullock carts, ploughs, solid cart-wheels, rafters of thached cottages, oil mills, furniture and small turnery articles. The wood has been tried as a source of paper pulp in admixture with other hardwoods. Leaves are eaten by cattle and readily consumed by goats. Its fodder value is greatest in arid regions. Pongamia is often planted in homesteads as a shade or ornamental tree and in avenue plantings along roadsides and canals. Incorporation of leaves and the presscake into soils improves fertility. Bark is used for cordage, leaves are used as fodder and also as green manure. Dried leaves are used as an insect repellent in stored grains. The presscake, when applied to the soil, has pesticidal value, particularly against nematodes. String and rope can be made from the bark fiber (2).

Harvesting Practices
The tree starts bearing at the age of 4-7 years. The fruits come to harvest at different periods of the year in different parts of the country. But the harvest season extends in general from Nov-Dec to May- June (1). No organised collection of seed is undertaken and the part of the produce is not collected. The most common method involves climbing the tree and beating off the pods with sticks. The seed yield per tree ranges from about 10 kg to more than 50 kg. There are 1500-1700 seeds per kg.

Produce and Products

Seeds yield oil which is used for tanning, leather and soap making apart from this it has many medicinal uses. Seed cake has high nitrogen content and is mostly used as a manure. Wood is employed in small article making. Well decomposed flowers are used as compost. Bark is used for making ropes and strings.

Chemistry

The fatty acid composition of the oil: Palmitic 3.7-7.9%, Stearic 2.4-8.9%, Arahidic 2.2-4.7%, Behenic 4.2-5.3%, Lignoceric 1.1-3.5%, Oleic 44.5-71.3%, Linoleic 10.8-18.3% and eicosenoic 9.5-12.4%. The non fatty acid components of the oil include Karanjin and pongamol (1).

Processing Techniques

The seeds are collected manually and decorticated using a wooden hammer. Winnowing separates the hulls. The oil is extracted from the kernels by traditional expellers. They yield about 24 to 26% of oil and 68 to 72% of cake. Solvent extraction of oil is also possible (1).

Other Uses
Dry flowers are used in decoction to quench thirst for diabetes patients. The juice of leaves is used in diarrhoea and cough. Juice of roots is used for cleansing foul ulcers and closing fistulous sores and for cleaning teeth and strengthening gums (1). The fruits and sprouts are used in folk remedies for abdominal tumors in India. In sanskritic India, Powdered seeds are valued as a febrifuge, tonic and in bronchitis, whooping cough and also for skin ailments. Today the oil is used as a liniment for rheumatism. Leaves are active against Micrococcus; their juice is used for colds, coughs, diarrhea, dyspepsia, flatulence, gonorrhea, and leprosy. Bark is used internally for bleeding piles. Juices from the plant, as well as the oil, are antiseptic. It is said to be an excellent remedy for itch, herpes, and pityriasis versicolor. Bark has been used for beriberi. Young shoots have been recommended for rheumatism. Ayurvedic medicine described the root and bark as alexipharmic, anthelmintic, and useful in abdominal enlargement, ascites, biliousness, diseases of the eye, skin, and vagina, itch, piles, splenomegaly, tumors, ulcers, and wounds; the sprouts, considered alexeteric, anthelmintic, apertif, and stomachic, for inflammation, piles and skin diseases; the leaves, anthelmintic, digestive, and laxative, for inflammations, piles and wounds; the flowers for biliousness and diabetes; the fruit and seed for keratitis, piles, urinary discharges, and diseases of the brain, eye, head, and skin, the oil for biliousness, eye ailments, itch, leucoderma, rheumatism, skin diseases, worms, and wounds. Yunani use the ash to strengthen the teeth, the seed, carminative and depurative, for chest complaints, chronic fevers, earache, hydrocele, and lumbago; the oil, styptic and vermifuge, for fever, hepatalgia, leprosy, lumbago, piles, scabies, and ulcers.

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

A single tree is said to yield 9–90 kg seed per tree, indicating a yield potential of 900––9000 kg seed/ha, 25% of which might be rendered as oil (assuming 100 trees/ha). In general, Indian mills extract 24–27.5% oil, village crushers, 18–22% oil. The total quantity available for collection in the country is estimated at about 1,10,000 tons against the actual collection of only 26,000 tons and the value of the current production is estimated to be 78 Million Rupees.

Wherever it is grown, the wood (calorific value 4,600 kcal/kg) is burned for cooking fuel. The thick oil from the seeds is used for illumination, as a kerosene substitute, and lubrication. It would seem that with upgraded germplasm one could target for 2 MT oil and 5 MT firewood per hectare per year on a renewable basis. The oil has been tried as fuel in diesel engines, showing a good thermal efficiency (4).
