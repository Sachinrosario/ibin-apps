Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:

Indian Mallow I an annual, softly tomentose herb, sometimes reaching upto 3.6m in height, occurring in Kashmir, Punjab, Orissa, Bihar, Andhra Pradesh, Maharashtra and Gujarat, ascending to an altitude of 1500m. The plant is native to China, and has run wild in some areas in Punjab and Deccan, its cultivation has also been reported in some places in India (1).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

The leaves are orbicular-cordate, with a long point, nearly entire, long petioled, flowers yellow, solitary, axillary, capsules with 15-20 carpels, longer than sepals, oblong, hairy, each with two long, horizontally spreading awns; seeds pilose.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
fiber - The stem yields a fast fiber (4) which has an ultimate fiber length, 2.5-4.5 mm and diameter, 18.0-18.5u, is greyish white, strong, coarse and lustrous, with similar characteristics and uses as jute. It is reported tobe superior to jute and finer than Mamla hemp and has considerable tensile strength; it can be readily dyed any colour. Its natural lustre can become more prominent with aniline dyes. The fiber is employed for rugs, ropes, twines in China(2), and threads, ropes for slippers, mats, nets, etc. in Japan (1). They are also utilized in paper making (3).

MEDICINAL -

The leaves contain mucilage. They are cooked and eaten for bleeding piles. Their extract is reported to be diuretic and demulcent and as an emollient formentation; along with ghee it is considered a remedy for diarrhoea. A decoction of the leaves is used as mouth wash in toothache and tender gums; it is also stated to be useful in gonerrhoea, inflammation of bladder, as a wash for wounds and ulcers, and for enema and vaginal injection.

The root is a nervine tonic and antipyretic and is given in piles. Mixed with honey, chaulmoogra oil and fresh paste of sandalwood, the root is reported to be an efficaceous treatment for lucoderma; addition of the oil from Psoralea corylifolia Linn. may improve the efficacy of the treatment. An infusion of the roots is considered a good cooling remedy in fevers and is also given in strangury and haematuria. The decoction is stated to be internally given for stones in bladder and also applied as a wash in eye diseases.

The seeds are considered laxative in piles, demulcent and used in treating cough and are also distinctly useful in gonorrhoea, gleet and chronic cystitis. A decoction of the seeds is given in dysentry and fistulae, and eye sores (1).

Parts Used:

Leaves, stem, seeds, roots.


Harvesting Practices:
The plants are ready for cutting in 120-150 days (1). To obtain the best quality fiber, the stems should be harvested at the right time, i.e. at full flowering, and the green stems should be retted just after they have been cut (2).

Produce and Products:
fiber-rugs, ropes, twines, nets etc.

Other uses:
The seed contains (dry basis) : protein, 23.1; and semi-drying oil, 15.3%. The oil from USA has the following fatty acid compsoition. Palmitic, 12.8; stearic 0.9; oleic, 14.8; and linoleic, 60.9%.

An ethanolic extract of the roots yields mucilage which contains pentoses, methyl pentoses and hexoses. Pentosans, methyl pentosans and uronic acids are also present in the mucilage (1).

Processing Techniques :

Stagnant water in ponds is used for retting. The stems are made up into bundles of about 6 in. to 1 ft. in diameter and are then submerged under the water. The time taken to ret depends on the climatic conditions at the time and also on the state of the stems; it varies from three to five days in August, when temperatures are lower, or if dried stems or stems from over-mature plants are being retted.

In Tientsin (China), one sack factory was making lacks entirely of Abutilon by putting the fiber through a special preparing process. This consisted of passing the fiber through a special preparing process. This consisted of passing the fiber twice through the softening machine, when oil was sprayed on it, and then batching, for a period of three months, the fiber intended for warp yarns. For weft yarns, however, the fiber was only batched for ten days. This batching process made the fiber softer and more elastic, so that it did not break when used for weaving into sacks. (2)
