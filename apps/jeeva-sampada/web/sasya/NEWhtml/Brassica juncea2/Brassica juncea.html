Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Brassica juncea L. (rai) was originally introduced from China into north-eastern India. From where it has extended into Afghanistan via the Punjab. Eastern Afghanistan, together with the adjoining north-western India, is one of the independent centres of brown sarson (Brassica campestris var. brown sarson). Yellow sarson (B. campestris var. yellow sarson) is commonly grown in the eastern parts of India where it shows much diversity of forms. (Eruca sativa Mill.) Taramira is a relatively recent introduction into India. It is believed to be a native of southern Europe and North Africa (1). Mustard is an annual herb with seedlings that emerge rapidly, but then usually grow slowly. Plants cover the ground in 4 to 5 weeks with favorable moisture and temperature conditions. The taproots will grow 5 ft into the soil under dry conditions, which allows for efficient use of stored soil moisture (2).

Under the name rapeseed and mustard, several oilseeds belonging to the crucieferae are grown in India.

1. Brown mustard, commonly called rai (raya or laha)—Brassica juncea (L.) Czern. & Coss

2. Sarson-

(i)Yellow sarson—B. campestris L. var. sarson Prain

(ii)Brown sarson—B. campestris L. var. dichotoma Watt

3.Toria (lahi or Maghi Labi)—B. campestris L. var. toria Duth.

4. Taramira or Tara (Eruca sativa Mill.)

In trade, sarson, toria and Taramira are known as rapeseed, and rai as mustard (1).


Banarsi rai (B. nigra Koch.) which does not fall under any of the four groups is a garden crop used as spice. The cultivation of white mustard (Brassica alba syn. Sinapis alba) is no longer found in India. Rai and Yellow sarson are self-fertile and the rest of the cruciferous oilseeds, viz. Brown sarson, toria, taramira, Banarasi rai and white mustard, are self-incompatible.

The acreage under yellow sarson is scantily (mainly in Bihar) and constantly on the decrease. In the recent past, the acreage under brown mustard is steadily on the increase (over 65%) at the expense of other Brassica due to its higher production, greater to pests and diseases and moisture stress (1).

The crop is distributed both in subtropical and tropical countries. In Asia, it is chiefly grown in China, India and Pakistan. It is also grown in Europe, Canada and the USSR, but the forms of rapeseed and mustard grown there are different from those grown in India. India occupies the first position, both with regard to acreage and production of rapeseed and mustard in the world. In India the Brassica crops occupy the second largest position after groundnut, with 3-5 million hectares, producing about 2 million tonnes of seed annually. In India it is distributed in Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Haryana, Assam, Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka, Rajasthan, West Bengal and Orissa

The rapeseed and mustard thrive best in light to heavy loams. Raya may be grown on all types of soil, but toria does best in loam to heavy loam. Sarson is suited to light-loam soil and taramira is mostly grown on very light soils (1).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Morphologically, these species are alike. They follow the same modalities of development and growth. Both of them pass through a rossete stage, characterized by a stem carrying spread out leaves Rapeseed and mustard include annual herbs ranging in height from 0.45 to 1.75 m. A slender erect much-branched annuals with broad-based, stem-clasping 1eaves, which are some what hairy and glaucous, basal leaves stalked, upto 20 cm long, with very large ovate terminal segment, small pale-yellow flowers, and fruits 2.5-5 cm long. Self-sterile (10). Roots, in general, are long and tapering. Plants produces an inflorescence of yellow, nectar bearing flowers (3).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Inflorescence an axillary, terminal or subscapose raceme; bracts 0, Flowers bisexual, usually regular. Sepals 4, free, imbricate, outer 2 often pouched at base. Petals 4, free, imbricate, clawed, sometimes absent. Stamens 6, free, outer 2 smaller (tetradynamous), rarely fewer, anthers 2-celled. Disc of 4 glands, opposite to sepals. Ovary superior, stipitate, 1 locular or spuriously 2 locular, with few-many parietal ovules, style present or absent, stigma capitate, entire or 2 lobed (11).

Rapseeed and mustard are annual herbs Toria is more or less a surface-feeder and brown sarson has long roots, with a limited lateral spread, enabling its successful cultivation under drier conditions. Yellow sarson has both extensive and lateral spread. The height of the stem varies from 45 cm (in some varieties of toria) to 1.75 m (in yellow sarson). In toria and brown sarson, the branches arise at an angle of 300 to 400.In yellow sarson, branches arise laterally at an angle about 100 to 200 and give the plant a narrow and pyramidal shape. The inflorescence is corymbose raceme. In the case of sarson the four petals are spread apart, whereas in brown sarson and toria the petals overlap or may be placed apart, depending upon the variety. The flowers bear a hypogynous syncarpous ovary. In brown sarson and toria, the ovary is bicarpellary, whereas in the case of yellow sarson, it may also be tri or tetra-carpellary (1).

The fruit is a siliqua. The pods are two-valved, three-valved or four-valved, depending upon the number of carpels in the ovary (2). The flowers begin to open from 8 a.m. and continue up to 12 noon.

Breeding System :
Rapeseed and mustard are capable of both self and cross-pollination. Depending on the species, fertilization is either a self-pollination or cross-pollination brought about by wind or insects mainly bees (4).

Phenology :
In India, they are grown in the Rabi season from September-October to February-March. The rapeseed and mustard crops grow well in areas having 25 to 40 cm of rainfall. Sarson and toria are preferred in low-rainfall areas, whereas raya and toria are grown in medium and high-rainfall areas respectively. Flowering starts around 50 days after sowing.

Pollinators:
Rapeseed has unexceptional entomophilous flowers capable of both self and cross-pollination. In cultivated fields cross-pollination has been reported to the greater extent, but varies depending on the availability of insect pollinators, cultivar and weather. Honeybees are the primary pollinators of rapeseed. Partial sexual compatability exists with some related spp. And other closely related species outside the genus (4).

Seed Biology :
Characteristics of the seed, like size and colour vary according to species, varieties and ripening conditions. Weight of 1,000 seeds comes to 2 g for certain types of brown mustard (3). The colour is generally dark and reddish brown to black. The tegument of the seed is lined by a very thin protein cell albumen layer. The biggest portion of seed (80-88%) is represented by the embryo formed by two double cotyledons and the beginning of the radicle. Oil content of the entire seed varies according to species, varieties, growing and ripening conditions. The seed rate in the case of mixed cropping depends on the proportion of the rapeseed to the main crop. When sown pure, 5 kg of seed per ha is used for all rapeseed and mustard.

Cultivation:

The rapeseed and mustard crops are of the tropical as well as of the temperate zones and require relatively cool temperatures for satisfactory growth. A fine seedbed is required to ensure good germination. In irrigated areas, the first ploughing is done with a medium sized soil-turning plough, followed by two to four ploughing with a desi plough or a cultivator. Sohaga (planking) is given after every ploughing.

In rainfed areas, one to two ploughings with a desi plough or a cultivator, each followed by planking may be given. Toria, in particular, requires a fairly moist seedbed for good germination, but excessive moisture should be avoided (1).

When sown pure, 5 kg of seed per ha is used for all rapeseeds and mustard. When sown mixed with other crops, the time of sowing rapeseed and mustard is governed by the sowing of the main crop. The first half of September is best for sowing toria (if wheat is to follow, it should be sown by the end of August), 25th September to 15th October for sarson, 30thSeptember to 15th October for raya, and taramira is sown throughout October.

The seed from healthy and desirable plants, grown in isolation in the case of self-sterile form should be used. Whenever moisture in the field is inadequate, the seed is mixed with moist soil and kept overnight. For distributing evenly, the seed is usually mixed with sand before sowing.


In mixed cropping rapeseed and mustard are sown in rows 1.8 to 2.4 metres apart across the main crop. The pure crop of rapeseed or mustard is sown at a depth of 4 to 5 cm in lines, 30 cm apart, with a drill, or with a (tube) attached to the plough. Thinning is done three weeks after sowing to maintain a plant-to-plant distance of 10 to 15 cm.

One hoeing in the case of toria in the third week after sowing and 1-2 hoeings in the case of sarson and raya are adequate. Forty kg of N per ha is optimum for all rapeseed and mustard crops in rainfed areas. Under irrigated conditions doses of 40,60 and 80 kg of N per ha are considered optimum for toria, sarson and raya respectively. For taramira, 20 kg of N per ha is sufficient. All the fertilizers should be drilled before sowing. Among the Brassicae, raya is most responsive to irrigation, followed by yellow sarson. Two irrigations, one at flowering and the other at pod formation, result in the maximum yield in the case of toria, sarson and raya. If there is water scarcity one irrigation at flowering is most economical. Taramira is not irrigated (1).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

1. Rapeseed and Mustard Aphid (Lapaphis erysimi)

Symptoms: Both nymphs and adults suck the sap from the leaves, and pods. Plants remain stunted do not produce pods under severe infestation.

Control measures: Spray 0.025% of methyl Demeton or 0.03% Phosphomidon or Dimethoate twice or thrice, depending upon the intensity of attack


2. Leaf-miner Phytomyza spp.

Symptoms: Maggots mine the leaves and eat away the green matter.

Control measures: The same as in the case of mustard aphid.


3. Mustard sawfly (Athalia proxima)

Symptoms: The larvae dig out holes in the leaves, leaving only veins in the

case of severe infestation.

Control measures: Dust 10% BHC @ 25 kg/ha.


4. Painted bug (Bagrada cruciferarum)

Symptoms: Both the adults and nymphs suck the sap from leaves and stems in the early phase of the attack and suck the sap from the pods in the latter stages

Control measures: Dust 10% BHC @ 25 kg/ha (1).


Diseases (18):

1. Alternaria blight (Alternaria brassicae)

Symptoms: Brown circular spots appear on all the parts of the plant, with sooty black concentric rings

Control measures: Spray the crop with Dithane M.45 at 1.5 kg/ha immediately after the appearance of the disease


2. Downy mildew (Peronospora brassicae)

Symptoms: Purplish-brown spots appear on the under-side of the leaf, with a

downy growth of the organism.

Control measures: Spray Zineb or Difolaton @1.5 kg/ha.


3. phyllody (Mycoplama like organisms)

Symptoms: Plants are malformed and become broom shaped. Floral structures are transformed into leafy structures.

Control measures: Avoid early sowing of the crop and control insect vectors with insecticidal sprays.


4. White rust (Albugo candida)

Symptoms: White blisters appear on the lower surface of leaves.

Control measures: Spray Zineb or Copper oxychloride or Difolation at 1.5 kg/ha.

Economic Potential and Uses

Rapeseed and mustard yield the most important edible oil. The oil content of the seeds of different forms ranges from 30 to 48 per cent which is edible and is used for cooking and as a substitute for olive oil. . In the case of white mustard, the oil content ranges from 25 to 33 per cent. The mustard oil is used in the preparation of hair oil, soap and in the manufacture of mineral oil for lubrication. It is also used in bakery, tea industry and in the preparation of vanaspati. Rapeseed oil is used in the manufacture of greases. In tanning industry, mustard oil is used for softening leather. But the use of mustard oil for industrial purposes is rather limited on account of its high cost.

The oilcake is mostly used as cattle feed. The leaves of young plants are used as a green vegetable.

The protein contained in rapeseed and mustard normally ranges between 24 and 30% on the whole-seed basis and between 35 and 40% on the meal basis. But the presence of toxic glucosinolates in the mustard cake is unsuitable as source of human protein and is at present used as manure and as cattle feed.

Uses:

The oil obtained is the main cooking medium in Northern India and can not be replaced by any other edible oil. The seed and oil are used as a condiment in the preparation of pickles and for flavouring curries and vegetables (1). The use of mustard oil for industrial purposes is rather limited on account of its high cost. The oil of rapeseed and mustard possesses a sizable amount of erucic acid (38 to 57%), together with linolenic acid up to 4.7 to 13.0 %. The oleic and linolenic acid, which have nutritive value, together constitute only about 27 per cent (5). It is desirable to increase the quantity of oleic and linolic acid and erucic acid. A lower proportion of erucic acid will make the oil more palatable, nutritive besides reducing metabolic disorders. Young tender leaves of mustard greens are used in salads or mixed with other salad greens. Older leaves with stems may be eaten fresh, canned or frozen, for potherbs, and to a limited extent in salads. Mustard greens are often cooked with ham or salt pork, and may be used in soups and stews (16).

Harvesting Practices

Harvesting is done as soon as the crop begins to turn yellow. Toriaa, which takes 75 to 90 days to mature, is the earliest crop to be harvested. Harvesting is done with hand-sickles. Threshing is done by beating with a wooden stick the seed bearing part of the plants, taken in convenient bundles or by trampling them under the feet of bullocks. Winnowing is done with by slowly dropping the threshed produce from a basket held shoulder-high. The seed after being dried in the sun is stored in gunny bags or bins.

The average yield of rapeseed and mustard is about 500 kg per ha. Under good crop management, toria, which is a short-duration crop, gives a yield of about 450 to 650 kg per ha, sarson 800 to 1,000 kg and rai 1,000 to 1,200 kg per hectare (1).

Processing Techniques

Pretreatment

  • Dry seeds naturally to safer moisture content

  • Cooking of Rapeseed and mustard is important in order to complete the breakdown of oil cells, coagulate the proteins to facilitate oil separation, reduce the affinity of the oil for solid surfaces, insolubolize phosphatides, increase oil fluidity, destroy moulds and bacteria, inactivate enzymes (2).


Extraction

Extraction of oil from the seeds can be done by one of the three processes by screw pressing, by direct solvent extraction or by prepress solvent extraction. Prepress solvent extraction utilizes a combination of two processes and is the most common and probably the most economical processes (7).

Other Uses
Indian Mustard is a folk remedy for arthritis, footache, lumbago, and rheumatism. Seed used for tumors in China. Root used as a galactagogue in Africa. Sun-dried leaf and flower are smoked in Tanganyika to get in touch with the spirits. Ingestion may impart a body odor repellent to mosquitoes. Believed to be aperient and tonic, the volatile oil is used as a counterirritant and stimulant. The plant is used as an antisyphilitic emmenagogue. Leaves applied to the forehead are said to relieve headache. Seeds are used for abscesses, colds, lumbago, rheumatism, and stomach disorders. Chinese eat the leaves in soups for bladder, inflammation or hemorrhage. Reported to be anodyne, apertif, diuretic, emetic, rubefacient, and stimulant, Mustard oil is used for skin eruptions and ulcers (16). In ancient days, yellow mustard seeds were used to fight against the sanke poison (8).

Miscellaneous Information

Glucosinolates and their hydrolysis products have presented a major obstacle to the utilization of rapeseed and mustard meal in animal or human nutrition. They are considered as anti-nutritional factors and have been implicated in several physiological disorders in animals, including goiter and hemorrhagic liver syndrome.

Essentially two approaches have been used to remove Glucosinolates: (1) heat inactivation of the enzyme, myrosinase, so that hydrolysis of Glucosinolates to goitrogenic materials, isothiocyanates, etc., does not occur and water leaching of the material to remove glucosinolates (2) removal of isothiocyanates by steam stripping.

In the first method the whole seeds or the dehulled meat are heat treated to inactivate the enzyme and the glucosinolates are water leached. The number of water leaching depends upon the efficiency of extraction. When the whole seeds are used increased heat consumption, large volumes of water and long extraction involved. The disadvantages of this method are losses of protein and oil, which amount to nearly 30 per cent by weight of the material and the problems of waste effluent treatment.

In the steam stripping method the seeds are ruptured under mild conditions so that the enzymes are not inactivated and the ground seed is subjected to steam distillation to remove the steam volatile isothiocyanates to give a detoxified material. This method does not appear to have been tried on a large scale. In addition to the problems inherent in subjecting large quantities of ground seed to steam distillation the heat treatment may affect the solubility of the protein and also lower the oil quality (15).
