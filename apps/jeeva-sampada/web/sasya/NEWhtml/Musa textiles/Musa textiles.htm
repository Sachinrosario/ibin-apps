HABIT & HABITAT

Abaca is a tropical plant, resembles the true banana, grows best were rainfall as heavy and mean annual temperature around 80oF. It is native to Phillipine Islands. The plant has been reported to thrive well in Andamans.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Abaca, unlike the true banana, has narrower, more tufted leaves and the stalks are usually more slender with narrower, lighter, often spotted and more pointed leaves. The plant consists of a clump of 12 to 30 sheathing leaf stalks 10 to 20 ft. height with a crown of spreading leaf blades 3 to 6 ft. in length. The fruits are quite small and inedible. The fruits are quite small and inedible, having numerous large black seeds. The branched underground rhizome with numerous surface, feeder roots, under favourable conditions throws up from lateral buds a clump of erect pseudostems (10-25 in no.) each attaining a eight of 6.1m or more. Each pseudostem is composed of from 15 to as many as 25 concentrically oriented leaf stalks (petioles), the thickened clasping bases of which form the pseudostem. The tightly rolled leaf blade, after being pushed out of the pseudostem, unfolds into a lamina of 0.9-2.44m long and about 0.3m wide. The true stem, at the end of the vegetative phase, rises through the middle of the pseudostem and finally terminates in a long drooping spike protected closely by overlapping coloured bracts.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

When the terminal bud of the aerial shoot has finished forming its sheaths, it develops into the inflorescence, which is pushed up on a long unbranched stem though the center of the pseudo-stem, and eventually forms a spike, with a great number of clusters or hands of flowers similar to those borne by the banana plant. Each cluster of flowers is covered with a reddish brown to green bract. The female hands, which are fewer in number than the male, are at the basal end of the inflorescence, and open first, while the male hands are at the terminal end. Occasionally, there are a few hermaphrodite flowers between the male and the female ones. The number of hands of flowers which are formed appears to depend on the nutritional conditions available to the plant and although the flowers are potentially bisexual they differentiate into male, female, or hermaphrodite. Ovule anatropous, nuclear type of endosperm development. Seed contains straight embryo and copious endosperm.

Breeding system and varietal improvement:
for crossing plants, the most favourable time is in the early morning. Self-fertilization in flowers is impossible, and Abaca is naturally cross-pollinated, although occasional flowers on the inflorescence may become fertilized by pollen from anther flower on the same plan. Sterility is very high.

Phenology:

 

Pollinators: Abaca flowers are typical 'hat' flowers, and in Indonesia are pollinated by small bats, mostly Macroglossus minimus Dots. These bats are attracted by the smell given off by the bracts of the flowers. The possibilities of flowers being fertilized through transport of pollen b the wind were found to be very remote.(

Floral and seed biology:

Seedling germination : Plants grown from the seeds take about ½ years longer to mature than those grown from suckers or root cuttings. It is usually planted in rows. The seed prices are sown in individual holes dug to a depth of 5 to 10 cm and 2.5 to 3m apart. Food crops such as cowpea, mungbean and others may be interplanted until the abaca becomes mature. Adequate fertilization particularly potash, appears to be useful in maintaining the crop. Although abaca can reach an age of 25 years or more, it is generally replanted after 12-15 years.



MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
There are various insect pests of Abaca, but on the whole their effects are far less serious than the diseases which have already been discussed. Moreover, insect attack can be very often be dealt with by means of insecticides or other methods, which help to keep down the breeding of these pests.
Diseases:

Bunchy top disease is one of the most serious diseases of abaca, if not the most serious. It is a virus disease trasmitted by Pentalonia nigronervosa Coq., the brown aphid, and to a much lesser extent, by P. caladii van der Groot.

Attacked plants develop chlorotic yellowish-white streaks, transparent veins, and parchment like areas on the blades of the youngest furled leaves. The plants become stunted, the stem thickens, and the top of the plant develops bunchy growths rather in the form of a rosette; it is this characteristic of the disease which gives it its name. The only remedy against bunchy top is to destroy all infected plants or even the whole plantation as soon as the disease has been discovered.

Mosaic disease, caused by a virus and transmitted by a number of aphids including the corn aphid, Aphis maidis, is another serious disease of Abaca. Plants which become attacked by the disease do not grow to full size, and the pseudostems are slender and consequently have little or no value from the point of view of production of fiber. The only remedy for mosaic disease is to destroy all infected plants.

Vascular wilt disease of Abaca is caused by a fungus, Fusarium oxysporum Schlecht. var. cubense Wr. & Rkg., which causes the whole plant to become yellowish in colour and eventually with, the whole fibrovascular system being attacked. The only remedy known for this disease is to burn or otherwise destroy any plants which are infected.

The three diseases mentioned above are probably the most serious of those attacking Abaca in the Philippines, but there are a number of others, such as dry sheath rot, stem-rot, and heart-rot, that are all caused by fungi.

In central America, the most serious disease seems to be 'tip-over' disease, the cause of which has not been established, although the banana borer, Cosmopolites sordidus, or nematodes may be one of the main causes, as they attack the corms and roots of the plant.

A nematode, Pratylencus musicola, was found to be a common parasite of Abaca, and where the plants were heavily infested, the lack of roots caused the plant to fall. Owing to the attack on the rots by the nematode, too, the plants were often arrested in their development.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
The individual fibers are 6 to 12 f. in length, lustrous and vary in colour from white to light ocher. They are light, stiff, elastic and exceptionally strong, durable and resistant to both fresh and salt water. For this reason the chief use of abaca is in the manufacture of high grade, cordage, especially marine cables. It is also used for kinder twine, bagging, paper mache, strong tissue paper, wrapping paper and manila paper for sacks. Japan imports a large amount for making the strong paper used for the movable partitions in the houses. fibers is sometimes used for weaving textiles. The individual fibers cannot be spun, but strands of fibers are utilized in making the lustrous cloth known as sinamay. Old manila ropes and waste are useful in the manufacture of tough paper. Also used in making wrappers of tobacco. Low grade fibers are employed to a greater extent in the paper industries for tea bags, mimeograph mats and for other speciality including heavy paper sacks, strong tissue paper etc. fibers from the inner sheaths are woven into fabric or ornaments by the Filipinos.

Parts Used:

Stalks.


Harvesting Practices:
The first abaca crop is ready for harvesting 2 and ½ to 3 years after planting. The entire stalk is cut close to the ground, care being taken not to damage young shoots and developing suckers. The individual leaf sheaths is then removed from the stem of the parent stalk.

Processing Techniques:
In order to obtain the best grade fiber, the stripping of the fibrous tissue should be completed within 48 hrs of cutting, failure to do so results in deterioration of quality. Two steps are involved in the extraction of fiber tunying and the stripping processes. The tunying operation is usually done in the field by inserting a knife between the outer and middle layer of the leaf sheath, thus freeing an end of the outer layer 2.5-7.5 cm wide. This ribbon on 'tuny' is then pulled out from the entire length of the sheath. Each leaf sheath provides 2 to 3 tuxies. Successive sheaths are 'tuxies' in the same manner. These tuxies are then brought to local centers for stripping which is accomplished in 2 ways; by hand stripping and spindle stripping. The former yields the first quality of abaca fiber. Stripping must be done immediately after tunying otherwise the pulp will harden and the fibers can never be properly cleaned. Hand stripping consists of drawing the ribbon between a block of wood and a knife with a serrated edge to remove most of the pulp and extraneous matter. A man can clean about 23 kg/day. fibers must be dried promptly otherwise they lose luster and tensile strength. If cleaning is done in thorough sunlight, the fibers will not discolour. The fibers may also be stripped from the tuxies by a small spindle machine known as the Hagotan. On large, estates, decorticating machines are used. The fiber obtained is 2-3% of the weight of the stalk.(
