REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:   
   Inflorescence:  Terminal and axillary corymbose racemes ; Flower: Bracteate, pedicillate, hermaphrodite ; Calyx: Glabrous leathery, concave, the outer two much smaller than the other three. ; Corolla: Petals with long claws, cripsed on the margin, bright yellow, veined with orange; Androecium: Stamens 10, the three upper reduced to minute staminodes , the remaining seven perfect, the 3 longer with larger anthers than the four lateral ones; Gynoecium: Ovary sessile, at the bottom of the calyx, ovules many, style short stigma many ; Fruit:  Pods 3-5, papery, oblong, thin, flat, transversaly veined pubescent, deeply depressed between the seeds; Seed: 10-20..


Floral and seed biology:
Flowering & Fruiting : Januarary-July. 
Mode of pollination and seed dispersal:

AGRO TECHNOLOGY:

Propagation:  Plant is propagated through seeds.

Soil type: Plant thrives on wide range of well drained soils from loams to heavy clays but is largely grown on dry red loam, sandy loams, including even coarse, gravelly soil with pH ranges between 6-8.5.

Nutrient requirements: Plant responds well to the application of manures and fertilizers especially under irrigated conditions. Where the soil fertility is low to medium the crop generally needs 50 –100 kg nitrogen,20-50 kg phosphate and 20 kg of potassium  oxide during  the period of growth of 135-150 days.

Water management: It is rainfed kharif crop requires little irrigation particularly if rains are irregular during the period of growth

Harvesting period / Method: Leaves are harvested when it is mature depending on the locality and tender leaves start loosing their green colour and appear bluish and thick. Pods are harvested when still green.

Economic Potential and Uses
  • UTILIZATION:
    Uses:
    Chemistry:
    A new leucoanthocyanin – goratensidine (fig. 1a) characterized as 4’,5,7-trihydroxyflavan-3,4-diol. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1960-1969)a.

  • (-) auriculacacidin (fig. 1b) characterized as 5,2’,4’-trihydroxyfalvan-3,4-diol. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1960-1969)b.

  • b-sitosterol and kaempferol isolated from flowers. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979)a.

  • Three new saturated higher fatty ketoalcohols and emodin isolated from leaves. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979)b.

  • Chrysophanol, emodin, rubiadin and b-sitosterol isolated from pod husk. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984)a.

  • Nonacosane and nonacosan-6-one from pod husk. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984)b.

  • Four dimeric procyanidins isolated from stem bark and characterized as fisetinidol (4a-8’’)catechin, fisetinidol(4a-8’’)gallocatechin and fisetinidol(4a-8’’)epigallocatechin. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994)a.

  • Isolation of a new flavone glycoside from roots and its characterization as 7,4’-dihydroxyflavone-5-O-b-D-galactopyranoside. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994)b.

  • 1,5,8-trihydroxy-6-methoxy-2-methylanthraquinone-3-O-b-D-galactopyranosyl(1®4)-O-b-D-mannopyranoside isolated from seeds and characterized. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994)c.

  • A new anthocyanidin glycoside isolated from heartwood and its structure established as pelargonidin-5-O-b-D-galactoside. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994)d.

  • The isolation and spectral data of di-(2-ethyl) hexyl phthalate from Cassia auriculata leaves. (Rao et al.,  2000).

Pharmacology:

  • Diamed is a herbal formulation composed of the aqueous extracts of Cassia auriculata and  other two medicinal plants. Which  showed that Diamed had antihyperglycaemic action in experimental diabetes in rats. (Pari et al., 2001)

  • An aqueous leaf extract of Cassia auriculata (C. auriculata) was found to lower the serum glucose level in normal rats. Glucose uptake and glycogen deposition studies suggest that C. auriculata leaf extract probably has no direct insulin like effect which can enhance the peripheral utilization of glucose. (Sabu and Subburaju, 2002).

  • C. auriculata leaf extract has a lipid-lowering effect in rats with experimentally induced, alcohol-related liver damage. This is associated with a reversal of steatosis in the liver and of spongiosis in the brain. The mechanism of C. auriculata leaf extract lipid-lowering potential is unclear. (Kumar et al., 2002).

  • Cassia auriculata flower extract (CFEt), at doses of 0.15, 0.30 and 0.45 g/kg body weight for 30 days, suppressed the elevated blood glucose and lipid levels in diabetic rats. Cassia auriculata at 0.45 g/kg was found to be comparable to glibenclamide. It suggested that Cassia auriculata flowers possess antihyperlipidaemic effect in addition to antidiabetic activity. (Pari and Latha, 2002).

  • Aqueous extract of Cassia auriculata flower extract possesses an antihyperglycaemic effect and suggest that enhanced gluconeogenesis during diabetes is shifted towards normal and that the extract enhances the utilization of glucose through increased glycolysis. The effect was more prominent than that of glibenclamide. (Latha and Pari, 2003)


Ethnobotany:

  • Medicine for asthma, cough (Seed, Singh and Pandey, 1980); Dysentery (Whole plant, Malhotra and Moorthy, 1973); Opthalmia (Seed, Shah et al, 1981); Rheumatism (Root, Sharma and Malhotra, 1984); Skin diseases (Seed, Baruah and Sarma, 1987); Toothache (Stem, Sharma and Malhotra, 1984); Edible as vegetable (Leaf, Sharma and Malhotra, 1984); Fish poison (Stem bark, Joshi, 1986); Tan (Bark, Sarin, 1989); Toothbrush (Stem, Shah et al, 1981)
