Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
It is a large evergreen tree indigenous to the evergreen forest of the Western Ghats. The tree are common in the forests of lower hills up to an altitude of 750 m extending to the adjoining area of the west coast and the Deccan table lands. It exhibits a strong tendency to form nearly pure patches of varying extent in favorable localities especially near the streams in the evergreen forests. Dhupa trees grow mostly in moist evergreen forests in the Western Ghats and foothills from North Kanara (Karnataka) to Kerala through the Ghat forests of South Kanara, Coorg, Malabar and Coimbatore. It is also occasionally found in deciduous forests, adjoining rivers in the aforementioned areas. Apart from the Ghats, Dhupa trees are also found on the Deccan Plateau and along the Arabian sea-coast, the altitudinal range being from 60 to 1220 metres. Dhupa trees have also been extensively planted as an avenue tree in parts of Karnataka and Kerala states. The tree prefers rich soils having a moderately thick top layers of humus with high moisture content and good drainage. In its natural habitat the climate is humid, absolute maximum shade temperature varies from 35-380 and the minimum from 12.5-180 and the annual rainfall from 200-500 cm (1, 2, 3, 4, 5).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Dhupa is a large handsome, elegant evergreen tree up to 30 m high, with a clean cylindrical bole of 15 metre and a girth of 4.5 metre, bark is rough, whitish to grey, peeling off in thick round flakes. Young shoots and all tender parts except the leaves covered with fine stellate pubescence. Leaves alternate, coriaceous, ovate or oblong, entire, petioled, petioles 1 inch in length, cordate at the base and shortly pointed and or obtuse at the apex. The fruits are fleshy, wingless, about 50 to 60 mm long and contain one large seed. It comprises of a thick pericarp (6mm) with semi-fibrous composition, weighing about 1/5th of the whole fresh fruit (2, 3, 4).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Flowers white, fragrant, in terminal corymbose panicles, bracts ovate, pointed, 40-50 filaments, anthers not auricled at the base, terminating in a single long bristle at the apex, style little longer than the stamens, stigmas acute, capsules ovate, pale brown, fleshy, 8-11 cm long, 3.5-6 cm in diameter, one seeded, seeds reddish white or cream coloured (4, 6).

Phenology :
The maximum leaffall is in the month of March. At the same time copper coloured foiliage begins to appear and attains the maximum proportion during April-May, imparting a very impressive appearance. The flowers appear from January to March and the fruits ripen from May-July (2, 4).

Seed Biology :

The seeds are hard and brittle and have a faint aromatic odour. About 75 fruits weigh to a kilogram. The fresh kernels are reddish white and the average weight of the kernel is 55g, some kernels are cream coloured (4, 7).

Seedling Germination :
The seeds remain viable for about a month and have good germinative capacity (80%) in favourable situations. Germination commences after 25 days and is usually over in 120 days.

Cultivation:

Natural regeneration -In its natural habitat, regeneration is quite satisfactory. The heavier seeds fall close to the tree. They germinate readily and a large number of seedlings spring up around the seed bearers and in other favourable places where seeds can reach by dispersal. The seedlings with stand a considerable amount of shade, but are sensitive to drought; both shade and moisture are necessary for their survival. They are also sensitive to frost. Retention of 70-100 trees in top canopy for shade and removal of all middle and understorey frees it from undue competition and encourages good growth. The tree is susceptible to fire; it wipes out the regeneration and occasionally: mature stands may also be destroyed.

Artificial regeneration -The tree can be propagated by direct sowings or by planting out entire transplants alongwith the ball of earth. Large transplants seem to be particularly advantageous. Stump planting does not give satisfactory results as the coppicing power of the tree is poor. The fruits generally ripen during June-July with b the commencement of monsoon. and start falling to the ground. They have, therefore, to be immediately collected is as they are liable to infestation by weevils. Treatment with rodenticides is preferable: otherwise the fruits are all eaten away by the porcupines. The seedlings are raised in a nursery, located in clear strips of evergreen or semi evergreen forest (4).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

A large number of insects have been reported to infest the tree. The larva of Massicus venustus bores the heartwood of stumps, fire-scorched poles and overmature trees. The beetles and larvae of Sphaerotrypes vateriae bore the bark. The dead sapwood is infested by Dilloderus minutus,Hetero- bostrychus dequalis, and Lyctus africanus, Ptilinus binodulus and cyphagogus corporandi and several others bore the deadwood. Species of Xyleborus damage newly felled or fallen wood. (4)


Diseases:

The wood is susceptible to several fungi. Lentinus praerigidus causes a white spongy heart-rot; Fomes melanoporus causes butt-rot. Polyporus gilvus produces a brownish pocket-rot; Polystictus fiabelliformi and P. xanthopus cause a white spongy rot; and P. tabacinus causes a speckled pocket-rot. The logs and plants when freshly cut are attacked by Ceratostomella and Graphium spp. which live mainly on the reserve foods and not on the cell-walls. Though they do not materially affect the strength of timber, they cause blue stains which reduce the market value of the timber. To prevent such discoloration, it is best to treat the logs and freshly sawn planks with a solution of sodium pentachlrophenate. Treated timber is required to be kept under cover, since the preservative is likely to be washed out by rain (4).

Economic Potential and Uses

Dhupa fat is obtained from seed kernels of Dhupa also known as Indian copal tree (Vateria indica Linn.). Dhupa kernels contains a pale-yellow fat having a tallow-like consistency, which turns white on standing . The fat is known by a number of names, such as Malabar tallow, Piney tallow and Dhupa tallow. In India, the fat derived from Dhupa kernels after refining is suitable for edible uses. It may also be used as a cocoa butter substitute or extender after proper processing. It may also be used in yarn sizing and manufacture of soaps, cosmetics (skin-care products), polishes and candles. It has sizable potential for availability. The fat in the dried seeds generally varies from 22-27%. The tallow is used for edible purposes after refining. It is used in confectionary and as an adulterant of ghee. It is reported to be used as a local application in rheumatism and allied afflictions. The seed cake is used as a manure, and also can be mixed with concentrates such as groundnut cake to make it a feed (4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9).

Uses

Bleached pulp from the wood is used in the manufacture of writing and printing papers. The resin from the tree is used for making incense. The resin can be used in varnish paints, anotomical preparations and it finds extensive use in Indian medicine. It is also used in setting gold ornaments and for caulking boats. Bark is used in the production of arracks and jaggery to control fermentations. The timber is in much demand for plywood making. It has been sold under the name of Malbar white pine. It has been found suitable for commercial plywood of the first class, and is used for making teachests, trunks, opium-cases, ceiling and partions. The plywood can be made fireproof by treating with urea formaldehyde and phenol formaldehydeThe timber can also be used for railway sleepers. Packing cases, coffee and tea boxes, interior fittings and floorings in buildings. It is also used in the manufacture of hardboards (4, 6, 7, 10).

Harvesting Practices
The heavy fruit falls down on the ground and starts germinating within a few days, making their collection and storage a difficult problem. The seed collection and oil extraction has not yet become a commercial practice for Dhupa. Average yield of fruits per tree is 400-500 kg (4, 7).

Produce and Products

Oil is extracted from the seeds, Resin extracted from the tree has industrial importance and finds prominent place in Indian medicine. Essential oil is obtained on distillation of resin. Timber is of much demand in ply wood making and constrructional works. Seed cake is used as manure. Leaves and bark are used in Ayurvedic preparations.

Processing Techniques

The fat in the dried seeds generally varies from 22-27 per cent. In traditional method of extracting the fat, the seeds are crushed and boiled in water till the melted fat rises to the surface. This method practised in Karnataka gives a very low yield of fat. The seeds are cleaned, depulped, dried, decorticated and the kernels solvent-extracted to obtain oil. The kernels yield 20 to 22% of pale yellow fat having a tallow-like consistency, which turns white on standing (3, 4).

Other Uses
It is orally administered to prevent vomiting. The bark is acrid and used as an alexipharmic in the ayurvedic preperations. Resin is credited with tonic, carminative and expectorant properties and is used in the treatment of several diseases such as throat troubles, chronic bronchitis, piles, diarrhoea, rheumatism etc. It forms a good emollient for plasters and ointment bases. The juice of the leaves is applied to cure burns and is also used in the diseases of blood. On distillation oleoresin gives essential oil which showed marked antibacterial activity against gram-positive and gram-negative microorganisms (4, 6, 7, 9).

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

The potential for Dhupa seeds and oil in India is about 10,000 tons and 400 tons respectively. The collection and processing of fruits/seeds for fat extraction poses considerable difficulties due to heavy rains and lack of infrastructural facilities. These are common problems for quite a number of tree bearing oilseeds maturing at the onset of monsoon. Despite above difficulties, small collections are being made and the full potential has to be tapped by removing the impediments (4, 5).
