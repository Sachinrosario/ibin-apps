Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

A small tree, commonly fond in the Andamans on raised sandy beaches extending into the forest behind, where it is confined to sandy or shingly soils. Tree is commonly planted in India.

In its natural range of distribution, the rainfall is about 3000 mm, falling in about 150 days. The mean maximum and mean minimum temperatures are 32 to 350 C and 15 to 170 C (January). Grows on permeable, siliceous sandstone consisting of beach detritus brought by streams and of sand and shingles banked up by winds and waves. It can be grown on sandy soil to arrest beach erosion. It is indifferent to light and able to grow in shade and full overhead sunlight.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

A small tree often with buttressed trunk. Branches in horizontal whorls, obovate leaves. 18-24 m tall, having a bole of 12 m, with a smooth grey bark and whorled branches. leaves large, obovate, usually crowded at the ends of the branchlets.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Flowers white, star-shaped, in slender racemes. Drupes yellowish or reddish, ellipsoidal, distinctly compressed, with a porous, fibrous to fleshy pericarp and a hard endocarp, enclosing the edible seed.

Phenology:

Flowers during February - April. Fruiting is during May - July.

Floral and seed biology:

Seed Germination & Propagation:On loose soils, the fruits germinate and the seedlings establish readily. On the mainland, natural reproduction is unknown as the tree is grown only in gardens and the fruits, being edible, are collected, giving little chance for natural regeneration.

Freshly collected seeds are sown in the nursery in about July, and the seedlings are transplanted in the south-west monsoonal rains of the following year.Nursery beds are sown with ripe seeds which give a large percentage of seedlings suitable for transplanting.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Fuel:Wood used as fuel.

Wood: Wood is moderately heavy (sp. gr. 0.59), hard and attractive. Sapwood is greyish in young trees, often blotched with yellow due to soluble dye. The heartwood is scarcely distinguishable from the sapwood in old trees. The former is light brick-red to brownish red, often with irregular darker and lighter belts. The wood is lustrous, rather smooth, shallowly interlocked-grained and often curly- or twisted- grained, medium coarse-textured, light to moderately hard.

It is suitable for mill work, furniture, cabinet work and useful for boat building, bridge timbers, cross-ties, flooring, boxes and crates. It is used in house building, wheel wrights work and in general carpentry. Red timber is of good quality.

Other uses:

Fruits - The fruit contains seeds which provide edible oil. The seeds are sweet, almond like and widely eaten raw or cooked.

Bark - The bark contains a small amount of colouring matter, soluble in water, which by various processes produces light brownish yellow, light drab, gloden-fawn and slate colours in silk, light drab, olive and grey in cotton and pale-fawn in wool.

Bark, leaves and roots contain tannin.

Leaves - The foliage is suitable for feeding tasar or katkura silkworms. Leaves found to contain moderate amount of colouring matter which produces various shades of brownishyellow on silk and wool. Leaves are used as feed for silkworms.

Leaves, bark medicinally useful.
Root & bark - Root and bark are used for tanning.
Seeds - Seeds are edible like almond with similar oil.
Fruit - Fruits are used as a source of dye.
