HABIT & HABITAT

Sapindus laurifolius is a medium-sized to large, deciduous tree, up to 18 m. in height and 1.5 m. in girth, common in the peninsular, India; also cultivated for ornament purpose and in avenues. The tree is a native of South India where it is common on the coast and in the open forests at low elevations. It is a good avenue tree and is occasionally cultivated around villages in West Bengal, Bihar, Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh. The tree thrives well in almost any kind of soil, but loamy, clayey, and black-cotton soils are more favourable. In its natural habitat, the absolute maximum shade temperature varies from 40° to 47.5°. The average rainfall varies from 50 cm. to 200 cm. or even more. (1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8)

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
The bark is grey, shining, covered with rough, deciduous scales; leaves abruptly pinnate, 12-30 cm. long; leaflets subopposite, 2-3 pairs, terminal pair the largest, lanceolate, elliptic or oblong, acuminate or emarginate (var. emarginatus), 12-20 x 3-8 cm., entire, glabrous above, more or less pubescent beneath, base acute; main nerves about 8-12 pairs; petioles 3 mm. long, pubescent. (1, 2, 4, 5, 6)

The flowers are white, polygamous; males numerous, a few bisexual flowers, both found in the same terminal, rusty-pubescent panicles; Sepals 5, rotund-ovate, ciliolate, fulvous-pubescent outside, glabrous within, 4 mm. long. Petals 4-5, shortly clawed, narrower. than the sepals, lanceolate, villous outside and more or less so within, usually furnished with 2 villous scales attached at each side of the petalabout half way up. Disk concave with a fleshy hirsute margin. Stamens 8; filaments villous; anthers oblong, apiculate. Ovary densely hairy. Fruit is a drupe, fleshy, covered with fulvous hairs when young, 1.3-2.0 cm. diam., 2-3 drupels partially united, wrinkled rind; glabrous and wrinkled when ripe, with 1 seed in each lobe. (1, 2, 4, 5, 6). Seeds pea-sized, enclosed in blackish, smooth, hard endocarp. (1, 2, 4, 5)

Seedling Germination :

The germinative capacity is c. 67 per cent and the survival per cent is very variable. (1)

Cultivation:

Regeneration -The tree can be raised by direct sowing of the seeds, with or without pericarp. Ripe fruits are collected during February-April. Seeds retain viability, probably, for a year or two. Untreated seeds" give higher germination than those treated with cold, hot or boiling water. In Andhra pradesh and Mysore, reproduction is also obtained by suckers and offsets. The seedlings, offsets, or root-suckers are planted at a spacing of 10 - 12 m. The tree grows well in the plains of North India where the frost is not severe. Seedlings attain a height of about 40 cm. in the first growing season and about 1 m. in the next. (1)

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests: (1, 15)

The most destructive pest of S. laurifolius was the blossom webber (Cydia sp.), which damaged flowers, nuts and twigs. Other important pests were the tree bug (Tessaratoma javanica), the fruit feeders Virachola [Deudorix] isocrates and Virachola sp., the soap-nut moth (Serrodes inara [S. partita]), weevils (Peltotrachelus pubes, Amblyrhinus poricollis and Myllocerus discolor), flower webbers (Dichocracia [Conogethes] punctiferalis, Acrobasis sp. and Lectithocera [Lecithocera] sp.) and the bark borer Indarbela tetraonis. The other species of insect pests that have been identified are, Florinia sapindi - Green (sap-feeder), Deudorix epijarbas Moore , Rapala varuna . The timber is also susceptible to the attack of insects.


Diseases: (1)

Two diseases, have been identified on Sapindus laurifolius.

  • Leaf spot - Phyllosticta Pers. .- ex Desm.

  • Blight – Colletotrichum gloeosporioides Penz (C. sapindi da Camera). A leaf-infecting fungus, has also been recorded.

Economic Potential and Uses

The oil from the seeds has been studied and reported that it is a rich source of a n-eicosanic acid. The oil could be used as a source of oleic and arachidic acids or as a 'hard' oil in soap making and also used as hair Oil. (1, 5, 10, 12)

Parts used (1, 2, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9)

Seeds, fruit, trunk, bark, root and leaves.


Uses (1, 2, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9)

Seed: The hard, black shell of the seed yields a fair amount of a dye, suitable for dyeing light shades on mordanted cotton, wool and silk. The dye is also reported to be suitable for dyeing leather, in colouring shoe-creams and polishes, and for staining wood.

Fruit: It is used in hair care as it cleanses hair and gives shine and softness. It prevents greying of hair and hair fall. It can also be used as detergent.

Trunk: Wood is bright yellow, with streaks of lighter and darker colour. It is fairly straight- to moderately wavy- grained, medium even-textured, hard and heavy to very heavy (sp. gr., 0.77-1.06; wt., 897-1,025 kg.fcu.m.) and tough. The wood is not durable, and splits and cracks on exposure. It is not used much except occasionally for carts, and for making small articles, such as combs, boxes and axe and similar tool handles. The wood is decorative and is suitable for cabinet-work and turnery. Being hard and heavy, the treated timber may be suitable for construction.

Leaves: It can be used as manure.

Root and bark have medicinal uses.

Harvesting Practices (1)

Ripe fruits are collected during February-April. These fruits are collected from the trees, or from the ground after they have fallen. These fruits are sun-dried, after which a gentle cracking of the pericarp releases the seeds.

Processing Techniques

The processing of oilseeds is carried out in bullock-driven wooden ghanis (kohlus) and powder-driven ghanis (rotary mills). The ghani is a familiar feature of the rural landscape contributing to village economy. The ghani (kohlu) is devised on a pestle and mortar rotating system driven by a pair of bullocks. In semi-urban areas, where electricity is available, most of the bullock-driven ghanis have been replaced by motorized rotaries for crushing oilseeds. (13, 14)

Other Uses (1, 2, 6, 7, 8, 9)

Fruits: Fruits have actions or uses as Tonic, expectorant, emetic purgative and anthelmintic properties and are also used in the treatment of asthma, colic due to indigestion, diarrhoea; cholera, tubercular glands, paralysis of the limbs and lumbago. A thick, watery solution of the pulpy mesocarp is introduced into the nose of the patient for relief in hemicrania and for restoring consciousness during epileptic and hysteric fits. Ethereal extract of the pericarp shows activity against Staphylococcus aureus Rosenbach and Escherichia coli (Migula) Castellani & Chalmers. It is also used extensively in the preparation of certain fungicides and insecticides. The saponins A and C and sapindosids A and B extracted from the rind of the fruit shows antifungal activity.

Roots and bark are employed as a mild expectorant and demulcent, a decoction of the bark is given to cattle suffering from worm-infested ulcers after calving. Root bark is saponaceous and is used in the same way as the fruits. Fruit as well as root bark are used as fish-poison. The honey from the flowers is reported to be poisonous to bees.

Seed: Pessaries made of the kernels are used to stimulate the uterus during childbirth.
