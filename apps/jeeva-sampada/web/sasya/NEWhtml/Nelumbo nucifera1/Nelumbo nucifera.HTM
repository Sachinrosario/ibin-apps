HABIT & HABITAT
A handsome aquatic herb with stout, creeping rhizome found throughout India, ascending up to800 m. A very small genus of aquatic herbs distributed in Asia, Australia and America. One species occurs in India. N. nucifera is a native of China, Japan and possibly India. It is commonly found growing in ponds, tanks and jheels; it is often cultivated for its elegant sweecscented flowers. Many cultivated races with flowers ranging in colour from white to deep rose and variegated leaves are known in Japan. Lotus .is culti- vated in China and Japan, in terraced fields, for its edible rhizomes and seeds. DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
 Leaves peltate, 60-90 cm. or more in diameter. orbicular, glaucous: petioles very long, smooth or with small prickles ; flowers solitary, large, white or rosy, fruit-torus large, top-shaped, 5-10 cm. Diameter, spongy, with many (10-30) uniovulate carpels sunk separately in cavities on the upper side, carpels maturing into ovoid nut-like achenes.
Two types of rhizomes, white and red, are generally met with. The farinaceous rhizomes of the plan. are collected and sold as vegetable. They measure 60-120 cm. in length am 6-9 cm. in diameter are white to buff-orange in colour and show in cross section a few large cavity and surrounded by several small ones. They are fleshy and when freshly cut, exude a mucilaginous juice ; they are somewhat fibrous and do not soften even after prolonged boiling.

Phenology :
The plant bear flowers in profusion during the hot and rainy season and seeds ripen towards the end of the rains. The rhizomes are ready for digging in October.

Seed Biology :

Seed Germination:
Propagation is usually by rhizomes; the plant may also be propagated from seeds. It may be grown in tubs, hut pond culture preferred as it permits the radial spread of rhizome up to 15 m. a year. The viability of lotus seed, exceeds that of any known species of flowering plan In Punjab, a small area of 60 hectare is reported be under this crop. Rhizomes, cut into small piece, are planted with eyes above the soil surface in March April, care being taken that enough water remain in the pond till October. When grown from seed 10-12 kg. of seeds are required to give enough nursery seedlings for a hectare.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Fresh and dried Rhizomes, Seeds, Young leaves, petioles and flowers.

Uses
  1. Fresh rhizomes are eaten after roasting, while dried slices are used in curry or fried as chips ; they are also pickled. They may be preserved in the frozen condition and used as an ingredient in precooked foods.
  2. Young leaves, petioles and flowers of the plant are eaten as vegetables. A kind of arrowroot is prepared from the fleshy rhizomes ; it is aromatic and sweet, and is reported to be not only nutritious but also tonic ; it is given to children in cases of diarrhoea, dysentery and dyspepsia.

Harvesting Practices
Rhizomes are ready for digging in October. The crop is reported to yield 3,600-4,600 kg. of rhizomes per hectare.

Other Uses
  1. A paste of the rhizome is applied in ringworm and other cutaneous affections Carpels are demulcent and nutritive and are used to check vomiting. A sherbet prepared from the plant is used as a refrigerant in smallpox and is reported to stop eruption.
  2. The milky viscid juice of leaf and flower stalks is used in diarrhoea. Saline extracts of stem, leaves and flowers possess bacteriostatic action against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria . Nematodes - see Parasitic Worms
  3. In the U.S., this plant is grown almost entirely as an ornamental. The starchy rhizomes are of some importance as foods in the Orient. The acorn-like seeds are also an oriental delicacy.
  4. The flowers of Nelumbo nucifera are used for ornament and as offering in temples. Cut flowers stand trans- portation if picked as buds one or two days before opening. They were once used as the source of a perfume, Lotus Perfume, which was highly prized ; the present-day lotus perfume is a blend of patchouli, benzoin and storax with phenylethyl and cinnamic alcohols. The honey from bees which visit lotus flowers is reported to possess tonic properties and considered useful for affections of the eye. The leaf stalk yields a yellowish white fibre.
