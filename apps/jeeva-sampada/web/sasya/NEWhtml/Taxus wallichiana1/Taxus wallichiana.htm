Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT :
Small to medium-sized evergreen tree, with red “berries” (seeds covered by arils). Individual trees are either male or female.  Asia from Afghanistan through the Himalayas to the Philippines.  Temperate moist forests, 1,500-3,500 m Afghanistan, Bhutan, China (Xizang), India (Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Himachal Pradesh, Jammu-Kashmir, Manipur, Sikkim, Uttar Pradesh), Nepal, Pakistan. The species occurs in temperate moist forest between 1500m and 3500m. Native of Nepal.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:
 Small to medium-sized evergreen tree, with red “berries” (seeds covered by arils). Individual trees are either male or female.  A very large fast growing tree. Must have very free draining dry soil.  Loves heavy shade. Rare in cultivation. 1.8 metres high x 1 metre wide in 10 years.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Parts Used:
Leaves and bark.

 

Uses:
The aril (but not the poisonous seed) is eaten. The foliage and seeds are toxic. For several centuries the young shoots, leaves and bark have been used for their medicinal properties. More recently huge quantities of leaves have been collected for medicinal extracts, which have anti-cancer properties. The timber is also sought-after and traded at domestic level.


Harvesting Practices:
Clippings are harvested mechanically from the nurseries or from the grown up trees.

Produce and Products:
 branded drugs, OTC products, food supplements, tonics and cosmetics


Chemistry:
It contain Taxol: a promising drug for treatment of ovarian and breast cancers. and Taxanes.

Processing Techniques:
Taxol or Paclitaxol is extracted from the bark, roots and foliage.
Other uses:

  • Young branches are used as fodder in Pakistan. 

  • Taxus wallichiana has other medicinal uses, both in Ayurveda and Tibetan Medicine. In the latter, it is used to treat fever and relieve muscular pain. The timber is excellent and resistant to decay. It is used to cover graves in Pakistan.

  • Used as an ornamental plant.

Economic potential to the country

Export potential:
 Numerous companies in India are involved in the processing or export of the species for medicinal use. Since 1992 substantial quantities of leaves and roots have been exported. For instance 495.137mtonnes were exported from Madras/Cochin and 53.75mtonnes through Delhi. 170,710kg of dried leaves were export from Arunachal Pradesh in one month (Molur & Walker, 1998). It is estimated that 5,500 tonnes of leaves were exported from India in 1994, but since 1996 exports from that country are prohibited (Schippmann 2001). The yield of leaves from a single tree varies between 15  and 25 kg (fresh weight).
