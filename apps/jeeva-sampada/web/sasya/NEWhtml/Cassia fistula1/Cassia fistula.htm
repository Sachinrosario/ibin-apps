Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:                                                                               
Deciduous medium sized tree, up to 24 m in height and 1.8 m in girth, with a straight bole up to 15 m, found both wild and cultivated almost through out India. The tree is one of the most widespread in the forests of India, usually occurring in deciduous forests throughout the greater part of India, ascending up to an altitude of 1220 m in the sub Himalayan tract and outer Himalayas. It is common throughout the Gangetic valley, particularly abundant in the bhabar tracts, Central India and South India. Tree is not gregarious, but is scattered in mixed, deciduous forests, often of a some what open type; it occurs frequently in sal forests. It is found in association with Santalam album Linn. in South India.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES                                                                        
Bark grayish, smooth, exfoliating in small, woody scales up to 1.5 cm thick; leaves 20-40 cm, rachis and petioles glandless, leaflets 4-8 pairs, distinctly stalked, 5-15 cm long, oblong or ovate, clothed with young, caducous, silvery pubescence; flowers bright yellow, in axillary, pendulous, lax racemes; pods cylindrical, pendulous, smooth, hard, dark brown or black, up to 60 cm x 2.5-3.0 cm; seeds light brown, hard, smooth, shiny, 0.7-1.0 cm x 0.5-0.7 cm, biconcave, 40-100, embedded in sweetish pulp.

Phenology :
The tree is rarely ever wholly leafless, but in some localities it is almost bare between March and May and new leaves appear during April-July. The flowers appear along with the leaves; in dry areas, the flowers however, appear till October. The pods develop very rapidly by October, when they are soft and green. They commence ripening in December and continue till April, when they begin to drop.

Cultivation:
Climate and Soil: It grows under a very wide range of climatic and edaphic variations. It is found on a variety of geological formations and can grow on poor, shallow soil, as on the dry outer slopes of Himalayaa. It appears to thrive equally well on trap, granite and sand stone soils. It is also frequent on khurda laterite. In its natural habitat, the absolute maximum shade temperature varies from 35.0 to 47.5 and the absolute minimum from 0 to 17.5; the annual rainfall from 450 to 3000 mm or even more, but it flourishes well when the rainfall is between 750 and 1900 mm. It is a light demander but withstands moderate shade. It is not frost hardy, but drought hardy.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:
In Assam, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu states of India, the early stage, the species is seriously defoliated by Catopsilia crocale Cramer, C. pyranthe Linn. and Margaronia conclusalis Walker (Syn. Glyphodes conclusalis) in the early stages resulting in the death of young plants or malformation of leading shoots. C. pomona and (Fabr.) and Nephopteryx rhodobasalis feed on this species.
The larvae of some insects bore young pods.
Xyleutes persona, the beehole borer and Zeuzera coffeae, the red borer, attack the young woody stemsand such trees should be destroyed.
Indarbela quadrinotata (Walker), is a bark eating caterpillar.
The caterpillar of Trachylepidia fructicassiella Rag. bores the fallen pods. The grub of Pachymerus (Caryoborus) gonagra Fabr., the Tamaind bruchid beetle, bores the pods and feeds on cotyledons and lowers the quality of pulp.
Diseases:
Several fungi attck the species, but the most important one Trametes incerta (Currey) Cke, causing the borwn spongy heart root.
The leaf spot is caused by Stenella cassiae abbasi & Shukla.
The angiospermic parasite, Dendrophthoe falcata (Linn. f.) Ettingsh. is also found to attack the plant.

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Seeds, Leaves, Pods, Root, Bark, flowers and Wood.

Uses

·        Leaves are eaten.

·        The crushed seeds and leaves are employed for making a sherbet.

·        The flowers are also eaten.

·        Though reported not to be grazed by cattle and goats, they are used as fodderfodder for livestocks.

Harvesting Practices
The pods commence ripening in December and continue till April, when they begin to drop. The ripe pods are 30-60 cm long or more, and 2.5 cm in diameter. They are collected during March-April; seeds are separated from the soft pulp, washed, and dried and stored in a cool, dry place in gunny bags.

Other Uses 

1.They are also a source of green manure.

2.The timber has been recommended as a suitable substitute for teak, Tectona grandis Linn. f., and sal, Shorea robusta Gaertn. f.; It is considered more durable than sal. It is largely used for houseposts, for fine cabinet-work, bridgeposts, electric poles, agricultural implements, yokes, stocks, wheels, shafts of carts, tentpegs, railway keys, scantling, bowls, paddy pounders, mortars and sugarcane abd oil press. It makes excellent tool handles such as pick-axe and axe handles, mallet heads and similar articles requiring strength and toughness. The timber is suitable for high class furniture and camp-furniture, inlay and turnery work and boat building. It is good for soft paper making. It is an excellent fuel and yields first class charcoal.

3.The bark is used in admixture with avaram bark in the tanning of East-India kips. Fishing nets are tanned and dyed with the extract of the bark, for preservation.

4.Medicinal uses:Th bark posesses tonic and anti dysenteric properties. It is also used for skin complaints. The wood is given in dysentery; the ash is reported to be employed as costic to open abscesses. The powder or decoction of the bark is administered in leprocy, jaundice, syphyilis and heart diseases. The stem bark is reported to be eaten raw for stomachache. The extract of stem bark showed high interferon-like anti-virl activity agaist Ranikhet Disease Virus and Vaccinia Virus (VV). In Papua New Guinea, bark-scrapings and leafsap are employed to heal broken bones and topical ulcers. A decoction of stem bark along with cumin seeds and garlic is given as a purgative to cattle.

5.The pods are official for their laxative properties. The drug consists of the dried pod, known as CASSIA-FRUIT, or CASSIA-POD, and its pulp, CASSIA-PULP. The ethanolic extract of the pods showed antifertility activity in female albino rats, which is due probably to its estrogenic nature. The fruits are reported to be used for asthma in Andhra Pradesh.
The pulp is a safe purgative and is recommended for children and pregnant women. It is given in disorders of liver and in biliousness, and acts as a tonic; it is also spplied in gout and rheumatism. The drug may safely be used as an analgesic. As an antipyretic, it is a renedy for malaria and black water fever. It is also used in blood poisoning, anthrax and dysentery and given in leprosy and diabetes and for the removal of abdominal obstructions. A decoction of it is given in hoarseness. An aqueous extract of the pulp exhibited slightly lower antibacterial activity than its dealcoholized extract against Micrococcus pyogenes var. aureus, M. pyogenes var. albus Hucker, etc. Fruit pulp and stem bark are used in Ayurvedic preparations for th treatment of blood impurities. The decoction of the pods is given in pneumonia and common fever. The pulp, mixed with rapeseed oil, is orally given to cattle suffering from cough and as a stomachic.

6.The seeds are slightly sweet and posess laxative, carminative, cooling and antipyretic properties; they are given in constipation. They are useful in jaundice, biliousness, skin disease and in swollen throat. The whole seed powder cures intestinal amoebiasis. A seed diet produced marked hypoglycaemic activity in normal albino rats but not in alloxan diabetic albino rats. The aqueous extract of the seeds inhibits Salmonella typhi and Corynebacterium diphtheriae. Powdered seeds are administered in Raktapradara by the Bhil tribals in Southern Rajasthan (India). The seeds yield a dark coloured gum slightly soluble in water and hence have a possibility as a source of industrial gum.

7.The leaves posesses antiperiodic and laxative properties. The leaves and bark mixed with oil, are applied to pustules, insect bitesand to lesses inflamation in facial paralysis. Internally, the leaf-juice is also given for paralysis and brain affection, and to cure ringworm; also used to allay irritation. A poultice is used to treat childrens.

8.The root and its bark posesses astringent, purgative, febrifungal and anti-bilious properties. The aqueous extract of the root bark exhibits antiinflamatory activity. The root is useful in cardiac disorders, biliousness, rheumatic condition, haemorrhages, wounds, ulcers and boils, tubercular glands and various skin diseases; also reported to be beneficial in leprosy. The alcoholic extract of the rootbark can be used for the treatment of black-waster fever. A paste of the roots, pepper, leaf roots of Asparagus heterophylus Lam. and red mud is applied to the swellings of throat in cattle. The root bark also contains tannins, phlobaphenes, reducing sugars and oxyanthraquinones.

9.The flowers are eaten. They posesses astringent, purgative, febrifungal and anti-bilious properties. A decoction of the flowers is given in stomach troubles. The pollengrains are starchyand lipid type, and the former type have been reported to cause allergy.
