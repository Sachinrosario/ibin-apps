HABIT & HABITAT

Deciduous tree Throughout India and Burma except in the moistest and driest tracts. Frequently abundant in grassland, open scrub jungle and village grazing ground.
Availability :

Throughout India except Jammu & Kashmir and Arunachal Pradesh.
Conservation/ Threat Status : Common

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

A small or medium-sized deciduous tree with crooked stem and large irregular branches. Bark fibrous, light brown or grey. Leaves consisting of 3 large leathery leaflets on a common stalk 4-9 inches long, conspicuously swollen at its base. Flowers 1½-2 inches long, clustered along the branches, bright orange-red. Calyx ½ inch long, dark brown-velvety as are also the flower stalks. Pods 4-8 inches long, 1-2 inches broad, the top ends where the solitary seed is situated and edges thickened, the rest thin, strongly nerved, grey-silky, pale yellowish-grey when ripe.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:


Breeding system and varietal improvement:
Propagation by seed, which should be removed from the pods and swon as soon as ripe as it, does not keep well. Seedlings and also plants transplanted are apt to die back to the ground either in the winter or in the hot weather. This process may be continued for 2 or 3 years till a shoot strong enough to stand the unfavorable period of the year is produced.

Phenology: Leafless or nearly so when in flower from February to April. The flowers usually cover the upper portion of the tree and make it a most conspicuous and gorgeous object at the beginning of the hot weather. The pods develop very rapidly and being green when young look like foliage at a little distance. The ripe pods are very light and are scattered far and wide by the strong wind of the hot weather.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
ORNAMETAL FLOWERING TREE
A moderate sized deciduous forest tree of India, unattractive when not in bloom, on account of its crooked and distorted stem. Leaves are pinnately trifoliate and used during religious functions and are stitched together to form plates to eat from, which are used all over India. The tree is gorgeous in bloom, in February-March, when it is leafless, bearing in great profusion vivid orange-crimson flowers in large showy dense racemes; hence it is planted in large gardens near the valuable; the lac insect thrives on its branches; the flowers produce a temporary dye; the tree provides the Bengal kino gum, which is largely used in tanning operations and in medicine

Harvesting Practices:


Produce and Products:
The flowers yield a brilliant but very fugitive yellow colouring matter. For extracting the colour, the flowers are digested for six hours with boiling water and the extract is treated with a little sulphuric acid (50ºC for 100 gm of flowers). These are again heated for one hour. A light, viscous precipitate, devoid of dyeing property separates out and is removed while hot. The filtrate is kept overnight and the clear liquid is decanted from a small quantity of tarry substance and gently evaporated for 3 hours in the water bath. A further quantity of black, viscous product is deposited. When this has been removed, the filtrate, after some days, deposit crystals. For purification, the product is repeatedly agitated with water until the washings are no longer dark in colour. The ethereal liquid is evaporated and the residue is dissolved in hot alcohol. The solution is treated with very little boiling water, with on cooling form pure crystals. Addition of alum, lime or an alkali deepens the colour to orange and also makes it less fugitive. A water paint or pigment is also obtained from an infusion of the flowers. The dye is extracted by simply pressing the flowers when fresh or boiling them dries in a weak solution of limewater. The decoction is used to dye cotton fabrics, sola articles and woolen carpets. On wool the yellow dye is said to be not permanent than that obtained from turmeric. It is also used with turmeric to dilute the greenish tinge of the latter and to give it greater permanence.
Soaking the flowers in water overnight produces red colour, with which filtered liquid 100 ml of burnt engine oil is added and mixed properly. The locals spray this solution during festivals and it gives permanent red colour spots on the clothes.
For obtaining deep red colour, flowers are boiled in a pot of water by adding a little of sesamum/ til oil till the red colour is imparted. To this white cloth of desirable nature is soaked for about 1 hour and dried. This colour is not decolourised for years to come. Sadhus generally practice this for their robes and by local chenchu tribals for their cloth dyeing.


Chemistry:
The colouring component of dye is butin, which is chemically 7, 3, 4-triyhydroxy flavonone found as colour needles of m. p., 225-226 ºC. It is found as the glycoside butrin in the flowers. Butin is converted into the chalcones on boiling with alkali during dyeing (Gulrajani et al. 1992). A yield of 2 % of butein is obtained by extracting the flowers with boiling water and hydrolysing the glycosides.

Other uses:
From wounds in the bark a gum known as Bengal kino exudes as round treas as large as pea and of an intense ruby colour. It is very astringent and is medicinally. The flowers yield a bright yellow dye of little permanency. The seeds yield oil used as an anthelmintic. Lac is grown on the dhak, which is one of the principal hosts of the lac insect. The bark yields a coarse fiber used for cordage. The leaves are used as plates and for covering umbrellas. The timber is of little value and is not durable except under water. It is a very poor fuel.
