Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Bushy, glabrous, dioecious herb growing to 1.8m by 1m, occurring in the subtropical Himalayas from Kashmir to Nepal, at elevations of 1000-6000 ft.
The plant prefers light (sandy), medium (loamy) and heavy (clay) soils and requires well-drained soil. The plant prefers acid, neutral and basic (alkaline) soils. It cannot grow in the shade. It requires moist soil.

Availability :  
A tall erect, herb, resembling hemp, hence the specific name.  It is met with in the temperate and sub-tropical Western Himalaya from Kashmir to Nepal, at altitudes from 1000 to 6000ft, but is by no means a plentiful plant.  


Status : 

Cultivated Beds.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

Perennial growing to 1.8m by 1m.  It is a glabrous herb or shrub.  Elegant large pendant axillary racemes of Flowers and are dioecious, greenish-yellow like an overgrown perennial amaranthus, green fruit-capsule with numerous minute seeds.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Breeding System :

The flowers are dioecious (individual flowers are either male or female, but only one sex is to be found on any one plant so both male and female plants must be grown if seed is required). The plant is not self-fertile.

Phenology :
It is in flower in September, and the seeds ripen in October

Seed Biology :

 

Seedling Germination :
Seed - sow in spring in a greenhouse, only just covering the seed. Germination usually takes place within 1 - 3 weeks at 18°c. When large enough to handle, prick the seedlings out into individual pots and plant them out in the summer.

Clump planting: Division is made in the spring. Very easy, larger clumps can be replanted direct into their permanent positions, though it is best to pot up smaller clumps and grow them on in a cold frame until they are rooting well. Plant them out in the spring.


Cultivation:
Requires a good deep soil and a sunny position. Plants can be grown in quite coarse grass, which can be cut annually in the autumn.

This species is not hardy in the colder areas of the country, it tolerates temperatures down to between -5 and -10°c.  Give the roots protection from winter frosts by mulching them.

At one time this plant was commonly cultivated as a dye plant, but with the advent of chemical dyes it has fallen into disuse.

Dioecious nature: Male and female plants must be grown if seed is required.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts used
Roots, leaves and twigs

Uses
Parts used for dyeing are yellowish wood, bark and root.

  • This dye is used for colouring handkerchiefs, turbans and towels.  It is also used for dyeing wool.
  • Roots known as Akalbir is used in Kashmir as special dye used  to dye silk a delicate yellow colour.    
  • Throughout the Himalaya it is more or less employed, being combined, it is said, with red colours to soften the tint and with indigo to produce a favourite green(pista).  It is used as a yellow dyestuff, chiefly on alum-mordanted silk. 
  • It is also used for dying wood and cotton. Colouring matter also present in the leaves and twigs .
  • In some places where it grows, the yellow root is used to aid in dyeing red.
  • It is stated in some reports that the dye-stuff is exported from Himalayas to the plains to be used both as a dye and medicine.

Harvesting Practices
In August the roots are dug up (the bark peeled off), dried in sun and then packed for market.

Chemistry:

The dye contains a glucoside, datiscin, which crystallizes in colourless needles or laminae.  It forms with alkalis a deep yellow solution; it dyes fabrics both mordanted and unmordanted.  It forms yellow lakes with lead salts.

The leaves and roots contain a glucoside which may be obtained by exhausting them with alcohol evaporating to a syrup and precipitating the resin with water; from the decanted liquid crystals may be obtained which should be redissolved in alcohol and the remaining traces of resin removed by re-precipitation with water.  Dastiscin may then be obtained in colourless silky needles or scales, little soluble in cold water, and only sparingly so in warm water and ether.  The crystals are neutral and have a bitter taste; they melt at 180° C.

Other Uses 

Medicinally, it acts as a sedative in rheumatism.  As a bitter and purgative, it is used sometimes in fevers and in gastric and scrofulous complaints.  It is administered in intermittent fevers also.  The bruised root is applied to the head as a sedative.  Used as an expectorant in cattarrh.  The bark also contains a bitter principle like quassia.
