Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
B. monosperma is a medium to moderate sized deciduous tree found in greater parts of India, Burma, ascending outer Himalayas up to an elevation of about 800 m and in the hills of South India up to an elevation of about 1,500 m. It is rarely found growing in the arid regions of the country. It is a characteristic tree of some edaphic climax types in dry deciduous forests, namely, Butea forest and babul savannah. Extension of agriculture in Indo-Gangetic plains and in central India has pushed it to such pockets as are not fit for cultivation. As a result, it is found growing as scattered trees or in small pockets in the plains and on hill slopes where it has been able to withstand heavy pressure of grazing and lopping.

In its natural habitat, the absolute maximum shade temperature varies from about 38° to 49°C, the absolute minimum temperature from about -4° to 15°C and the annual rainfall from about 600 to 4,500 mm. It is capable of growing in water logged situations, black cotton soils, saline, alkaline, swampy badly drained soils and on barren lands except in arid regions (7, 8, 9, 10, 11).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
It is a tropical and subtropical tree up to 15 m in height and 1.6-2.0 m (sometimes upto 3.8 m). Full grown trees are moderate in size with a crooked irregular trunk and a light open crown. The bark is moderately thick, grey or light brown in colour and exudes red juice when injured. The shoots are clothed with gray or brown silky pubescence. The leaves tri-foliate, large and stipulate. Petiole is 10-15 cm long. Leaflets are coriaceous, obtuse, glabrous above, finely silky and conspicuously reticulately veined beneath with cunnate or deltoid base. The fruit of palas is a flat legume (pod) nearly 15 cm long and 3 to 5 cm wide. Young pods have a lot of hair-a velvety cover. The mature pods hang down like peculiar legumes (8, 9, 10, 11, 12).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Flowers scarlet and orange in rigid racemes of 15 cm long, densely brown velvety on bare branches. Calyx is dark, olive green to brown in colour and densely velvety outside. The corolla is long with silky silvery hairs outside and bright orange red. Stamens are diadelphes, anthers uniform. Ovary 2 ovule, style filiform, curved and stigma capitate (3, 4, 8, 9, 10, 12).

Phenology :
Leaf shedding takes place in November-December and the new leaves appear in April-May. The trees remain leafless during the winter months. Some trees may however, retain some leaves on the lower branches throughout winter till the new leaves appear. The flower buds appear in January and the scarlet blossom bursts forth through February and March. Some trees flower in January. The blossom, which is borne on the branches on which new leaves are yet to appear presents a gorgeous sight. Butea monosperma is therefore known as the 'Flame of the forest' (8, 11).

Seed Biology :
The seeds are flat, reniform, 25 to 40 mm long, 15 to 25 mm wide, and 1.5 to 2 mm thick. Seeds looses viability within a year. About 1,480 seeds weigh one kilogram. The seed coat is reddish brown in colour, glossy, wrinkled, and encloses two large, leafy, yellowish cotyledons. The hilum is conspicuous, and situated near the middle of the concave edge of the seed. The odor is faint, and the taste slightly acrid and bitter (7, 8, 10, 11).

Seedling Germination :
Fresh seeds have high germinative power. Germination starts in about 10-12 days and takes about four weeks to complete. Germination is hypogeous. The primary root develops rapidly and is long, thick, fleshy, terete and tapering with lateral roots moderate in number. The growth of seedlings under irrigated conditions is moderately fast and a height growth of about 3.5 m can be expected in three years (7, 8, 10, 11).

Cultivation:

Butea monosperma can be raised either by direct sowing or by planting out nursery raised seedlings or by stump planting.

Direct sowing: Sowing is done with the commencement of monsoon rains in lines about 3 m apart. The lines are cleared of all the bushes and weeds about one metre in width. The soil is worked well in strips to about 30 cm width. The seeds are sown about 10 cm apart and are only lightly covered with soil. About 14 and 22 kg of seed wll be enough to sow one hectare with 5 m and 3m spacing respectively. Regular weeding and bush cutting are necessary to protect young seedlings from suppression. The seedlings are subsequently spaced about 20-30 cm in lines to reduce competition. Further thinning of the seedlings will depend upon their growth rate. An ultimate spacing of about 2-3 m between plants is given.

Nursery and Planting technique: The nursery beds are prepared in June and sowing is done with the onset of monsoon rains. In irrigated nurseries the sowing can be done in June also. Seeds are sown about 8 cm apart in lines spaced about 15 cm apart. Seeds are only lightly covered. About 50 g seed will be sufficient to sow each square metre of the nursery area. Regular weeding of the nursery beds is necessary. One year old seedlings are planted out in July. The seedlings are uprooted from the nursery beds with care. The lateral roots and part of the taproot are pruned. The lower leaves are plucked to reduce transpiration. The roots of the seedlings are wrapped in moist hyasin cloth lined with a thin layer of soil. Planting is done 30 cm3 pits dug in advance. Bushes likely to shade the plants are cut at the time of planting. Planting is preferably done on rainy or cloudy days.

Stumps prepared out of one year old seedling also give good success. Stumps should have as long root portion as can be accommodated in 30 cm3 pits and unnecessary cutting or pruning of root should be avoided. Planting of stumps is done in the same manner as of entire plants (7, 8, 9, 11, 12).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

A large number of insect pests have been reported to attack different parts of the tree in various ways. Several defoliators have been recorded belonging to Arctiidae, Eucosmidae, Lasiocampidae, Lymantriidae, Noctuidae, Notodontidae, Pieridae, and Sphingidae families. Some insects of the family Coccidae feed on the sap. The larvae of about half a dozen insects of Lycaenidae feed on the flowers. Cyclopelta siccifolia attacks the young shoots which show desiccation of leaves often associated with leaf fall and withering away of the shoots (8, 11).

Seedlings and saplings are browsed and damaged by cattle. Rats and porcupines feed on fleshy roots and kill the saplings. Barbed wire fencing to keep the browsing animals out and killing of rodents and porcupines through poisonous baits should be helpful in affording necessary protection to the plantations.


Diseases:

No serious disease has been reported on palas. Xanthomonas buteae causes black leaf spots which in case of severe infection, cover the entire leaf surface and cause premature defoliation. Phomopsis buteae and Pseudodiplodia buteae have also been recorded on the leaves. Powdry mildew caused by Erysiphe polygoni was reported for the first time on palas in Madhya Pradesh (8, 11, 18).

Economic Potential and Uses

The seeds contain up 18-20 per cent of a fatty oil known as Moodooga Oil or Kino-Tree oil. The oil on low temperature crystallization yields two major fractions of the triglycerides, each comprising 30 per cent of the total. The fraction, rich in saturated glycerides, may be used as a base for ointments and the other as a solvent for drugs. The oil can be used in soap-making, the de-oiled meal is a good source of protein. The seed-meal blended with tannin-urea-formalin mixture forms a tannin adhesive suitable for plywood manufacture. The seed oil showed cardiac effects with inhibition of ventricular movement, reduction in the amplitude of contraction of isolated frog heart and duodenal muscle relaxation in the rabbit. The seed oil is also effective against the insect pest of wheat grain at rate of 5 ml/kg of seed (8, 12, 13, 14, 19, 20).

Uses :

Dhak is a useful plant in many ways. Its leaves are essential for various religious rituals in Hindu homes. These are also used as cheap leaf plates and cups for rural feasts. In some parts of the country these are used for wrapping tobacco to make biddies. These are further used as packing material for parcels. The bark of yields a kind of coarse and brown colored fiber, which is used for rough cordage. Gum from the bark, finds use for caulking boats. The gum is also used for dyeing and tanning. The flowers yield an orange dye. The green leaves are commonly lopped for fodder, the yield of milk in buffaloes fed with Butea leaves, is reported to improve. The flowers yield a brilliant, but very fugitive, yellow dye. It is contained in the sap and may be obtained in the form of a decoction or an infusion from dried flowers. The decoction is used to dye cotton fabrics, sola articles and woollen carpets and to control white ants in the field. The powder of the flowers is used during holi (the festival of colours).

The tree acts as a host for lac insect and is therefore, useful in producing natural lac. The wood of Dhak is greenish white in colour. It generally perishes fast when used at sites open to vagaries of weather, but lasts much longer when used under water. It is therefore used for making well curbs and piles. It is the source of charcoal used for gun powder. It can also be employed as a cheap board wood. The pulp obtained from a mixture of hardwoods containing the wood of B. monosperma is suitable for Braille printing paper and wrapping paper. The pulp from B. monosperma can also be used for making newsprint (8, 9, 12, 21).

Harvesting Practices
Ripe pods are collected from the trees in June. These are dried in the sun, thrashed and winnowed to separate clean seed, which is then dried in the sun.

Produce and Products

Leaves are valued as fodder. Oil is extracted from the seeds. All plant parts have greater medicinal importance. Pulp from the wood is employed in paper making.

Processing Techniques

The processing of Dhak seeds is carried out in bullock-driven wooden ghanis (kohlus) and powder driven ghanis (rotary mills). The ghani (kohlu) is devised on a pestle and mortar rotating system driven by a pair of bullocks. In semi-urban areas, where electricity is available, most of the bullock-driven ghanis have been replaced by motorized rotaries for crushing seeds. These rotaries take less space than the ghanis, but Butea monosperma oil is not being exploited on a commercial scale (15).

Other Uses
Gum: The fresh juice is applied to ulcers and in relaxed, congested and septic sore throat. The gum is a powerful astringent, it is given internally for diarrhoea and dysentery, phthisis and haemorrhage from stomach and bladder, its infusion is occasionally employed as a local application in leucorrhoea. A solution of the gum is applied to bruises and erysipelatous inflammations and ringworm.

Bark: The bark is astringent, bitter, pungent, alterative, aphrodisiac and anthelmintic properties. It is useful in tumours, bleeding piles and ulcers. The decoction is prescribed in cold, cough, fever, various forms of haemorrhages, in menstrual disorders and in the preparation of tonics and elixirs. An alcoholic extract of the bark is reported to inhibit the activity of Escherichia coli and Micrococcus pyogenes.

Roots: The roots are useful in elephantiasis and in curing night blindness and other defects of sight. They are also reported to cause temporary sterility in women. The rootbark is used as an aphrodisiac and as analgesic and anthelmintic. It is also applied in sprue, piles, ulcers, tumours and dropsy.

Leaves: Leaves are credited with astringent, tonic, diuretic and aphrodisiac properties. They are used to cure boils, pimples and tumorous haemorrhoids and are internally given in flatulent colic, worms and piles. The shoot apex is used by the Tribals to prevent conception.

Flowers: The flowers are reported to possess astringent, diuretic, depurative, aphrodisiac and tonic properties. They are used as an emmenagogue, as poultice in orchitis and to reduce swellings, for bruises and sprains. They are also effective in leprosy, leucorrhoea and gout. A decoction of the flowers is given in diarrhoea and to puerperal women. The aqueous extract of the flowers shows significant anti-implantation activity in rats.

Seeds: The seeds possess a faint odour, and taste slightly acrid and bitter. They are reported to possess aperient and rubefacient properties. A composite powder from the dried seeds was found to be effective in controlling the worm in human intestine. Seeds are also used as vermifuge in veterinary medicine. The hot alcoholic extract of the seeds showed significant anti-implantation and anti-ovulatory activity in rats and rabbits respectively (8, 9, 10, 13, 16, 17).

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

All the plant parts are highly priced for their medicinal importance. Apart from that seeds have an excellent oil, which is being used in pharmaceutical industry and soap making, but till now, no attempt has been made for extensive cultivation of tree. As a result of which, there is no organised collection of seeds and hence seed oil is not being exploited on a commercial scale.
