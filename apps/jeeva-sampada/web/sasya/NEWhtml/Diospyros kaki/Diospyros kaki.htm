HABIT & HABITAT
A small tree, native of the Khasia hills, Upper Assam and Burma. Also found in the mountains of Nepal to the northward of Bengal.

The persimmon is native to Oriental Asia, most probably China, where it has been cultivated for centuries (Kajiura, 1979). However, most selection and development of the fruit have occurred in Japan, where it is called kaki. Before 1900, kaki was the most important fruit tree in Japan, however, today it is only the fifth most important tree fruit in that country. At the end of the nineteenth century, the persimmon was introduced in many countries in the temperate zone. Commercial plantings exist in the USA, Brazil, Italy, Israel, New Zealand and Australia. It has a compact growth habit (3 m tall at maturity), bears very early (usually in the second year), gives high annual yield with medium sized fruit

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
  The Oriental persimmon is a round topped, usually decidious, treeto 20 feet height unless it is competing with other trees for light, when it might reach 40 feet. It has 3-7 inch elliptic glossy leaves and 3/4 inch long, yellowish- white flowers. The fruit is variable in shape- oval, round, globular, or elongated- and ribbed with brownish pulp surrounding the seed, if any are Cultivation is simillar to that of the citrus or stone fruit trees grown in warmer areas.present This new growth emerges from one year old wood. Three types of flowers are possible: female (pistillate), male (staminate) and hermaphrodite (perfect) flowers. In addition, persimmon trees may be monoecious, dioecious, or rarely polygamous. The companulate flowers are three quarters of an inch long and yellowish white, with outfolded, prominent geen sepals extending beyond the corolla. The staminate ones have 16-24 stamens, the pistillate ones have eight staminodia. Nector secretion is probably similar to that of the American species. The blossoms hang downward, with the stigmararely exposed beyond the petals, which offers little opportunity for wind pollination.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Breeding System :
 Persimmon trees may be monoecious, dioecious, or rarely polygamous.

Phenology :
Flowers are produced on new growth in the spring period i.e., March - April (01).

Pollinators:
Cultivars of persimmon are known to have problems with low fruit set and fruit fall. Planting of adequate numbers of pollinizer trees are essential for adequate fruit set. The few cultivars, which bear pistillate and staminate flowers, are used as pollinizers. The most important pollinizers used are Gailey, Akagaki and Omiyawase.

Seed Biology :

Seed Germination :
Seed - best sown in a cold frame as soon as it is ripe. Stored seed requires a period of cold-stratification and should be sown as early in the year as possible[78]. It usually germinates in 1 - 6 months at 15°c. Pot up the young seedlings as soon as they are large enough to handle into fairly deep pots and plant them out into their permanent positions in early summer. Give them some protection from winter cold for their first year or two outdoors. Cuttings of half-ripe wood, July/August in a frame. Layering in spring.

Cultivation:
Prefers a good deep loamy soil in sun or light shade but succeeds in most soils. Dislikes very acid or wet and poorly drained soils. Requires a sheltered position.
Dormant plants are quite hardy in Britain, tolerating temperatures down to about -14°c, but they require warmer summers than are normally experienced in Britain in order to ripen their fruit and wood. The young growth in spring, even on mature plants, is frost-tender and so it is best to grow the plants in a position sheltered from the early morning sun. A warm sunny wall improves the chance of producing ripe fruit and trees fruit freely when grown under glass. Fruits are frequently produced outdoors at Kew. A tree seen in a open position with afternoon shade at Kew in November 1993 (after a cool summer) had about 200 almost ripe fruits around 8cm in diameter. The same tree, after a fairly warm summer in 1996, had a large quantity of fruit just about ready for harvesting in the middle of December.

Trees produce a long taproot and should be planted out into their permanent positions as soon as possible. The young trees require some winter protection for their first winter or two.

The persimmon is widely cultivated for its edible fruit in warm temperate areas of the world, especially in Japan and China, there are many named varieties. Some cultivars, such as 'Fuyu', lack the usual astringency and can be eaten whilst still firm, though they develop a richer flavour if allowed to become soft. These non-astringent forms require a warmer climate and do not ripen in cooler areas. The astringent cultivars are somewhat hardier and ripen well in cooler climates than the non-astringent forms. The fruit colours better and is sweeter in warmer areas but in hot conditions has a poor texture and deep black spots develop. If allowed to become very ripe (almost to the point of going rotten), they develop a better flavour than non-astringent forms.

Dioecious, but the female tree can produce seedless fruits in the absence of a pollinator. However, unfertilized fruit tends to be smaller and more astringent. This astringency is due to the high content of tannin but once the fruit is fully ripe it loses this astringency and becomes sweet. If fertilized fruit is required, then growing one male for every 8 - 10 females is usually adequate.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:
Insect pests of persimmons vary considerably in different regions of the world. The major pests include mealy bugs, fruit flies, thrips, spotting bugs and twig girdlers and borers (George and Nissen, 1984; Kitagawa and Glucina, 1984; Miller, 1984).

Eriococcus lagerstroemiae: The first occurrence of the larval stage is reported to be from late June to early July; the second from mid- to late-August and the third from mid- to late-September. Two applications of lime sulphur [calcium polysulphide] and machine oil in late June and late August gave 97.8% and 96.8% control respectively (Kwon et al., 1995).

Hemipterous insects: Pentatomid yellow brown stink bug (Halyomorpha halys) and coreid bug (Riptortus clavatus). Symptoms of damage to persimmon fruits by Hemiptera include concave surfaces or a dark blue colour like black-eye. Fruit surface on cutting reveals spongy structure. In severe attack the fruit becomes soft and red like ripe fruit. Most serious damage occurs from late August to late October.

In recent years, an increase in the damage from mealybugs (Pseudococcus citriculus) in persimmon fruits was observed (Izhar, 1999). The mealybug settles under the sepal and the connection between fruit and leaves. It sucks the fruits and the honeydew causes black knots.Dorsan (chlorpyrifbs) 5% and diazinon 4% applied around the trunk gave excellent results against ants.

Diseases:
Crown gall: (Agrobacterium radiobacter var. tumefaciens) Symptoms are large galls or swellings at the crown often with smaller, nut-sized galls on large roots. Control involves not planting affected nursery trees and not using D. lotus as a rootstock as this species may be more susceptible to crown gall.

Dematophora root rot (Dematophora necatrix): In Israel, Dematophora root rot is a major cause of tree loss. Diospyros kaki is less susceptible than D. virginiana (Szteinberg and Jabareen, 1986). Vegetative propagation of resistant rootstocks is recommended to maintain resistance.

Cercospora leaf spot (Cercospora kaki): Symptoms are small, dark brown, angular spots on leaves. Some cultivars are more resistant than others.

Circular leaf spot (Mycospharella nawae): Symptoms are small, round, brown spots on the leaves.
Bitter rot or anthracnose (Gloesporium kaki): Anthracnose infects both fruits and shoots, but large differences in resistance occur between cultivars.

Post harvest diseases: The most serious post harvest disease is Penicillium (Kitagawa and Glucina, 1984). However, Penicillium can not invade the fruit unless an injury occurs to the skin. In general, damage caused by fruit softening during transportation and storage is a more serious problem than damage from decay.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Dried and Green fruits, Fruit skin, Seeds, Leaves,

Uses:

  • Fresh kaki fruits are often included in particular dishes, especially in various jellies and jams, and the peeled skins are added to pickles to provide a bright orange colour. In Japan, a small amount of kaki is canned in sugar syrup for out-of-season consumption. Green fruits, especially from strongly astringent cultivars, such as Tsurunoko and Tsunagaki, are used in production of tannin of particularly high quality. Several hun- dred recipes for using persimmon fruit were presented by Griffith et al. (1982). Piretti (1990) reviewed the uses of kaki-tannin as mordant for protecting wood; as treatment of hangover; as antidote against some cobra venoms and bacterial toxins; in pertussis vaccine; as control agent for viral infections in plants, notably against tobacco mosaic virus on tomato.
  • Fruit - raw or cooked. The fruit has an exquisitely rich flavour when it is very soft and fully ripe (almost at the point of going bad), but the fruit of many cultivars is very harsh and astringent before then. In Britain, the fruit needs to be harvested whilst it is still very hard. This is done very late in the season (in December or even January if possible), it is then stored in a cool but frost-free place until very soft and fully ripe. The fruit can also be used in pies, cakes, bread, desserts etc. It contains 25% sugars. A fuller nutritional analysis is available. The fruit can also be dried for later use. The fruit is about 7.5cm in diameter.
  • The peel of the fruit can be powdered and used as a sweetener.
  • The leaves are used to improve the flavour of pickled radishes.
  • The roasted seeds are a coffee substitute.
  • In Japan black dyes are produced from the fruit of this tree with sulphate of Iron.

Harvesting Practices
The fruits mature in mid- to late September, they are oblate in shape with a glossy orange-yellow/orange-red skin and weigh 138 g on average. The flesh has some brown spots; it is crisp and sweet, with a soluble solids content of 14%, no astringency, and is of good eating quality. Fruits may be kept for 10 days without softening. Fruits mature in late September weigh 150 g on average and can be stored for up to 2 months at room temperature after harvest. The fruit skin is orange yellow and the flesh orange-red. Trees are self-fertile, very productive, resistant to drought and diseases, and adaptable to infertile soils. A minimum Brix between 14° and 16° at harvest is required.

Dried kald is a traditional feature of the New Year festival season, and a good portion of the Japanese production of kaki fruit is dried in the autumn. Mature but firm fruits are harvested, peeled and sun-dried after threading onto strings. Small fruits may take 18-25 days to dry but large fruits may require 35-40 days and since the late autumn for drying is often short, this may not always be successful. There is considerable risk of contamination of impurities, moulds and discoloration (Kim et al., 1993). In larger commercial operations, fruits are first fumigated with sulphur dioxide and then artificially dried at 32-33°C. Fruits dried by combinations of sun and hot air dried faster than either of the methods alone. However, artificial ventilation resulted in the best quality fruits, followed by sun dried fruits (Lee et al., 1994). The far-infrared method could shorten drying time by approximately 25 days compared with sun drying and the dried fruits were better in quality with respect to colour and appearance than sun drying method (Kim et al., 1993).The best dried kaki fruits are judged by high sugar content and a thick coating of sugar crystals as an indication of good quality. The texture should not be too mealy and the flesh should be free from stringy fibre. Seedless cultivars are preferred for drying and there should be no remnants ofastringency after drying. Hiratanenashi is the best kaki cultivar for drying, but Yetsumizo, Koshu-Hyakume and Yamato-Hyakume are very popular. Ichidagaki and Dojo-Hachiya are also used as dried kaki.

Chemistry
According to Ito (1962) persimmons contain approximately 79 per cent water, 0.4 per cent protein, 0.4 per cent crude fibre and 0.7 per cent pectin. Persimmons are high in vitamins A and C, containing 2710 International units of vitamin A per 100 g of fruit and 11 mg of ascorbic acid per 100 g of fruit (Griffith et al., 1982).

Other Uses :

  1. The pulp of unripe fruits is used in cosmetics to make face-packs because of its firming qualities.
  2. Wood - hard and durable with a beautiful grain. Used for making fine furniture. In Japan, astringent persimmons have become an important source of condensed tannins for use as a deproteinizing agent and coating material for wood, paper and fibres .
  3. Anthelmintic; Antitussive; Antivinous; Appetizer; Astringent; Demulcent; Expectorant; Febrifuge; Hypotensive; Laxative; Sialagogue; Stomachic; Styptic.
    Appetizer, sialagogue.
  4. The stem bark is astringent and styptic.
  5. The fruit is said to have different properties depending on its stage of ripeness, though it is generally antitussive, astringent, laxative, nutritive and stomachic.
  6. The fresh fully ripe fruit is used raw in the treatment of constipation and haemorrhoidsand when cooked is used to treat diarrhoea.
  7. The dried ripe fruit is used in the treatment of bronchial complaints, whilst when ground into a powder it is used to treat dry coughs.
  8. Juice from the unripe fruit is used in the treatment of hypertension.
  9. The fruits, picked green and ripened in containers with the leaves, become very sweet and are considered to be antifebrile, antivinous and demulcent.
  10. The fruits are also peeled and then exposed to sunlight by day and dew by night. They become encrusted with a white powder and are then considered to be anthelmintic, antihaemorrhagic, antivinous, expectorant, febrifuge and restorative.
  11. The peduncle is used to treat coughs and hiccups.
  12. The calyx is used to treat hiccups.

Miscellaneous Information

Cultivars:
'Saijo'
A small, elongated fruit, astringent when not fully ripe, but becomes sweet with an excellent flavour if allowed to ripen fully[183]. The fruits are usually seedless and store well[183]. The dried fruit is very attractive[183]. A medium-size tree, it bears consistently and tolerates temperatures down to at least -23°c[183].
'Great Wall' 'Atoma'
The small to medium size fruit is up to 6cm in diameter, the flesh is dry and very sweet, but astringent if not fully ripe[183]. The fruit ripens in mid-Autumn[183]. A relatively slow-growing, upright tree, it is very cold hardy and bears heavy crops every second year[183]. It is recommended for colder areas, though it also does well in Florida.

Disorders:

Calyx cavity:
Calyx cavity (syn. dehiscence) can be a serious problem in persimmons. Symptom is a space or cavity that occurs directly beneath the calyx of the fruit. The cavity becomes a habitat for mealy bugs and fungal growth. Some cultivars are more susceptible than others.Control measures recommended in New Zealand include the avoidance of excessive N and K fertilization, especially in late spring/summer and close to harvest; thinning early in the season to enhance calyx growth; optimising pollination to produce more than 3 seed/fruit.Planting of trees on preformed ridges or mounds or into plastic is suggested.

Skin russeting:
Causes are not totally known, but may be due to excessive nitrogen and high relative humidity during fruit maturation.

Calyx-end cracking:
The problem may occur with some cultivars. Causes are not known. However, there was a positive correlation between the severity of cracking and seed number in some genotypes. Generally, cracking was minimal in fruits with no seeds, regardless of whether flowers were bagged or open pollinated. Some genotypes also showed a correlation between fruit weight and cracking.

Sujika:
Sujika, a disorder producing white lines on the rind of fruit was studied by Kimura et al. (1989). Sujika was more frequent on light-fruiting than on heavy-fruiting trees and was not correlated with numbers of seeds/fruit. The rate of sujika was temporarily reduced about 10 days after ringing in mid- or late-June; ringing in mid-June was particularly effective. When fruits still attached to the tree were covered with plastic film the incidence ofsujika increased to 70%, compared with 40% for the controls A night temperature of 18-20°C produced more sujika than one at 15°C. Sujika is concluded to be closely related to fruit enlargement.

Nutritional disorders
Many cultivars are susceptible to fruit drop and calyx cavity if nitrogen levels are too high when fruits are sizing. Nitrogen rates should be reduced if these problems are evident, dark and Kajiura (1986) and dark and Smith (1985) described the nutri- tional disorders of persimmon. The most widely occurring nutritional disorder is green blotch. With this disorder the skin, particularly around the apex, stops developing and remains green as the fruits continue to mature (Dark and Kajiura, 1986). Comparisons between affected and healthy orchards in Japan indicate that the manganese concen- tration in the leaves of trees in bearing affected fruits (600-2000 ppm Mn) is typically 2-4 times greater than normal (300-500 ppm Mn). Spray application of calcium carbonate to young fruit may reduce the disorder.
