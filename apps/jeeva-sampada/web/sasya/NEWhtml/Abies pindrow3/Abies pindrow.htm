Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Tall evergreen tree found in temperate Himalayan region between 2100-3600 m above sea level, from Kashmir to Nepal. Mostly in shady areas and on northern aspects.

It is commonly found mixed with spruce (Picea smithiana Boiss.) and sometimes with deodar (Cedrus deodara Loud.), blue pine (Pinus wallichiana Jackson) and oak (Quercus spp.).

Frost hardy, suffer little from snow damage and are sensitive to fire. It prefers cool, moist situations with deep, rich soil. It avoids dry, shallow soil, though sometimes found on the tops of ridges, and in such situations it is usually stunted.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

A tall tree (50-60 m) with cylindrical trunk and dark foliaged crown. Dark green foliage frequently extends to the whole length of the tree, concealing the entire bole. Bark is smooth on young branches and greyish brown but deeply cleft in vertical fissures when old.

Leaf is 5-7 cm long, apex with 2 sharp teeth, underside with two faint lines on either side of the raised midrib, upper side is dark green and shining.

Male catkins axillary, many 1-1.5 cm long. Cones cylindrical, 10-15 cm long and 4-6 cm in diameter, dark purple when ripe, scales obovate, edge rounded, thin, ripen in autumn.

Phenology:

Flowers appear in summer. New shoots appear during April - May. The pollen is shed chiefly from the end of April to mid May, at which time the scales of the cones are open to receive it and they close soon after pollination. Young cones ripen in October or early November.

Floral and seed biology:

Seed Germination & Propagation: Natural regeneration takes place on newly exposed mineral soil in absence of sour humus, provided the seedlings are protected in early stages from sun, grazing and fire. Seeds are shed during October - November and germinate mostly during the following May and early June. The growth commences in April or early May and is practically completed by the end of June. The growth of the natural seedlings is slow for several years and varies during the first three years. Subsequent growth is comparatively faster.

Artificial regeneration is practised through nursery raised seedlings, which take 5-6 growing seasons to reach transplantable height of 22 cm. The period in nurseries has been successfully reduced to 4 growing seasons by application of growth promoting hormones and fortification of seedlings with mycorrhiza. Seeds are sown in lines in beds by spreading or dibbling. It should be sown about 0.25 cm deep and covered with a thin layer of well rotten Farm Yard Manure. Seeds are sown in early spring and germination is complete in about 4-5 weeks.

Generally, 2-3 year old seedlings are transplanted in the monsoon season in the field with spacing of 2-3 m. Seedlings are sensitive to drought and frost.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Wood used as fuel.

Wood: Wood is creamy white to pale yellow, turning light brown on exposure. Soft to moderately hard, light to moderately heavy, average weight 450 kg/m3 (28 lb/ft3) at 12 per cent moisture content; somewhat lustrous when fresh, medium fine and even-textured with straight grain. The wood is quite lustrous at first, turning dull with age, without characteristic odour or taste, very light (sp. gr. 0.427-0.534), softer than the wood of Picea smithiana.

A non-porous wood. Growth rings distinct on all the three surfaces. Transition from early wood to late wood gradual to abrupt. Resin canals absent. Rays very fine, not visible to eye, but seen under the lens as fine, closely-spaced lines.

It is light and used for packing cases, matchwood and wood wool, aircraft work and plywood. This odourless wood is much in demand for butter, lard and grocery boxes.

The timber is employed for planking, shingles, as ceiling- and floor-boards, trusses, struts, beams, rafters, purlins, for fruit-crates, opium chests, tea chests and other packing cases, after treatment for railway sleepers and water troughs. It is also suitable for camp furniture. It is used for battery separators, foot rules, penholders, cheap grade pencils, photo- and slate-frames, laminated boards and ammunition boxes. The green wood is good for matchsplints.

Wood is also used for higher grades of writing and printing paper. The wood appears to be a promising raw material for textile rayon and for tyre cords, chipboards comparable in strength to imported ones can be made.

Wood yields turpentine and essential oil.
