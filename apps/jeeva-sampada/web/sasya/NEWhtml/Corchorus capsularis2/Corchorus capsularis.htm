Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Jute plant is a herbaceous annual with a straight stem. It is native to South China where it occurs wild. The major jute growing countries are India, Bangladesh, China, Thailand, Myanmar, Indonesia, Nepal, Russian Federation and Brazil. In India it is grown in West Bengal, Bihar, Assam and Andhra Pradesh.
Availability : Cultivated abundantly


Conservation/ Threat Status : Both wild and cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Jute plant reaches a height of 4.5 mt. with a straight stem which is 1-2 cm in diameter near the tip. Leaves are light green, alternate, oblong, 5-10 cm long, and serrate, the two lowermost teeth are prolonged into sharp whiskers. The flowers are small, yellow and generally in extra-axillary cymes in groups of 2-5 or more. There are usually 5 sepals and 5 petals. The fruit is a globose capsule, 1.5-2 cm in diameter, and wrinkled. Seeds are copper red.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:
C. capsularis flowers bear 10 or more but less than 30 stamens. The ovary is 2 to 5 celled. Ovule is anatropous, bitegmic and crassiucellate. Capsule dehisces loculidically into five parts without transverse partitions between seeds. Seed is endospermous. The embryo is straight.

Thirteen days after pollination the embryo becomes heart shaped and the upper half of the embryo sac contains cellular endosperm. In another 2 days the entire endosperm becomes cellular and the embryo has well differentiated cotyledons; 21 days after pollination the cotyledons enlarge and 30 days after pollination they expand and become leafy.

Breeding system and varietal improvement:
C. capsularis is highly self-pollinating; it is an annual, seed-propagated crop. Seed is not generally marketed; each grower conserves some plants for seed collection.

Several attempts have been made to make crosses between C. olitorius and C. capsularis, the two jute yielding species. Ganesan et. al. Reported that following the interspecific hybridization a few celled embryo and few free endosperm nuclei were formed. The failure to obtain hybrid seedlings has been attributed to premature degeneration of embryo and endosperm. Islam and Rashid crossed C. capsularis with C. olitorius and induced full development o seed by using the hormone 3-indole acetic acid. The factor which may have led to precocious degeneration of embryo and endosperm may have been counteracted by the hormone.

Sulbha and Swaminathan grafted he two species of Corchorus reciprocally and used the grafted plants for interspecific hybridization. Whereas the grafted plants increased the percentage of fruit set, they did not lead to any marked improvement in the growth of the hybrid embryo, when C. oliotorius was used as the pistillate parent; however, striking increase in fruit set occured when C. capsularis was used as pistillate .


Phenology: C. capsularis flowers during the rainy season; fruits ripen during September-October


Pollinators:
Although C. capsularis is principally self pollinated, some crossing is effected by insects or wind. Ghose and Das Gupta 1945 (15) state that natural cross-pollination in C. capsularis is 2-5%. Natural crossing in C. capsularis in two areas of Taiwan ranged from 0.1% to 1.88% usually less than 1% (Taiwan fiber Crops Experiment Station 1970). There was little evidence of insect pollination. Wind pollination has been said to be responsible for crossing.
Floral and seed biology:

Seedling germination :

The traditional method of planning jute is by broadcasting. Over planting is a scarcity of seed. Jute seeds are tiny in comparison to seeds of other commercial fiber crops. C. capsularis seeds average about 300/g and those of C. olitorius 500/g. Seeds are broadcast usually mixed with dry sad to obtain a more uniform stand; half the quantity is broadcast is north-south direction and the other half in east-west direction to obtain a more uniform distribution. The seeding rate for Capsularis jute is generally greater than that of Olitorius jute because the former seeds are larger. The seeding rates of C. capsularis and C. olitorius used in India are respectively 10.0 kg/ha and 6.0 kg/ha. The advantages of row cropping for outweigh the traditional method of broadcast seeding.

Row-planted jute is more uniform; it is easier to cultivate, thin, harvest, and ret; the yield is substantially greater, and the fiber from row-planted jute is more uniform. In general, closer row width and plant spacing in the row give higher, higher yields, and this increase is greater with capsularis than with Olitorius varieties.


MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
Agrotis ypsilon Ritt (the black cutworm) and Feltia subterranean (Fab.) (granulate cutworm) cut off plants near the soil level. These pests can be controlled by preparation of land if weedy and application of toxaphene.

Aphis gossypii (Glov) stunts young plants; 1-2% parathion dusts give adequate control. Estigmene acraea Drury (salt marsh caterpillar) is another pest of jute plant which can be controlled by parathion and toxaphene dust.

Prodenia eradania (Cram.) acts as a defoliator. Good control is obtained with 10% toxaphene dust.

Apion spp. (small weevils) breed in stem tunneling half way round generally near leaf axils and causes stunting of plants.

Cosmophila sabulifera (leaf eating semi looper) attacks leaves and leaf buds.

Indigo caterpillar (Laphyma exigua) feeds on young leaves. In worst cases they wholly defoliate the plant.

Diacrisia oblique (Hairy caterpiller) feeds on jute leaves (5).


Diseases:

Root rot and stem rot occur at all stages of plant growth and damage leaves and stem. The affected plants wilt and shed their leaves prematurely. Both the diseases are caused by Macrophomina phaseoli (Maubl.) Ashby. Control measures include field sanitation practices together with balanced fertilizer application, spraying fungicides to prevent foliar infections, and use of resistant varieties. Powdery mildew cased by Oidium sp. produces powdery white ash on leaves which later brown and wither. Control measures include dusting of finely powdered sulphur or use of resistant varieties.

Anthracnose caused by Colletotrichum corchorum results in necrotic lesions on stem and pods.

Stem gall is caused by Physoderma corchori Lingappa. Black band is caused by Diplodia corchori Syd, sclerotial disease by Pellicularia rolfsii, and leaf spot by Phyllosticta sp.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
fiber

Jute is used in larger quantities than all other plant fibers combined except cotton. Jute is used for making hessian, sackings, linoleum backing, carpets (in conjunction with cotton yarns), jute twines, roofing fabrics, laminated jute (as a substitute for metal and plywood), for sandbags, tents, water holding stores, webbings, theatrical canvas and scrims, kit bags, tool bags and luggage cover. For clothing material, jute is bled with wool or other fibers for blankets, carpets, rugs and antiseptic surgical dressings.

MEDICINAL
Jute are eaten as pot-herb, stomachic, laxative, carminative, stimulant, demulcent, toxic and diuretic

Parts Used:

Stem, leaves, fibers.


Harvesting Practices:
Jute crop is ready for harvest in 3-4 months from seeding. As soon as the plants begin to bloom usually during August-September, and before seed formation, whole plants are cut close to the soil surface.

Jute harvesting has not been mechanized because crop is grown by small holders in relatively small plots. However, cultivating the crop in larger plots would allow mechanization of harvesting..

The fiber strands are 6-10 feet long. The individual fire cells are 1-5 cm long and about 1/40 mm wide. The fiber cells are so firmly connected together that they fail to separate in retting or even in manufacture. The walls are thick but not uniform


Produce and Products:

Produce : Jute fiber
Product : Jute fabric, jute reinforced plastic (JRP) and several items of utility. Many substances are used to make jute water resistant. Flame proofing is achieved by a treatment with an aqueous solution of mineral salts.

Chemistry:
An average sample of jute contains 56-63% cellulose, 22-26% hemicellulose, 11-12% lignin, and minor components such as fats, waxes, gum and minerals. This chemical composition makes jute a very harsh fiber.

Processing Techniques:
Cut stems are dried to eliminate foliage, and tied into bundles (jaks) for submergence in pools or ditches for retting; soft tissues binding the fibers disintegrate in 1-4 weeks. In India a number of bacteria responsible for retting have been isolated and proven able to hydrolyze the pectins in as short a period as 5 days . Jute bundles have been found to ret better when steeped at a depth of 6-9 inches in slow-flowing, clear water. The top layer is covered with weeds or other refuse and is submerged by weighting it down with logs of wood, clods of earth or various other locally available materials. However, banana stems, freshly cut log of mango and bel or clods of earth are rich in tannin which in contact with the iron of the retting water discolours the finished jute fiber. By using aquatic plants as cover, the jaks may be weighted down by stones, bricks or dry logs .

Manual separation of the fibers from the stalks follows retting, is usually accomplished while the stem stands in the pond. The fibers float on the surface and are swished to from fragments of bark. Water retting (anaerobic retting) is the only method to separate the fibers. Yields may be as high as 1500 lb of dry fiber and acre . Jute fiber is susceptible to microbiological decay, especially under high temperature and high RH. Rot proofing is accomplished by the use of antiseptics which are fixed on or within the fabric.

Jute Reinforced Plastic (JRP) is made when jute fabric of different types is impregnated in a thermosetting resin solution to desired level, dried and cut into required length manually or with automatic cutters. Jute can be readily dyed, but not bleached.


Other uses:

Although jute is one of the poorest of the textile or fast fibers, it is the most important from the stand point of quantity used, and the diversity of use to which the fiber is put.

JRP is used in manufacturing of boxes for packing tea, apples, horticultural produce, readymade garments and preservation of forests.

NIRJAFT (National Institute of Research on Jute and Allied fiber Technology), West Bengal has developed disposable jute carry bags with a cellulosic lining, a jute substitute of wool, an alternate process for growth of mushroom on jute waste, a technology for growth of mushroom on jute waste, a technology for using jute pulp for manufacture of paperboards. Jute carry bags would replace the non biodegradable plastic bags. Non woven jute is used for separation of gravel and sub-soils. It also has been used in controlling river bank erosion, protection of plants and moisture control in agricultural activities.

Jute is also used in interior decoration, manufacturing of footwears, garments and ornaments.

Economic Potential to the Country
India is the largest producer of jute in the world accounting for about 40% of the world's total output and the second only to Bangladesh in exporting jute and jute products. Jute is a Rs. 6000 crore industry in India with annual exports of Rs. 450-650 crores.

In recent years, the demand for jute and jute products has been falling both domestically and the world over. The domestic demand in India is being forcibly sustained by a government policy which has made it mandatory to pack commodities such as food grains and sugar and to some extent fertilizers and cement in jute bags only. The world demand for jute arose mainly from carpet backing cloth which has also started falling with replacement of jute by synthetic materials .

In 1999, globally the area under jute was 19.48 lakh ha and the yield was 1708 kg/ha was 8.74 lakh ha in 1996-97. According to the Ministry of Agriculture in India the area under jute was 7.55 lakh ha (1997-98) and the production of jute during 1997-9 was ca 9.7 million bales.
