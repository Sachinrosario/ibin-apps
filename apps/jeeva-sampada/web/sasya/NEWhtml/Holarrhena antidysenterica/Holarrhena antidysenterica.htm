Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
A genus of shrubs and trees found in the tropics and subtropics. This species occurs almost throughout India upto an altitude of 4000 ft., often gregariously in deciduous forests and open waste lands it is especially abundant in the sub-Himalayan tract. The species is important in reclothing waste lands. It is one of the first to come up and is the last to disappear in denuded forests. It acts as a nurse to more valuable species, especially sal seedlings, in forests. In its natural habitat, the maximum shade temperature varies from 105 to 118° F and the minimum from 30 to 550 F. The normal annual rainfall varies from 30 to 150 in. Though sensitive to frost, the tree recovers easily from damage. It can stand slight shade hut develops best in full light, and is drought-hardy. The plant coppices well and shoots up readily after severe scorching by fire it produces root-suckers in abundance. Widely distributed throughout greater part Inidian peninsula, down to Malbar and Travancore (1).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A decidious laticiferous shrub or small tree 30-40 ft. high and upto 4 ft in girth, with a clear bole of 10-20 ft. Bark rather rough, pale brownish or greyish, peeling off in irregular flakes, leaves glabrous or pubescent, opposite, entire, base obtuse, subsessile, elliptic or ovate-oblong, 4-12 in. x 2-5 in., main lateral nerves 10-14 pair, membranous. Wood white, soft, even grained. Follicles divaricate, cylindric, 6-18 in. long and 0.2-0-4 inch diam, usually white spotted.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Flowers are white, indorous, 1-1.25 inches across, on slender pedicels which are arranged terminal corymbose cymes with small lanceolate ciliate bracts. Corolla tube slender many times longer than calyx, lobes nearly as long as tube.

Phenology :
The flowering period is somewhat variable, flowering may commence as early as April, but usually takes place in May and June, sometimes continuing until July and August, when flowers may be found along with nearly full-sized unripe fruits (3).

Pollinators:

Seed Biology :
The seeds are 0.5-0.8 in. long, linear, light brown, with a thin, somewhat brittle, closely adhering testa, and crowned at one end with a coma of pale brown silky hairs 1.5-2 in. long, with the aid of which the seeds are distributed to some distance by the Wind (3).

Seedling Germination :
Freshly harvested seeds have higher germination percentage. Seed viabilty lasts upto one year. Seedlings attain a height of 4-6 in. by the end of the first year. Epigeous. The radicle emerges from the end of the seed: the hypocotyl arches slightly, soon straightening and carrying above ground the cotyledons with the testa usually enclosing their extremities. The cotyledons, which are convolute in the seed, unroll themselves and expand and the testa then falls to the ground, often adhering for sometime to one of them before falling (3).

Cultivation:

Natural reproduction is abundant owing to regular and copious seeding from an early age, comparative immunity of the plant from damage by animals and its power of recovery from injury of all kinds. Seeds germinate during the early rains and seedlings attain a height of 4-6 in. by the end of the first year. Weeding and watering stimulate growth. In subsequent years growth is more rapid, the mean annual girth increment being 0.78-0.9 in. Artificial reproduction can be secured both by direct sowing and by transplanting. Fresh seeds have a high percentage of germination but in seeds more than a year old the viability is low. The species is successfully grown by line sowing with field crops. Weeding, periodic thinning and loosening of soil at intervals are beneficial.


A protocol for large-scale propagation of H. antidysenterica through in vitro culture was developed. Explants of H. antidysenterica were cut into small pieces (2cm) and kept in MS medium supplemented with different concentrations and combinations of 6-benzylaminopurine [benzyladenine] (BAP, 1.0-3.0 mg/litre), kinetin (0.2-1.0 mg/litre) and NAA (0.2-1.0 mg/litre) for shoot regeneration, and half-strength MS medium supplemented with NAA, IAA and IBA, each at 0.5 mg/litre, was used for root induction. Urea (75-150 mg/litre) was used for shoot multiplication. Shoot tip and nodal segment explants when cultured on MS medium containing BAP with NAA and BAP with kinetin produced multiple shoots. Maximum multiple shoots was found in MS medium supplemented with BAP (2.0 mg/litre) and NAA (0.5 mg/litre). Subculture on the same medium resulted in rapid shoot multiplication at an average of 16 new shoots per subculture. Addition of urea (100 mg/litre) in the medium increased the number of shoots up to 22 per culture. For best rooting, the shoots were excised from the culture flask and implanted individually on half-strength MS medium with IBA, IAA and IBA. After 20 days of transfer on root induction medium, 95% rooting was achieved. Regenerated plantlets were successfully acclimatized and established in soil. About 90% of plantlets survived under open field conditions (1, 10, 15).

Economic Potential and Uses

The seed yield 19-30 % of a greenish yellow drying oil, with penetrating odour and mild taste. The oil has been used as an anthelmintic.

Parts used

Bark, seeds, leaves and wood

Back to Top

 

Uses

The floss from the seeds is used for stuffing pillows. A dye similar to indigo is extracted from the leaves. The flowers are eaten and also employed in blood diseases. The stem and root barks are medicinal and have long been used in India in the treatment of dysentery. The root bark is similar to stem bark, but of a deeper and more rusty colour. The plant possess antibacterial properties. The wood is used for making small articles, such as combs, picture frames, carved boxes, toys, spoons, cups and platters, bobbins, bedstead legs, paper knives, walking sticks, hookah stems, ploughs, beads and sometimes for furniture. It is a first class timber for slate frames. It is also suitable for pen holders, mathematical instruments, brush backs and spools and pirns for silks textiles (1, 8, 10, 13).

Chemistry

Around 30 alkaloids have been isolated from the plant, mostly from the bark and seed. These include conessine, kurchine, kurchicine, holarrhimine, conarrhimine, conaine, conessimine, iso-conessimine, conimine, holacetin and conkurchin. In addition to the alkaloids, kurchi contains: gum, 9.56; resin, 0.2; and tannin, 1.14%. A triterpene alcohol, lupeol (C31H50O; m.p., 213-14°) and â- sitosterol have been isolated from the unsaponifiable matter of the bark. Kurchi gum is rust brown in colour, bitter in taste A new sterol, named holarrhenosterol, isolated from the seeds along with beta-sitosterol (1, 2, 11, 14).

Processing Techniques

The processing of seeds is carried out in bullock-driven wooden ghanis (kohlus) and powder-driven ghanis (rotary mills). The ghani (kohlu) is devised on a pestle and mortar rotating system driven by a pair of bullocks. In semi-urban areas, where electricity is available, most of the bullock-driven ghanis have been replaced by motorized rotaries for crushing seeds. These rotaries take less space than the ghanis.

Other Uses
The bark and seeds are bitter, constipating, astringent, powerful antidysenteric, acrid, refrigerant, anthelmintic, antiperiodic, aphrodisiac, canninative, digestive, expectorant, febrifuge and tonic. Both are useful in amoebic dysentery, diarrhoea, asthma, bronchopneumonia, hepatic and gastric disorder, flatulence, hepato-splenomegaly, internal haemorrhages, bleeding piles, rheumatism, fever, malaria, vomiting, worm infestation, urological disorders (calculus and gravels), wounds, leprosy and skin diseases. The bark is rubbed over body in dropsy. Seeds are canninative, astringent, lithontriptic and aphrodisiac. The leaves are used in chronic bronchitis, and for boils and ulcers. In Bihar, the roots and leaves are used in dysentery. A powder prepared from the roots and leaves is administered to stop haemorrhages after child birth and bleeding from the nose. Conessine from the bark killed free living amoebae and also kills Entamoeba histolytica in the dysenteric stools of experimentally infected kittens. It is markedly lethal to the flagellate protozoan. It is antitubercular also. Conkurchine is hydrochloride hypotensive and vasodilator (1, 10).

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

Holarrhena antidysenterica is known to the western world as an Indian medicinal plant since 1563, when Garcia de Orta, a Portuguese pharmacist visited the Malabar coast and recognised its merit for inclusion in his Materia Medica. This species is one of the most celebrated and authenticated among the Indian medicinal plants, occurs almost throughout India. Over exploitation has made it rather rare. As the name of the species indicates, its chief use is against dysentery. In dropsy the bark is ground and rubbed over the body. The bark has astringent, ansti-dysenteric, anthelminthic, stomachic, febrifugal and tonic properties. The alkaloids conessine, kurchine, kusrchicine are the principle. Holarrhimine, conarrhimine, a base, conamine, conessimine, isoconessimine, and conimine also were found in the bark. The alkaloids are toxic in larger doses. The seeds contain a fatty oil with anthelminthic properties (12).
