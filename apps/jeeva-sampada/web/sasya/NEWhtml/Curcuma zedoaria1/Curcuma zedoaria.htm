HABIT & HABITAT
A species growing wild in eastern Himalayas and in the moist deciduous forests of the coastal tract of Kanara. It is a native of north east India and is widely cultivated in many parts of India, Ceylon and China.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
  Curcuma zedoaria closely resembles Curcuma longa in appearance. The plant grows to a height of about 1 1/2 ft. and bears green leaves with brownish purple veins. The round zedoary is greyish white, externally heavy, compact; grey and often horny internally, having a bitter taste.

Cultivation:
The plant is propagated by tubers. The rhizomes are cut into small pieces bearing buds, and planted in raked soil at the beginning of the monsoon. Areca nut plantations which provide shady conditions, and banks of irrigation channels, afford congenial conditions for the cultivation of Curcuma zedoaria. A two-year rotation provides sufficient time for the full development of the rhizomes.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Tubers, Rhizomes.

Uses

  1. The tubers are rich in starch. The Shoti Starch of commerce is a product extracted from the tubers and used as a substitute for arrowroot and barley. It is highly valued as an article of diet, especially for infants and convalescents.
  2. The rhizomes posses aromatic, stimulant and carminative properties. Employed in native practice as a stomachic and also applied to bruises and sprains.
  3. The decoction is given with long pepper, cinnamon and honey in colds and the pounded root is applied as a paste to the body.

Harvesting Practices
Whole plant is uprooted by crow bar to recover rhizomes and tubers.

Produce and Products
Shoti starch, Abir,
The rhizomes are large and fleshy. They are cut into thin transverse sections and dried. Dried slices, usually of a greyish buff colour and possessing an agreeable musky odour with a camphoraceous note, form the article of commerce. They have a pungent and somewhat bitter taste.

Back to Top

Chemistry:
Steam distillation of the rhizomes yields (1-2%) a light yellow oil. A sample of commercial oil from the Kerala Soap Institute, Calicut, had: It contained: d- a-pinene, 1.5; d-camphene, 3.5; cineol, 9.6; d- camphor, 4.2; d-borneol, 1.5; sesquiterpenes, 10; sesquiterpene alcohols. The odoriferous constituent is said to be a sesqui-1 terpene alcohol belonging to the tricyclic group (Finnemore, 184).

Back to Top

Processing Techniques
For preparation of shoti starch, the rhizomes are washed, cleaned and made into paste in a grinding stone. The paste is stirred up with water and the starch is allowed to settle. The supernatant liquid is decanted, the residue again stirred up with water, strained through muslin and the starch allowed to settle. The process is repeated several times until the bitter taste is removed and a white product is obtained. The flour is finally dried in the sun. (Das Gupta and Subrahmanyan, 1934 and Dhaneshwar, loc. cit.)

Back to Top

Other Uses

  1. It is cooling and demulcent, and is often made into confections. A red powder, Abir, is prepared from powdered rhizomes by treatment with a decoction of sappan wood.
  2. The rhizome possesses stimulant and carminative properties. A decoction of the rhizome administered along with long pepper, cinnamon and honey, is beneficial for colds. In indigenous medicine, it is prescribed as a stomachic. It has a local effect on the digestive organs similar to but milder than, ginger and has been occasionally employed as a gastro-intestinal stimulant in flatulent colic (U.S.D., 1652). Zedoary is used in the manufacture of liqueurs, stomach essences and bitters and in perfumery and cosmetics.
