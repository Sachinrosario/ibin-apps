Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:

Middle sized evergreen tree. Occurs in moist and evergreen forest. In sub-Himalayan tracts from Nepal to Arunachal Pradesh, Bangladesh, Myanmar and Sri Lanka. In Peninsular India in Maharashtra, Mysore, Kerala, Andhra Pradesh, Orissa, Bihar and Madhya Pradesh. Commonly seen along the banks of forest streams. Thrives best in damp situations in deep fertile soil.

Conservation/ Threat Status : Wild/ naturalised/ cultivated

Lower risk - least concern

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Tree with straight trunk like a sculptured female figure. Bark reddish brown peeling off in papery flakes. Branches spreading, forming a round and shady head. Leaves fascicled at the ends of the branches, oblong-lanceolate, acuminate, sharply serrate, the nerves close, running into the serraturres, not forking at the margins. Leaves characterized by parallel lateral nerves which give s them a beautiful fluted surface. Upper surface and the nerves beneath more or less pubescent; petioles channelled, sheathing.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Flower often exceeding 6" in diameter, white, fragrant, appearing with the leaves, usually solitary towards the end of each branchlet; pedicels clavate, concave, round, smooth, thick and fleshy. Sepals orbicular, concave, thick and fleshy. Petals 5, oblong, obovate, large. Stamens many, the inner larger and arching over the shorter outer ones, forming a yellow crown around the white spreading rays of stigma. Fruit always green, large, 3-4" in diameter, hard outside, fleshy within, consisting of 5 closely-fitting sepals enclosing numerous kidney-shaped seeds, embedded in glutinous pulp, compressed, with hairy margins.

Breeding system and varietal improvement:

Phenology:Normally evergreen almost leafless in Dehra Dun briefly in June. New leaves appear in July. Flowers July to August. Fruit ripens in October and continue to fall to the ground during winter and summer.


Pollinators: Fruits are dispersed by wild elephants, more often by water and are carried away to germinate on banks of streams. The fallen fruits quickly decay, white ants eat the pulp, filling up the shell with earth where the seeds germinate.

Floral and seed biology:

Seedling germination: Propagated from seeds sown during the monsoon.

Tree reproduces in damp situations in deep fertile soil. The tree reproduces satisfactorily from coppice shoots. Seed dispersal under natural conditions is brought about by wild elephants which eat the fruits and eject the seeds by forest streams which carry down, until stranded, the buoyant fruits which fall into them. On reaching the ground, the fruits dry up and decay, the fleshy portions are eaten away by white ants and the seeds, which remain unaffected, germinate at the commencement of rains. The seedlings are washed away by rains. The seedlings are washed away by rains and take root wherever they gain the footing.

For artificial reproduction, the seeds are sown about May in boxes or nursery beds, or seedlings which develop from fruits falling to the ground are collected and planted in nursery beds. Seedlings are transplanting at the commencement of first rains when they are one year old or 3-4 in. high.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts Used:

alorific value of the sapwood 5,226 cal., 9,408 B.t.u.; heartwood 5,277 cal., 9,499 B.t.u. Used as firewood. Used for making charcoal.

Wood: Sapwood yellowish-brown gradually merging into light brown heartwood. Moderately hard to hard. Moderately heavy to heavy. Average unit weight at 12 per cent moisture content 640 kg/m3. Specific gravity 0.58 – 0.82 air dry, 40 lb./ c ft. Usually twisted or interlocked-grained. Coarse and even textured.

Veneer of the timber is attractively figured and is used in paneling.

Timber is used in the form of planks and rafters for internal work. It is also used for making tool handles, gun-stocks and bottoms of boats. It may be used for oars and telegraph posts. Tests on the suitability of the wood for use as railway sleepers showed that after treatment with preservatives a majority of the sleepers remain in good condition up to 9 years.

Selected quarter sown planks are suitable for high class cabinet work.

Processing Techniques:

Wood moderately refractory to air seasoning. Slight tendency to split at the ends and warp. Kiln seasoning offers no difficulties. Timber comes out in excellent condition provided that it is well sawn and carefully piled. Also seasons well when cut from girdled logs.


Other uses:

Thick sepals have an agreeable acid flavor and eaten by the natives. Thick sepals have the flavour of sour unripe apples. They are more agreeable when stewed with sugar and have the flavour of apples stewed in the same manner. They are often mixed in curries in Bengal, especially prawn curries, to which they impart agreeable flavour. When added to syrup, used as a cough mixture.

Fruits are relished by wild elephants (hence the popular name Elephant Apple). Ripe fruits used as flavouring in curries or made into jams and jellies. The acid juice sweetened with sugar used as a cooling drink.

Fruit is said to possess tonic and laxative properties, and is used for abdomminal pains. Bark and leaves are astringent.

Bark used in tanning.

Green leaves used as feed for tasar and Atlas moth silk worms.

Dried leaves used as a substitute for sandpaper in polishing ivory and horn.

Popular ornamental tree for parks.

It is grown in the gardens for its handsome foliage and attractive flowers.

Fruits - Fruits are edible, also used in shampoo.
Flowers - Fleshy petals are eaten as vegetable.

Miscellaneous Information

 The genus was named by Linnaeus to commemorate Johann Jacob Dillenius (1648-1747), a German botanist and Professor of Botany at Oxford; ‘ Dillenia has all plants the showiest flower; Dillenius is likewise conspicuous among botanists’. Linnaeus, Critica Bot., 80: 1737. Cultivated ornamental.
