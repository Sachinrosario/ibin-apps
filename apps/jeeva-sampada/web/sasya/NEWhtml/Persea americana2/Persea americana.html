Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

The avocado may have originated in southern Mexico but was cultivated from the Rio Grande to central Peru long before the arrival of Europeans. Thereafter, it was carried not only to the West, but to nearly all parts of the tropical and subtropical world with suitable environmental conditions (3). It reached India in 1892 and is grown for sweet fruits especially around Madras, Bangalore, Nandi hills, Courtallam, Nagercoil, Shevaroys, lower Palnis and the foothills of the Nilgiris, it has also been grown in Poona. Now it is distributed in larger part of Maharashtra and South India. Avocados are almost evergreen, shed leaves briefly in dry seasons and at blooming time. There are three races of avocado grown: (1) the West Indian, (2) the Guatemalan, and (3) the Mexican, distinguished on the basis of the size and nature of the fruit, oil content of the pulp and anise-like odour present in their bodies. All the superior cultivated types of both the West Indian and Guatemalan races are considered to be of P. americana Mill. while the small fruited Mexican types are assigned to P. americana var. drymifolia Blake. The avocado tree is remarkably versatile as to soil adaptability, doing well on such diverse types as red clay, sand, volcanic loam, lateritic soils, or limestone. The desirable pH level is generally considered to be between 6 and 7. The tree's primary requirement is good drainage. It cannot with stand excessive soil moisture or even temporary water logging (1, 6, 7).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
An evergreen tree may be erect, usually to 30 ft (9 m) but sometimes to 60 ft (18 m) or more, with a trunk 12 to 24 in (30-60 cm) in diameter, (greater in very old trees) or it may be short and spreading with branches beginning close to the ground. Shallow-rooted and there are no visible root hairs. Growth is in flushes. The leaves are alternate, spirally arranged, dark-green and glossy on the upper surface, whitish on the underside, variable in shape (lanceolate, elliptic, oval, ovate or obovate), 3 to 16 inches (7.5-40 cm) long. Petioles 1.5-5 cm long, lamina elliptic, glaucous beneath, pinnatinerved. The fruit, pear-shaped, often more or less necked, oval or spherical or nearly round, may be 3 to 13 inches (7.5-33 cm) long and up to 6 inch (15 cm) wide. Yellow-green to maroon and purple, skin thin or woody, pulp when ripe, has the consistency of firm butter, creamy to bright yellow in colour, of a delicious nutty flavour (3, 5).

Small, pale-green or yellow-green flowers are borne profusely in racemes near the branch tips. They lack petals but have 2 whorls of 3 perianth lobes, more or less pubescent, and 9 stamens with 2 basal orange nectar glands. Each panicle will produce only one to three fruits. Flowers in axillary many-flowered panicles crowded at the ends of the branches, peduncles and pedicels yellowish-green, pubescent, flowers bisexual, fragrant, subtended by lanceolate deciduous brown hairy bracts, 4 mm long, calyx of 6 sepals in 2 whorls, 1.0--1.5 cm in diameter, yellowish, densely tomentose within and without, petals absent, stamens 9 in 3 whorls, those of inner whorl are longer and each has 2 orange nectaries at the base. Staminodes 3 in innermost whorl, filaments hirsute, anthers dehisce by 4 small valves or flaps hinged at upper end, ovary one celled with slender hirsute style and simple stigma (5, 9).

The single seed is oblate, round, conical or ovoid, 2 to 2.5 in (5-6.4 cm) long, hard and heavy, ivory in colour but enclosed in two brown, thin, papery seedcoats often adhering to the flesh cavity, while the seed slips out readily (9). Seeds large, globose with 2 large fleshy cotyledons, whitish or pink in colour, enclosing a small embryo.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Breeding System :
Avocados are protogynous and exhibit synchronous dichogamy in which the flowers open on the first day when the pistil is receptive and then close and open again on the second day when the pollen is shed and the pistil is no longer receptive. The flowers are perfect, but are either receptive to pollen in the morning and shed pollen the following afternoon (type A), or are receptive to pollen in the afternoon, and shed pollen the following morning (type B). About 5% of flowers are defective in form and sterile. Production is best with cross-pollination between types A and B (2, 9).

Phenology :
The trees flower in March-April and occasionally produces a second blossom in November-December. The avocado comes to bearing about 5 years after planting and continues to fruit for about 25 years.

Pollinators:
The flowers are entomophilous. The flowers attract bees and hoverflies and pollination is usually good except during cool weather. Bees being the principal pollinating agents.

Seedling Germination :
Under normal conditions the seeds are viable for 2-3 weeks after removal from the fruits. Some seeds can be stored up to 5 months if placed in non-perforated polyethylene bags and kept at 40ºF (4.4ºC). Fresh seeds germinate in 4 to 6 weeks. Quicker and better germination is obtained if the seed coats are removed. By splitting the cotyledons and leaving meristemic tissue on each section, several clonal seedlings can be produced from one seed. Germination is hypogeal (5).

Cultivation:

For raising seedlings, the seed should be selected from fully mature fruits of healthy vigorous trees and planted as soon as possible after removal from the fruits. Seedlings raised in pots are transplanted in the orchards when they are 20-30 cm high. Although seedlings grow rapidly and vigorously, the fruits produced by them are not exactly of the same quality and shape as of the parent trees. Vegetative propagation is preferable, using the seedlings as rootstocks for budding or grafting. Shield budding and Inarching on seedling rootstocks has been recommended in some parts.

Avocado is usually planted in pits, 36 cm. x 36 cm x 36 cm., the distance between trees varying from 8 to 10 m. depending upon their vigour. The young trees need support by staking against damage by winds. No pruning is needed for at least 7-10 years after planting, except removal of some of the basal branches, when they get shaded by the upper ones. Avocado needs irrigation at intervals of 6-10 days according to the availability of moisture in the soil and the intensity of heat. Manuring of 10 year old trees with 45 kg of farmyard manure, 4.5 kg. of castor cake and 0.9 kg of superphosphate two months prior to flowering has given good results (1,6, 9).

About a dozen varieties are grown in different states. Pollock, Paradeniya Purple Hybrid and Feurite were introduced from Ceylon and have established themselves successfully at the foot of the Nilgiris.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

Avocados have no major insect enemies, but migrating cedar waxwings feed on leaves, flowers and very young fruits and the fruits are commonly attacked by squirrels, rats and mice. The avocado red mite, Oligonychus yothersi is the most common predator on the leaves in some groves and not in others. Red-banded thrips, Selenothrips rubrocinctus, the greenhouse thrips, Heliothrips haemorrhoidalis, and red-spider, Tetranychus mytilaspidis, may feed on avocado leaves and blemish the fruits from time to time. There are several scales also which may feed on foliage, especially the Florida wax scale, Ceroplastes floridensis, the pyriform, or soft white, scale, Protopulvinaria pyriformis, Dictyospermum scale, Chrysomphalus dictyospermi; and the black scale, Saissetia oleae.

Other minor pests are the citrus mealybug, Pseudococcus citri and avocado mealybug, P. nipae. Stinkbugs may prick the fruits leaving little dents in the skin coupled with gritty areas at the same locations inside.

Amorbia cuneana and the omnivorous looper, Sabulodes aegrotata, when present in large numbers, cause severe defoliation and fruit-scarring. Biological control is being achieved by release of the egg parasite, Trichogramma platneri; which is now commercially available to growers. The orange tortrix (a leaf roller), Argyrotaenia citrana, has been an increasing menace to the avocado, the larvae feeds on twigs, terminal buds and foliage, flowers, and fruits.

Other important pests are: fruit-spotting bug, Amblypelta nitida, banana spotting bug, A. Iutescens, Fruit fly, Dacus tryoni; seriously damages only Mexican cultivars or Guatemalan X Mexican hybrids, The avocado weevil, Heilipus lauri; tunnels into the seeds.


Diseases:

Avocado fruits are found to be seriously infected with a fruit spot disease caused by Colletotrichum gloeosporioides. Infection results in shedding of young fruits. The remaining unshed fruits do not attain normal size and become deformed. In some fruits the infection may be latent. The isolated fungus from the fruit doesnot infect the leaves, but there is another strain of fungus which causes leaf spot disease. Controlled atmospheric storage of fruits in 2 per cent O2 and 10 per cent CO2 at 7.20C for 3-4 weeks prevent development of C. gloeosporioides.

Infection of avocado fruits by Fusarium solani and F. sambucinum, causes accelerated softening of fruits. Mushroom root-rot from Clitocybe tabescens may occasionally occur. Cercospora spot (brown spots on the leaves and fruits), caused by the fungus, Cercospora purpurea, may cause cracks in affected areas of the skin and thus allow entrance of the anthracnose fungus, Colletotrichum gloeosporioides, which invades and spoils the flesh. Glomerella cingulata is an important source of anthracnose. Fuerte varieties are found to be susceptible to anthracnose (Glomerella cingulata var. minor) and stem end rot (Dothiorella aromatica) from fruit set until harvest. The time of infection varies with seasons and is related to rainfall incidence. Other diseases of avocadoes are scab (Sphaceloma perseae), which attack the leaves as well as the fruits.

The major disease of avocados in various growing areas, is root-rot caused by the fungus, Phytophthora cinnamomi, which may kill the tree. Root rot is accentuated by poorlydrained soils, which is being combatted by the use of strict sanitary procedures and resistant rootstocks. Also, soil fumigation experiments with methyl bromide and newly developed chemicals are being carried carried out. The disease has been so devastating in the high rainfall area that plantings have expanded into the semi-arid in the hope of avoiding it.

More than 30 other pathogens are responsible for wood rot, collar rot, dieback, leafspot, stem-and rot of fruit, branch canker, and powdery mildew. Sun blotch viroid cripples young trees and damages fruits (3, 9).

Economic Potential and Uses

The pulp has an exceptionally high content of fatty oil (66-84% on dry basis), which resembles other fruit coat fats in composition. A small quantity of avocado oil is produced from culls or damaged fruits in some central American countries. The oil has a heavy consistency, a weak odour and a taste reminiscent of hazelnuts. Avocado oil commands high price and is valued for use in cosmetic preparations and has a digestibility coefficient of 93.8% but has remained too costly to be utilized extensively as salad oil. It is used in superior quality soaps and in pharmaceuticals (1). The oil is also used as hair-dressing and is employed in making facial creams, hand lotions. It is said to filter out the tanning rays of the sun, is non-allergenic and is similar to lanolin in its penetrating and skinsoftening action. The meal left after the extraction of oil from the pulp can be used as an animal feed or as manure (3, 10).

Uses

The fruit is eaten as salad flavoured with pepper, salt or sugar. It is also used in ice-cream. As compared to most other fruits, avocado fruit is highly nutritious being rich in fat and containing appreciable amounts of protein, minerals and vitamins(1). In some places Avocado slices have been pickled and marketed in glass jars. The leaves and bark contains 0.5, 3.5 % of essential oil respectively. Methyl chavicol is the chief constituent of the oil. The seed yields a milky fluid with the odour and taste of almond. Because of its tannin content, it turns red on exposure, providing an indelible red-brown or blackish ink which was used to write many documents in the days of the Spanish Conquest. The ink has also been used to mark cotton and linen textiles. The wood has been utilized for construction, boards and turnery (3, 10).

Harvesting Practices
The time of harvest depends upon the variety. Commercial standards requires fruit to reach 8% oil content before harvesting. Mexican types ripen in 6 - 8 months from bloom while Guatemalan types usually take 12 - 18 months (2). Generally fruits ripen in August- September and those produced from the second blossom mature in May-June. The long fruited type matures usually a fortnight earlier than the round fruited type. The fruits vary in weight from about 85 g to as much as 1,000 g and above. The avocado fruits do not soften on the tree. A slight change of rind colour to dull yellow in the round fruits and to a darker shade in the long ones is a good indication of the maturity for harvesting. The fruits may be harvested with the aid of a clipper.

The yields vary from 100 to 120 fruits for each tree in South India. In America and elsewhere, an average tree produces about 45-67.5 kg of fruits per season; some trees yield as many as 1,200 fruits (1).

Produce and Products

Fruits are pickled. Oil is extracted from the pulp. Leaves and bark yield essential oil. Blackish ink is obtained from the seeds. Cake after oil exraction is used as manure and livestock feed.

Processing Techniques

After harvesting, the fruit should be allowed to ripen fully to give maximum oil yield. Depending on the method to be used for oil extraction the fruit may then be opened seeds removed and then sun dried. A number of methods have been proposed including mashing the pulp and afterwards boiling skimming off the oil, high pressure pressing of dried slices, solvent extraction, expellers, presses and centrifugal extraction.

Oil is obtained by pressing the dehydrated pulp or by macerating the undried pulp with water and centrifuging the resulting slurry; it can also be obtained by solvent extraction with petroleum ether.

Another proposed method involves rendering; heating the pulp in avocado oil and evaporating off the moisture. The remaining slurry of oil and plant material is then pressed. Small scale trials showed that 57% of the oil could be decanted off after rendering and after subsequent pressing, oil recoveries of 94% were achieved. The addition of chalk, which precipitates the fruit pectins, has been found to improve oil release from the cells.

The crude oil is dark green/brown in colour, red under reflected light due to its high chlorophyll content and may be refined by alkali refining, bleaching, deodorizing and winterising. If very old rotten fruit is used as a raw material the oil may need alkali refining to remove FFA (4).

Other Uses

The fruit skin is antibiotic; is employed as a vermifuge and remedy for dysentery. The leaves are chewed as a remedy for pyorrhea. Leaf poultices are applied on wounds. Heated leaves are applied on the forehead to relieve neuralgia. The leaf juice has antibiotic activity. The aqueous extract of the leaves has a prolonged hypertensive effect. The leaf decoction is taken as a remedy for diarrhea, sore throat and hemorrhage, it allegedly stimulates and regulates menstruation. It is also drunk as a stomachic.

The seed is cut in pieces, roasted and pulverized and given to overcome diarrhea and dysentery. The powdered seed is believed to cure dandruff. A piece of the seed, or a bit of the decoction, put into a tooth cavity may relieve toothache. An ointment made of the pulverized seed is rubbed on the face as a rubefacient to redden the cheeks. An oil extracted from the seed has been applied on skin eruptions (3, 10).

Miscellaneous Information

Related species

Persea schiedeana Nees, called coyo, coyocte, chalte, chinini; chucte, chupte, cotyo, aguacate de monte, aguacaton, wild pear, and yas, grows wild in mountain forests from southern Mexico to Panama at altitudes between 4,600 and 6,200 ft (1,400-1,900 m). The tree is usually from 50 to 65 ft (15-20 m) tall, occasionally to 165 ft (50 m). Young branches are densely brown-hairy. The leaves are deciduous, obovate to oval, often cordate at the base; 5 to 12 in (12.5-30 cm) long, 2 3/4 to 6 in (7-15 cm) wide, white-hairy on the underside. Downy flowers, borne in densely grayish-hairy panicles, are light greenish-yellow, the perianth and stamens turning red with age. The fruit, resembling that of the avocado and equally variable, is generally pear-shaped, weighing 8 to 14 oz (227-397 g), with thick, leathery, flexible skin. Variously described as brownish-white, light-brown, pale-green, greenish-brown or dark-brown, the flesh is oily with a milky juice, few to many coarse fibers, but a very appealing, avocado-coconut flavor. The seed is very large. The cotyledons, unlike those of the avocado, are pink internally.

A more distant relative is Beilschmiedia anay Kosterm. (Huielandia anay Blake), called anay, payta, escalalan or excalan, which is native to moist, relatively low altitudes of 985 to 2,300 ft (300 to 700 m) in southern Mexico, Guatemala, Costa Rica and Colombia.

The tree attains a height of 66 ft (20 m); the young branches are brown-hairy. Leathery leaves, broad-elliptic or broad-ovate, are 4.25  to 12 in (12-30 cm) long and 3 to 7 .5 in (7.5-19 cm) wide, white-hairy only on the veins. The flowers (in December and January) are fragrant, greenish, in slender panicles to 5 in (13 cm) long. The fruit is ellipsoid-pyriform, 2 3/4 to 6 in (7-15 cm) long, with very thin, glossy, purplish-black skin and sparse green, oily flesh similar to that of the avocado in texture and flavor. The seed is obovoid, up to 2 3\4 in long, with thick, purplish-yellow, red spotted coat, and strong almond odour (3).
