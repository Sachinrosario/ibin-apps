Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
A small genus of herbs., variable erect annual, 2-4 ft. high, closely allied to Brassica, distributed in South Europe, North Africa and West Asia. Eruca sativa is grown in north India for its oil-bearing seeds.
The plant is cultivated, mostly as a cold weather crop, in North India and is met with in western Himalayas up to 10,000 ft. it is grown in the highlands of Punjab, in areas where drought conditions prevail; it is also grown to a small extent in Uttar Pradesh and Gwalior.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Annual growing to 0.6m by 0.25m at a fast rate. A variable erect annual, 2-4 ft. high, with fleshy toothed leaves and yellow or lilac, self- sterile flowers. Pods 1 inch in length, erect, numerous, small (200-600 per g.), light reddish brown and ovoid, with a smooth surface, arranged in a double row in each compartment of the fruit.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
The flowers are hermaphrodite (have both male and female organs). Pedicels shorter than the deciduous calyx, petals white or pale yellow, with very dark veins, retuse or somewhat emarginated. and are pollinated by Insects.

Breeding System :
Highly cross-pollinated. When long and short styled plants are crossed, the flowers set seeds freely. Greater pod and seeds formation is also seen when bud pollination is done.

Phenology :
The flowering occurs during May-July.

Pollinators:
It is pollinated by insects.

Seed Biology:
The yellowish brown seeds may be substituted for mustard, but are less pungent.

Seed Germination:
Seed - sow outdoors in spring in situ. Germination is usually very quick and free. In order to obtain a continuous supply of edible leaves, success ional sowings can be made every few weeks until mid August. A late summer/early autumn sowing can provide leaves in winter, though the plants might require some protection in very cold winters.

Cultivation:The plant prefers light (sandy), medium (loamy) and heavy (clay) soils and requires well-drained soil. The plant prefers acid, neutral and basic (alkaline) soils. It can grow in semi-shade (light woodland) or no shade. It requires dry or moist soil and can tolerate drought. The plant can tolerate strong winds but not maritime exposure. Very easily grown and fast-maturing plant, it succeeds in most soils and conditions, though it prefers some shade in summer. Once established, plants are quite drought resistant.

Rocket is occasionally cultivated for its edible leaves and flowers, it can be ready for harvest within 40 days of sowing the seed, there are some named varieties. 'Rocket Improved' is less prone to bolting, though it still grows best in cooler weather. Plants usually self-sown freely if the ground is disturbed in some way, such as by hoeing.

This species is normally untroubled by pests or diseases.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:
Aphids have been reported to attack the plant; they suck the sap from all parts of the plant and sometimes completely destroy the crop

Diseases:
The crop is susceptible to white blister, caused by Cystopus candidus (Pers.) Lev. and downy mildew, caused by Peronosporo parasitica (Pers.)

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Younger plants, Leaves, Flowers and Seeds.

Uses

·       The oil cake is used as cattle feed in U.P. and Punjab. The young plant is used as salad and vegetable.

·       It is also used as green fodder for cattle, sheep and camels.

·       Fresh, dried or fermented plant material is readily eaten by sheep. The coefficient of digestibility of the dried plant is the same as that of good meadow hay.

·        It is used as a salad oil in Southern Europe.

·       Young leaves - raw or cooked. A distinct strongly spicy flavour, the taste is best from fast, well-grown plants. A few leaves added to a salad are acceptable though the flavour is too strong for many tastes. Some people really like these leaves though most are not very keen.

·        Older leaves that have become too hot to eat on their own can be pureed and added to soups etc.

·       In the milder areas of Britain it is possible to produce edible leaves all year round from successional sowings, especially if the winter crop is given some protection.

·       Flowers - raw. A similar taste to the leaves, they make a nice garnish on the salad bowl.

·       The seed yields a semi-drying oil which is edible if stored 6 months and is a substitute for rapeseed oil. It contains 32% fat, 27% protein. It is known as 'jamba oil'.  

Harvesting Practices
It may be cut green and fed to cattle or allowed to grow to maturity and harvested fort he oil-bearing seeds. The yield of seed varies from 4 to 12 md. per acre according as it is grown in admixture with other crops or as a pure crop. Taramira seeds grown in U.P. are bolder than those grown in Punjab.

Produce and Products
Oil

 Other Uses

1.     Taramira seed is used mostly as a substitute for sarson. It is sometimes used as an adulterant for rapeseed, but it can be readily distinguished by its ovate shape and characteristic colour. Adulteration above 3% is not permitted in the trade.

2.     The seed yields semi-drying oil, which is a substitute for rapeseed oil. It can also be used for lighting, burning with very little soot. The seed oil is used for burning, but now also adulterd with 'Sarson ka tel'. T

3.     ender leaves of the plant are considered stimulant, stomachic, diuretic and antiscorbutic.

4.     Seeds are vesicant. Taramird oil is burnt in lamps for illuminating purposes. It is reported to give a more luminous and less sooty flame than brassica oils.

5.     It is also used for massaging the body and as an adulterant for rape oil.

6.     A mustard is obtained from the seed, the strong flavour comes from an essential oil that is contained within the oil of the seed. The pungency of mustard develops when cold water is added to the ground-up seed - an enzyme (myrosin) acts on a glycoside (sinigrin) to produce a sulphur compound. The reaction takes 10 - 15 minutes. Mixing with hot water or vinegar, or adding salt, inhibits the enzyme and produces mild bitter mustard.
