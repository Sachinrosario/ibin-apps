HABIT & HABITAT

A small to medium-sized, deciduous tree, restricted to parts of Andhra  Pradesh, particularly Cuddapah district and neighbouring  areas of Tamil Nadu and Karnataka states at an attitude of 150-900 m.

P. santalinoides is a shade tolerant tree commonly found along riverine forests in Africa and tropical South America.

Altitude: 200-500 m Mean annual temperature: 26 deg C Mean annual rainfall: 1 600 mm Soil type: Prefers well drained soils.

It prefers areas of dry and hot climate, with daily mean temperature ranging from 30 to 40°C with around 100mm of rain in each of the two annual monsoons and relative humidity ranging from 32 to 79 percent.  It grows on rocky soils consisting of quartzite, lime stone and laterite and requires very good drainage otherwise the trees fail to stand stiff.

Availability : 
Endemic to Eastern Ghats of Cudappah, Nellore Chittoor and Prakasam districts of Andhra Pradesh.  Also recorded in the adjoining regions of Karanataka and Tamil Nadu.  In Karnataka, reported from Devarayanadurga, Sandur and Karpakapalli MPCAs.  In Tamil Nadu, reported from the drier zones of Chengalpattu, Salen and Dharmapuri districts.  Commonly planted as monoculture and also an an ornament.

Considerable plantations are now cultivated in certain districts of Madras and to a smaller extent in Bombay and Bengal.  

This is a large forest tree inhabiting Ceylon and the mountains of the opposite Coromandel coast on the Indian continent. Only in the Madras Presidency does it grow wild. The wood is the official Red Saunders, or Red sandal-wood. It is "a hard, heavy, dark reddish-brown, coarsely splintery wood, deprived of the light-colored sap-wood; usually met with in chips, or as a coarse, irregular, brownish-red powder, nearly inodorous and nearly tasteless. Red Saunders does not impart any red color to water when macerated with it"—(U. S. P.).

Native: Brazil, Cameroon, Ghana, Nigeria, Senegal


Status : Critically Endangered (Globally) as per the Red list.

Included under the negative list of exports notified by Govt. of India (Notification 2 (RE-98) dt 13.4.98,1997-2002).  Also included in Appendix-II of CITES .
The felling of trees to obtain the dye-stuff is under strict Government control, and yearly yields a considerable revenue.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Pterocarpus santalinoides is a tree 9-12 m tall, 1 m DBH, with low straggling branches. Bark thin and flaking in small patches, blackish brown, deeply clefted into rectangular plates, and exuding a deep red juice when cut. Leaves usually imparipinnate; leaflets 3, rarely 5, flowers yellow, in simple or sparingly branched recemes; Flowers up to 2 cm across, pedicel up to 5 mm; calyx tomentose, tube 5 mm, lobes ovate; corolla yellow, standard  ovate 2.1x 1.5 cm .Wings 1.8 x  0.8 cm, keels 1.4 x 0.35 cm, staminal sheath 4 x 3 mm; filaments 5 mm; monoadelphous (10) ovary stipitate, 7 mm, style 4 mm; pods ca. 5 cm in diameter including  the wing. The central hard and long portion containing the seed;  seeds reddish brown, smooth, leathery.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Breeding System :
P. santalinoides is monoecious, flowering from December-March, fruits ripening between March-April.


Phenology :

Leaf fall January to march, new leaves: February to March
Flowering from April-May, fruits ripening between May-September.


Pollinators:

Seed Biology :
The dry pods are collected and are dried in sun for 3 days and stored in bamboo baskets or gunny bags till required.  Seeds retain their viability for at least 8 months. 


Seedling Germination :
Natural regenerations through seeds is profuse. Early development of seedlings is favoured by shelter from the sun and a loose soil clear of weeds. Seedlings may show little stem development or may die back annually for several years but ultimately  shoot up after they have developed a long stout tap root. For artificial reproduction , one-year old seedlings are transplanted from nursery into bamboo baskets and then planted.  

Direct seeding, cuttings and rootstocks can be used to propagate P. santalinoides.

The seeds are gathered in May or June, and sown in July in small beds about 8 ft square, within easy reach of water.  They are thrust into the light soil perpendicularly or at an angle, and about an inch deep.  From seven hundred to eight hundred may be put into nursery beds of the above-mentioned dimensions.  The seeds should be watered by hand every second evening.  They germinate, if previously soaked for a night in cold water, in twenty to twenty-five days, if not so treated, in thirty to thirty-five.  After germination, the beds should be moderately watered, particular care in this respect being very necessary during the first six months. Too much water proves equally destructive to giving none at all.  At six months the shoot has attained some height and requires the support of  a forked stick.  The nursery must be kept free of weeds.

When six months' old the plants should be transferred to wicker and bamboo baskets the removal being carefully performed so as not injure the long tap-roots.  These baskets must be placed in a shady place, and watered every second or third day.  When the roots have taken firm hold, the baskets should be buried in pits and watered till the rains set in.  

The seedlings when 4 to 10cm high are transplanted into polythene bags and the bags are kept under shade, duly watered for a month.  Then the shade is removed and watering continued for another 3 to 4 months daily.  At the end of this period, roots develop and are 20 to 30cm long.  These are planted out during rainy season.  

Cuttings are planted in polythene bags with sandy-loam soil mixed well with FYM, watered daily and kept till they are one year old.

Cultivation:
The seedlings when 4 to 10cm high are transplanted into polythene bags and the bags are kept under shade, duly watered for a month.  Then the shade is removed and watering continued for another 3 to 4 months daily.  At the end of this period, roots develop and are 20 to 30cm long.  These are planted out during rainy season.  One year old cuttings are field planted at a spacing of 3.5 to 4.5m in 30cm  cube pits.  Both water logged sites and overshadowing by other trees must be avoided.  One or two hoeings around the plant to a 1m radius and 15cm depth are done in the first two years.

Pollarding, coppicing and lopping are recommended management practices for P. santalinoides.

Uses
Dye;

The dye obtained is known as bright red Santaline dye.  It is extracted from the heart wood of the tree.

It is "a hard, heavy, dark reddish-brown, coarsely splintery wood, deprived of the light-colored sap-wood; usually met with in chips, or as a coarse, irregular, brownish-red powder, nearly inodorous and nearly tasteless. Red Saunders does not impart any red color to water when macerated with it.

The wood contains a 16% red colouring matter called santalin, which is easily dissolved out by means of any alkaline solution and is used as a dye.  

  • In India it appears to be chiefly employed as a stain or pigment for marking idols and the forehead in ceremonies.  
  • It is however, occasionally employed as a red dye in the same way as sappan.  
  • The cloth to be coloured is boiled with the wood, not merely soaked in it, and the tint produced is said to be lasting.  
  • It dissolves in alcohol, ether and essential oil.
  • Other dye-woods generally communicate their color to water, which is not the case with red saunders; the latter, however, imparts to alkaline solutions, ether, and alcohol, a scarlet color.
  • It is employed as a colouring agent in pharmacy, foodstuffs and paper and pulp.  It is also used in dyeing leather, silk and cotton clothes, when mixed with Brazilian dye.
  • Ground wood is chiefly used for dyeing leather, wool cotton, linen and for staining wood.
  • It is used in combination with other dyes to produce beautiful colours.
  • A cloth dyed in a solution containing santaline dye mixed with alcohol or ether results in a beautiful orangish pink colour cloth.

Chemistry
The wood contains a resinoid substance- Santalic acid or Santalin which is readily soluble in ether,alcohol, alkaline solutions or concentrated acetic acid, and is said to form ruby red crystals, devoid of odour and taste.  By the action of ether on the wood a red powder is obtained, having a green hue in reflected light and which when melted with potash, yields Resorcin.

The coloring principle of red Saunders is santalic acid (santalin), discovered by Pelletier. It is a red, tasteless, and odorless, crystalline powder, insoluble in water, soluble in ether, with yellow color, and in alcohol, with blood-red color (L. Meyer. 1848). It likewise dissolves in alkalies and acetic acid, but not in essential oils. H. Weidel (1869) obtained a similar substance, santal (C8H6O3), by extracting the wood with boiling alkaline water, precipitating with hydrochloric acid, and recrystallizing from alcohol. The yield was 0.3 per cent. Cazeneuve and Hugounenq (Jahresb. der Pharm., 1887, p. 159; and 1889, p. 127) digested the powdered wood with milk of lime, extracted the coloring substance with ether, and crystallized from alcohol. Carbon disulphide differentiated the product obtained into insoluble pterocarpin (C20H16O6) and soluble homo-pterocarpin (C24H24O6). Both substances are probably orcin-derivatives. A small amount of tannin is contained in red Saunders.

Other Uses
  1. Wood is immune to white ants and other insects and does not require antiseptic treatment. Wood is highly prized for house posts, agricultural implements, poles, shafts and bent rims of carts,  picture frames, boxes and other joinery purposes. Small pieces are carved into dolls and images. Wood is exported to Japan where it is used for manufacturing of a musical instrument called Shamisen. Wood yields an excellent charcoal. Wood white or yellow, not hard but termite-resistant.
  2. Bark is used in the  curing of areca nuts.
  3. Red Sandal wood is considered astringent, tonic and diaphoretic.
  4. A paste of wood is used in external application for inflammation and headache. It  is said to be useful in bilious affections and skin diseases.
  5. A decoction of the fruit is used as an astringent tonic in chronic dysentery.
  6. The leaves are also eaten as a vegetable.
  7. Fodder: Livestock browse its young shoots and leaves.
  8. Medicine: The tree bark is used as a stomach ache remedy.  It is also used to cure chronic fever, defects of vision, skin diseases, bone fracture, leprosy, spider poisoning, scorpion sting, hiccough, ulcers, general debility and mental aberrations.  Also on psoriasis, chronic dysentery, blood vomiting, controlling haemorrhage and in piles.
  9. Erosion control: An important species for soil conservation in water catchment areas.
  10. Shade or shelter: A good windbreak around settled areas and farms.
  11. Nitrogen fixation: P. santalinoides forms nodules with nitrogenase activity. The nodules are generally spherical but occasionally elongate.
  12. Soil improver: Leaf litter from P. santalinoides on decomposition slowly releases N and significantly increases soil exchangeable Ca and Mg in the soil.
  13. Ornamental: A beautiful tree with good gardening attributes its; showy flowers, beautiful foliage and form make it a suitable ornamental tree.
  14. Boundary or barrier or support: Poles from A. santalinoides are used for fencing.

 Miscellaneous Information

Pterocarpus is based on the Greek words ‘pteran’ meaning a wing and, ‘karpos’ meaning’ fruit. The specific epithet ‘santalinoides’ refers to its likeness to P. santalinus found in Asia.
