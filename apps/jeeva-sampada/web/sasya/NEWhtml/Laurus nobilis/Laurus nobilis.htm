Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT                                                                                                                  

L. nobilis is an evergreen shrub, or more rarely a tree. The bay leaf of commerce is derived from the leaves of Laurus nobilis L. It is traded as sweet bay leaf, true Roman and Turkish laurel. The crop is a native of Mediterranean and grows spontaneously in scrubland and woods in Europe and in California. It is widely cultivated in Europe, America and in Arabian countries from Libya to Morocco (Bailey 1963, Anon. 1962) (1). The English term bay leaf (Middle English baye, Old French baie) derives from Latin baca "berry", meaning originally the fruits (4).

Climate and soil:
Grows well in regions with annual temperature of 8-27°C and annual rainfall of 300-2200 mm. Light frost is tolerated, light and temperature requirements are high. Although well-drained, deep and fertile soils are preferred, growth is satisfactory on a wide range of soils with pH of 4.5-8.3. Water logging is not tolerated.


Availability :

Originated in the eastern Mediterranean and Asia Minor. Grown up and naturalized throughout the drier tropics, subtropics and warm temperate areas. Also cultivated as garden and pot plant worldwide (2). In India, bay is grown in a relatively small scale in homestead gardens (3).
Conservation/ Threat Status : Cultivated, less in wild.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

L. nobilis is an evergreen shrub, or a tree with a height of 15-20 m. In cultivation usually pruned to less than 3 m. The smooth bark is olive green or has reddish-hue. Branchlets terete, young ones dark brown, nearly glabrous or with soft hairs. The luxurious evergreen leaves are alternate with petiole 0.5-1.5 cm long, violet red when fresh, glabrous or with sparse soft hairs, lanceolate or lanceolate oblong, acuminate, 5-8 cm or longer and 3-4 cm wide, coriaceous, pellucid-punctate, and with revolute, entire wavy margins; the upper surface is glabrous and shiny, olive green to brown and lower surface is dull olive to brown with a prominent rib and veins. Leaf has a characteristic fragrance when crushed and tastes bitter and aromatic (2).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:
Inflorescence an axillary, 4-5 flowered umbel, solitary or mostly arranged in a fascicle or short raceme; peduncle 2-12 mm long, glabrous or with soft hairs. Bracts 4, rounded, inner ones 0.7-1.0 mm long, outer ones smaller, coriaceous, glabrous outside, with silky hairs inside; tepals 4, ovate-oblong to rounded, 4.5-6 mm long, obtuse with soft hairs on both sides; flowers are small, yellow in colour, ovoid, coarsely wrinkled and contain a single seed with loose kernel. Male flowers with 8-12 fertile stamens, outer whorl eglandular, second and third whorls with 2 sessile, reniform glands at middle of filaments, anthers ellipsoidal, 2-celled, introrse, pistillode present; female flower with 4 staminodes bearing an appendage at apex of connective, ovary 1-celled, style short, stigma somewhat enlarged (2).

Fruit
The dried fruits are drupaceous, ovoid, about 15 mm long and 10 mm wide. Outer surface is glabrous, shining, dark violet to glossy black at maturity and is coarsely wrinkled owing to the shrinkage of narrow succulent region beneath the epidermis. Remains of the style appear as small point at the apex and a small scar at the base marks the point of attachment of the fruit to the thalamus. Endocarp is thin and woody and the testa is adherent to its inner surface. The entire pericarp is about 0.5 mm thick. Kernel of the seed consists of two large plano-convex cotyledons and small superior radicle; it is brownish-yellow, starchy and oleaginous, with an aromatic odour and aromatic and bitter taste (Bailey 1963; Wallis 1960; Francesco and Francesco 1971) (1).

CULTIVATION ASPECT:

Related taxa:
There are many ornamental cultivars like 'Aurea' - the golden or variegated laurel, 'Crispa' - with undulating leaf margins, 'Angustifolia' - with narrow leaves (2).

Chromosome number:
2n=48 (most common in a ployploid series of 36, 42, 48, 54, 60, 66, 72).

Selection of planting material:
Propagated by seeds or preferably by cuttings.

Planting:
In some areas seeds are better than stem cuttings. Seeds are soaked in warm water for 12-24 hours before sowing; only fresh seeds are to be used as viability is lost very quickly. Shading and mulching seedbeds result in faster germination and early seeding growth (2).

From a mature stem, cuttings of about 7.5 to 10 cm length are put in sharp sand either under bell-glasses or in glass cases. Rooted cuttings are placed in small pots containing fairly rich sandy loam with good drainage, and then put in a hot bed, with gentle bottom heat where they will make a good strong growth. Spacing recommended is initially 3 m x 3 m and later thinned to 6 m x 6 m. Stem cuttings produce better roots in July/August. Ligneous, subapical stem cuttings of bay laurel have higher rooting percentage than herbaceous apical cuttings (Raviv 1983 a,b). Efficient and rapid rooting occurs at a temperature of 20°C to 30°C, especially during winter (1).

Management after planting:
Then they may be planted in nursery beds with rich sandy soil and good drainage. In one growing season the plants may attain a height of 1 to 1.5 m. At the end of growing season and long before the cold season the young plants together with their stakes are kept in a well lit ventilated sheds, and temperature is kept just above freezing. These plants are kept in close rows and watered once or twice a week. They are then taken out in spring and either potted or plunged in nursery. Rich peaty soil with plenty of water near the seacoast is congenial for the luxuriant growth (Bailey 1963). It also grows well under partly shaded conditions in gardens and orchards (1). Crop can be intercropped with annuals during the first 2-4 years.


MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
Not common. Scale insects and psyllids cause some damage in Italy.


Diseases:

Less, but more important and widespread than pests.
Root rot - caused by Phytophthora spp.
Leaf spot - caused by Colletotrichum spp.
Wilting, yellowing of leaves and dieback of twigs - caused by Phytophthora cinnamomi.
Brown spots on leaves by Colletotrichum nobile.

Control: affected plants must uprooted, burned and replaced by healthy stocks (2).

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Dried leaves, called bay leaves, are used as a flavouring material in a variety of culinary preparation especially French cuisine. Essential oil is used for flavouring all kinds of food products, particularly meats, sausages, canned soups, baked goods, confectionery etc (1).Leaves are applied as seasoning, imparting and excellent fragrant aromatic flavour with notes of lemon and clove to meat, game, poultry and fish dishes, stews, syrups, sauces and stock. Leaves are to be added early in cooking as taste gradually intensifies. They should be removed before serving as they remain fibrous and their sharp edges can cause internal injuries. Crushed or powdered leaves are essential ingredients of mixed pickling spices and are used industrially in meat products, sauces, vinegar and pastries. In Italy and Turkey leaves are used for packaging liquorice. Leaves are also placed in containers of cereal products to repel insects. Leaf oil used in perfumery to provide a masculine note. Used especially in bath products. Berries to little extent are also used for culinary purposes. Laurel can be sheared into various shapes making it a popular container and garden plant. In South-East Asia the plant is planted only as an ornamental, mainly at higher elevations in highlands. Laurel wood is used occasionally used for posts. Flexible twigs were formerly used to make hoops for small barrels (2). Bay oil is popular in a variety of liquors (3).

Parts Used:

Dried leaves

Harvesting Practices:
Leaves are harvested in summer or autumn in temperate regions. Up to 3 harvests can be made annually. Two harvests give better yield. Harvesting is done manually early in the day. In Russia, mechanical harvesting is done. Yield: strongly affected by frequency of harvesting and irrigation. Rainfed trees harvested twice a year yields 5 t/ha per year of dried leaves (2).

Post Harvest Handling:
After harvesting leaves are dried under shade in thin layers in trays for 12-15 days. Leaves are pressed lightly under boards to prevent curling. Steam distillation is done for obtaining leaf oil from leaves and essential oil from berries (2). The greener the colour, the better the quality. Bay leave cannot, however, be stored as long as their tough texture might suggest, but should not be kept more than one year after plucking. Overaged leaves have lost their fragrance, show a brownish hue and taste mostly bitter (4).

Produce and Products:
Laurel leaves
Essential oil: The leaves contain essential oil of aromatic, spicy odour and flavour which can be isolated by steam distillation. It replaces the dried leaves to great advantage as it can be dosed more exactly, thereby giving uniform results than the dried leaves (Guenther 1953). Berries contain 1% aromatic oil and 25-30% fat (which is dull green, granular and has aroma) (1).
Medicinal uses
Used traditionally for treatment of a number of diseases. The essential oil has antimicrobial, antifungal, hypoglycaemic (for diabetes) and antiulcerogenic properties. Infusions and decoctions made from bay leaves are diaphoretic, powerful emmenagogue, treating problems of urinary organs and dropsy, have carminative, narcotic effects and serve as general gastric secretion stimulant. The oil or butter from fruits is a vital ingredient of laurin liniment, a popular medicine for rheumatism, gout, spleen and liver diseases. It also finds application in veterinary medicine (Anon. 1962; Duke 1989; Wren 1975; Francesco and Francesco 1971).

Oil is effective against Staphylococcus aureus, E. coli, Shigella flexnerii, Salmonella typhi and pathogens of the intestinal tract (Syed et al. 1991). Bay leaf potentiated the action of insulin in glucose metabolism (Khan et al. 1990) and reduced glucose transport (Gurman et al. 1992). Cockroach repellant activity has been reported in bay leaf (Verma and Meloan 1981). The bay also has antioxidant properties (Lagouri and Bouskou 1995) (1).

Chemistry:
Leaf of laurel has a pleasant odour and its taste is characteristically strong, pungent and aromatic. Bitterness of leaves is removed by drying. Essential oil from leaves comprises over 180 components with 1,8-cineole predominating (up to 50%). Catechin content of leaves is high and preserved by drying (2). Presence of 1,8-cineole in appreciable amounts makes the oil important in perfumery. Various chemical constituents in bay leaf are: a-pinene, eugenol, phellandrene, 1,8-cineole, lauric acid, alcohols, sesquiterpenes, esters (Mecinnamate - 17.9%), free cinnamic acid (1.3%), free phenols (2.0%), terpene hydrocarbons (15.4%), and different carbonyl compounds, citral terpineol, caryophyllene, camphene, sabinene, limonene, careen, ß-pinene, a-terpinene, myrcene, p-cymene, terpinolene, ocimene, ß-selinene, ? and d-cadinene, linalool, methyl eugenol, p-cymol, terpenyl formate etc. The shoots, in addition to some of the above, contained acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid, caproic acid, caprylic acid, pelargonic acid, enanthric acid etc. The flowers contained (E)-ocimene, ß-caryophyllene, viridioflorene, ß-clemene, germacrene-D-4-ol and germacrene-D (Rattu and Maccioni 1952; Rattu et al. 1953; Nigam et al. 1958; Teisserie 1966; Florini et al. 1997). Chemical composition of flower essential oil is quite different from the leaf, stem bark and stem wood of the plant. Fruits in Indian regions contained 1,8-cineole (28.4%), methyl cinnamate (20.1%), a-phellandrene (10.1%), a-pinene (9.3%), a-terpenol (5.8%), sabine (4.9%), a-thujene (3.8%), ß-humulene (3.3%), linalool (2.3%), camphor (2.2%) and a-gurujunene (2.2%) (Nigam et al. 1992; Appendino et al. 1992). Cis-thujzen-4-ol, pinocarvone and (E)-pinocarveol are new compounds extracted from leaves in Turkey and Greece (Novak et al. 1985; Tucker et al. 1992) (1). Seeds contain about 25% fatty oil comprising at least 20 fatty acids with lauric acid (11-52%), palmitic acid (5-17%), oleic acid (15-40%) and linoleic acid (17-23%) as main constituents (2).

Per 100 g of leaves contain approx.: water - 4.5-10.0 g, protein - 7.6-10.6 g, fat - 4.5-8.8 g, carbohydrates - 65 g, ash - 3.7-4.2 g (Ca - 1 g, Fe - 530 mg, P - 110 mg, Na - 20 mg, K - 530 mg, Zn - 40 mg) ascorbic acid - 47 mg, thiamine - 0.10 mg, riboflavin - 0.42 mg, niacin - 2 mg and vitamin A - 6185 IU. Energy value is 1725 kJ/100 g. In US the regulatory status 'generally regarded as safe' (GRAS) has been accorded to laurel leaves (GRAS 2124), laurel leaf oil (GRAS 2125) and laurel leaf oleoresin (GRAS 2613) (2).

Economic potential to the country

World trade in dried leaves is over 2000 t/year. Grown in India in small-scale and as a homestead.

Export potential:

Turkey is the major producer and exporter. It is marketed mainly in Europe (largely, Germany and The Netherlands) (2).
