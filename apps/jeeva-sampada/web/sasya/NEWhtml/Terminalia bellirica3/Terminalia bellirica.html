Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Terminalia bellirica is a large deciduous tree common throughout the greater part of India except arid regions in westren Rajasthan, Punjab and Haryana. It grows scattered in the sub-Himalayan tract while in the Indian peninsular it grows mainly in moist valleys. In its natural habitat the obsolute maximum shade temperature varies from 360 to 460C, the obsolute minimum temperature from -10 to 150C and the normal annual rainfall from about 1, 000 to 3, 000 mm. It is capable of growing on various types of soils provided the moisture supply is adequate. The best growth is attained on deep sandy loam soil with good drainage. It is also found in drier zones, but requires watering for establishment (7, 8, 9, 10, 11).

Status : Wild

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A large, round crowned, handsome deciduous tree attaining a height of 30-40 m with straight tall bole, buttressed at base. The bark is bluish or ashy-grey covered with numerous fine longitudinal cracks. Leaves large, alternate, broadly elliptic, 10-20 cm long, clustered at the end of branchlets, Leaves are long petioled , obovate, glabrous, spikes axillary, solitary, almost as long as the leaves. Fruits sub-globose, very faintly five ridged when dry, minutely brown tomentosa. It is a fleshy drupe 2.5-4 cm long with a hard, thick walled and woody (2, 5, 6, 7, 11, 12).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Flowers are solitary, simple, sessile dirty grey to greenish yellow in colour on axillary apikes with a strong offensive smell. Spikes slender, interrupted 3-6 inch long. Flowers bisexual mixed with male, male shortly pedicillate. Bracts linear, very early caudaceus. Free part of the calyx cup shaped, cleft half way into 5 acute triangular segments. Stamens as many as the calyx lobes or twice and 2 seriate, on the calyx. Ovary completely inferior, 1 celled, style and stigma simple (2, 5, 6, 7, 12).

Breeding System :

Phenology :
The old leaves start falling in November and some trees become leafless in December, while some may continue to be in leaves till January. Greenish white flowers appear from March to May (8, 9, 11).

Pollinators:

Seed Biology :
About 400-450 seeds weigh one kilogram. For raising 1000 plants, about 5 kg seeds are needed. Seeds retain their viability for one year under ordinary conditions.

Seedling Germination :
The seeds have high fertility and germinate readily when provided with favourable conditions. Germination capacity and germination energy of Terminalia bellirica seeds can be enhanced or increased by various presowing treatments viz., (including soaking in water, concentrated sulfuric acid and gibberellin (GA3), stratification and scarification). Germination is hypogeous. Germination commences after 10 days and continues till 7 weeks. The germintive capacity is 35-70%, fresh seeds give best germination. The growth of the seedlings during the first season is moderate. From the second season onwords, the height growth of the seedlings is comparitively faster. The seedlings grow errect and produce strong side branches (8, 9, 11, 19, 20, 21).

Cultivation:

This species can be propagated either by direct sowing or by planting out nursery raised seedlings. In case of direct sowing, seeds are sown in June-July with the commencement of monsoon rains. The seeds are soaked in water for 24 hours to soften the seed coat. Sowing is done in lines about 3 m apart. The soil along the lines to be sown is worked well. The seeds are covered lightly. The lines should be kept weeded and bushes likely to shade the seedlings should also be cut.

Nursery and planting technique: Sowing in irrigated nurseries is done in March-April. The seed from the current year's collection is preferably used. Lines are spaced about 20 cm and the seed is sown about 5 cm apart. A germination percentage of 50-90 can be expected. Regular irrigation and weeding of the nursery beds are necessary. Planting of entire plants is done in June-July when the plants are about 3-4 months old as the roots of the seedlings grown in the nursery for more than a year become fairly long which renders planting out of such seedlings difficult. Planting of the seedlings is done with balls of earth in 30 cm3 pits. Spacing adopted is normally 3 x 3 m. Stump planting gives good success and is preferred to planting of entire plants in some areas. Stumps are prepared from 12-15 months old plants. Planting is done in July-August after the commencement of monsoon rains. The stumps can be planted either in 30 cm3 pits or in crow bar holes. It can also be propagated by vegetative means i.e., side veneer grafting and chip budding (7, 8, 9, 11, 13, 23).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

A number of insect pests have been reported to damage different parts of the tree. The larvae of a large number of insect pests belonging to the families Arctiidae, Gelechiidae, Hesperiidae, Lasiocampidae, Limacodidae, Lymantriidae, Noctuidae, Pyralidae, and Thyrididae defoliate the leaves. Acrocercops calycophthalma mines the leaves. Fulgora delesserti and Drosicha spp. feed on the sap. In the felled condition, the species is liable to insect- attack, and the best and simplest method of natural protection against the borers of sap and heartwood is to debark the timber immediately on felling the trees and stack it in the open rather than in the shade of the forest (7, 8).

Diseases:

The fungi recorded on the tree are Fornes fastuosus, F. robineae, F. senex, and Ganoderma applanatum causing heart rot, Corticillm repens causing thread blight, Puccinia terminaliae causing woody galls on branches and leaves and Phyllachora terminaliae causing tar spots. Other important fungi attacking the tree are Cercospora terminaliae, and Trametes ractinea. Phytophthora citrophthora is known to cause leaf blight on Terminalia bellirica (7, 8, 22).

Economic Potential and Uses

On extraction the kernels yield 38.6 per cent of a clear, bright- yellow, non-edible fatty oil which is employed in the manufacture of soaps. The soap made exciusively from T. bellirica oil does not give an adequate detergency and foaming, blending it with 30-40 per cent of other oils gives soaps comparable to any of the quality commercial soaps. Soap containing 60 per cent of T. bellirica oil, 25 per cent coconut oil and 15 per cent ground nut oil showed -excellent power of foaming and superior detergency over commercial soaps. Also, the soap made from bellirica oil is reported to be pure white instead of the conventional pale yellow, and can be employed in the preparation of toilet-soaps of attractive appearance. The oil obtained from seeds is trichogenous and is useful in dyspepsia, skin diseases and leucoderma. The oil is also considered a good application for the hair and in some parts of India, it is used by poorer classes as a substitute for ghee (7, 11, 13).

Fruits, seeds and leaves

Uses

The fruit is the well-known commercial myrobalan, called belleric myrobalan. It contains all the components of chebulic myrobalan, except corilagin and chebulic acid, and can be used as a substitute for myrobalans in tanning. The seeds contain protein from which satisfactory adhesives, both cold-pressed and hot-setting can be developed. The fruits (without kernels) can be used for the preparation of oxalic acid. They can also be used in ink-making. Wood is used in house-building, chiefly as rafters and boards, and for packing-cases. The timber has been found suitable for second quality slate frames. It is at present largely employed in petty construction and for heavy packing. The timber is suitable for the manufacture of plywood for general purposes and for tea-chests. The wood in mixture with other hardwoods has been found to be suitable for the manufacture of chemical pulps for writing, printing and wrapping papers. The wood is also used for fuel and for making charcoal. The leaves can be fed to livestock and Tasar silkworm (7, 11, 13, 14 ).

Triphala, an Ayurvedic preparation, consists of the dried fruits of Terminalia chebula, T. belerica [T. bellirica] and Emblica officinalis [Phyllanthus emblica] in equal proportions. It is extensively used as adjunct to other medicines in almost all diseases. Some of the uses of triphala are as follows (18):

  • It is a strengthening rejuvenator.

  • It also nourishes the nervous system and increases both nutrient assimilation and digestive enzymes.

  • It regulates metabolism, reduces excess fat, builds blood, muscle and nerves, purifies the blood, and removes abdominal gas and distention.

  • It cleanses out toxins and accumulations of toxic mucus throughout the body, and prevents further accumulation.

  • Recently Triphala is generating significant interest around the world because each of the three fruits displays anti-HIV activity.

  • Over a period of time it revitalizes the whole body by detoxifying practically every system in the body, vitalizing the major and minor metabolic processes, and increasing the red blood cell count.

  • It also promotes digestion, appetite, and the assimilation of nutrients, especially Vitamin B which are so necessary for energy production and stress.

Harvesting Practices
Seeds are collected from the well grown trees in November-February. Freshly fallen fruits are collected from the ground, the pulp is removed immediately and the seeds dried in sun. There is no organised way of seed collection or production (7, 8, 11).

Produce and Products

Oil is obtained from the seeds which is being used in the manufacture of soaps. Fruits have industrial importance and has wide number of uses. Leaves are good source of fodder and wood is extensively used in plywood industries. Apart from that fruits and seed oil have lot of medicinal value.

Processing Techniques

On a commercial scale oil is obtained from the seed by expression or by extraction by solvents the former being a common practice in the villages. There are three traditional methods of expressing oil from seed. (1) Boiling method (2) Chappa method and (3) Bullock Ghani method. In boiling method the depulped seed is boiled in water for some time and then dried. Thereafter it is roasted on a hot pan until the shell become crisp and friable. The whole seed is crushed into powder and it is boiled in water for some time after which the oil floating at the top water is separated by decantation. In the Chappa method the powdered kernel is steamed over a vessel of boiling water and pressed between the two limbs of chappa. In bullock ghani method the powdered kernel is sprinkled with little hot water and pressed in a Bullock-Ghani (7, 10).

Other Uses
The fruit possesses antibacterial properties. It is employed in dropsy, piles and diarrhea.. The fruit possesses myocardial depressive activity. The bark is mildly diuretic and is useful in anaemia and leucoderma. Fruits are acrid, sweet, themogenic, anti-inflammatory, anodyne, styptic, digestive, anthelmintic, aperient, expectorant, ophthalmic, antiemetic and rejuvenating. They are useful in cough, asthma, pharyngitis, insomnia, dropsy, dyspepsia, flatulence, dipsia, cardiac disorders, haemorrhages, ophthalmic disorders, strangury, cephalagia, skin diseases, leprosy, fevers, ulcers and general debility. The mature and dry fruit is constipating and is useful in diarrhoea and dysentery. The kernels possess narcotic properties, eaten with betel nut and betal leaf for treatment of dyspepsia. They are also useful in urinary calculus. While using herbal eye drops containing T.bellirica, encouraging results have been obtained in cases of myopia, corneal opacity, pterigium, immature cataract, chronic and acute infective conditions (7, 11, 12, 13, 15, 16, 17).

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

This tree is grown mainly for timber and for its leaves as livestock and silkworm fodder. Fruits are used in tanning industry. The plant has not gained importance as an oilseed crop. Further full potential of the crop has to be tapped by removing the impediments.
