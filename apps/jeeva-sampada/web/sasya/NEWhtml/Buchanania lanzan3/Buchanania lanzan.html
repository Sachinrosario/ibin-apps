Other Biological Details
HABIT & HABITAT

An evergreen tree with a straight, cylindrical trunk up to 15 m in height, commonly found throughout the greater part of India in dry deciduous forests up to an altitude of 1200 m and in the sub-Himalayan  tract up to 900 m.

This tree is found mostly on eroded ravine lands and occurs locally in clay soils.  It avoids waterlogged areas.  As a moderate light demander, it prefers partial shade.  It is very sensitive to frost.

Back to Top


Availability :  
Globally, the distribution of this species is reported to be in the Indo-Malaysian region, but mainly found in Indian subcontinent, Bangladesh and  Myanmar (earlier Burma).  In India it occurs in Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Tripura, Goa, Himachal Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil nadu, Kerala and Karnataka.  This tree is widely distributed in the deciduous forests of Western Peninsula, also from Sutlej Eastward along the Himalayan forests hills and Southwards through Central India.

It is a moderate sized tree with a small crown.  It is generally found in the dry deciduous forests up to an elevation of 1200m.  It is also found in the moist deciduous forests of the Western Ghats.  It avoids water logged areas. 

It is frequently found in dry mixed deciduous forests of Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, Orissa, Maharashtra, West Bengal and Andhra Pradesh.


Status : 

Data collected from Southern India show that this species falls under " Lower Risk-least concern" category.  However, data on global scale is deficient.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Branches tomentose when young; bark rough; leaves thickly coriaceous, broadly oblong, obtuse, base rounded; flowers small, greenish white, in axillary and terminal panicles; Carpel 5, only one perfect, other four reduced  to cylindrical filaments. Drupes ovoid or globose, black, 8-12 mm in diameter; stones hard.

Seed Biology :
Ripe fruits are collected and pulp is washed off, seeds are dried in shade and stored in sealed containers.  Viability is up to one year.  Seed weight is 4000per kg.  

Seedling Germination :

Seeds freely regenerate under favourable conditions, provided they are quickly covered.  The fruits fall immediately before or at the commencement of the rains.  If they are quickly covered with earth or debris by the rain or are protected by grass or other low cover, germination soon commences and the seedlings have a chance of establishing themselves by developing slowly.  Radicles may dry up or may be damaged by insects.  It has poor coppicing capacity and produces root suckers sparingly.  Part of natural regeneration is from root suckers on hill ground where roots are liable to be exposed.

Seed propagation: Seeds are collected in April-May.  Fresh seeds are not exposed to sun.  The fruits are collected from the trees and de-pulped mechanically to extract seeds which are then dried in the shade for a day.  Seed viability is found to be variable, usually up to 1 year when stored in sealed tins.  The number of seeds per kilo vary between 3000 to 5000.  Seeds have hard and stony seed coat, hence the seed treatment should be given: i) Soaking in cold/tepid water for 4-6 days; ii) immersion in hot water(80-100°C) then allowing it to cool followed by soaking for 24 hours; iii) Mechanical disruption of seed coat; iv) Soaking in concentrated sulphuric acid; v) Soaking in GA3- 5ppm for 24 hrs.  Germination starts in 20 to 30 days.  When the seedlings are about 5 to 7cm in height they are pricked out with ball of earth and planted in polybags.  These bags are kept under shade for a period of one week.

Seeds should be sown directly for better success than transplanting from nursery beds.  

Seeds can also be sown in seed pans or trays or in polybags in June and a layer of hay is spread over bags.  Germination of seeds is completed in 15 to 25 days with 60 to 70 percent seeds germinating.  About 80 to 85 percent seedlings survive.  Development of the seedling is slow.

Vegetative propagation is through cuttings of 1cm x 15cm with two nodes are used.

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Roots, kernels, bark, wood, leaves, fruits

Uses

  • Bark - The bark is used in tanning. Furnishes a natural varnish and is used in tanning in Kerala.  It yields a dark reddish brown leather of somewhat stiff and harsh texture.  
  • Stem exudes a gum suitable for dressing textile. Used in diarrhea and intercoastal pains. In A.P. gum dissolved in cow’s milk is used internally in rheumatic pains.

Other Uses
  1. Roots are acrid, astringent, cooling, depurative and are useful in vitiated conditions of pitta and kapha, leprosy, skin diseases and diarrhea.
  2. Kernals have a pleasant sub-acidic flavour. They are nutritious and eaten raw or roasted. They form a common substitute for almonds in flavouring  sweetmeats, confectionery and betal nut powder. They are reported to be used by tribals of Gujarat as brain tonic. Kernels have a mixed flavor of pistachio and almond, and are eaten raw or roasted. They are commonly used in preparing desserts. The market price is about Rs120 per kilogram.
  3. An ointment made out of the kernels and is used to cure itch of the skin and to remove blemishes from the face.
  4. Kernels yield a  light yellow, sweet oil with a mild, pleasant aroma.  Oil is used as a substitute for olive and almond oil in indigenous medicine. Oil is applied to glandular swellings of the neck.
  5. Wood is utilized as small beams to support light roofing and rafters, in mining, for making doors and window-frames, boxes and yokes and making cheap furniture. Suitable for matches and medium fuel-wood.
  6. Leaves - tonic and cardiotonic properties, and their powder is a common medicine for wounds. Leaves have cooling, digestive, expectorant, leprosy, skin diseases and seminal weakness. Leaves are also used as fodder.
