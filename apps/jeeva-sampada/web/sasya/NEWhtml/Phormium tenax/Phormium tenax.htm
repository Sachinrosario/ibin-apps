Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Phomium tenax is a robust perennial herb. It is widely cultivated in cool tropics and warm temperate regions for fiber and ornamental foliage. It has been introduced in gardens in the hills of South India and is reported to be growing well in Ootacamund. Phormium grew wild in New Zealand long before the arrival of Europeans and will grow readily on practically ay soil of the islands. It grows best, however on light rich soils which allow good drainage and permit plenty of air to reach he roots. Reclaimed marshlands are suitable, and much of the fiber is raised in semi-wild state on such land

Conservation/ Threat Status : Wild and cultivated.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Phormium tenax does not have a central upright stem or trunk but consists rather of leaves which grow in the manner of a fan directly from the upper ends of the root stock. Each fan consists of about 8 leaves with the youngest centre. The individual leaf is folded longitudinally along the midrib with the upper surface on the innerside of the fold and is usually split near the end. Leaves vary from 3-14 ft in length, from 1-5 inches in width, and in colour from a yellowish green to a dark blue green.

The root is a creeping rhizome which branches out to form a ring of plants around the original root bark. Flowers in panicles, upto 6m long, yellowish red; capsules 5-10 cm long, many seeded.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

The flower-stalk grows out from the center of the fan, and bears deep-waxy-red or yellow, paniculate flowers. A single flower-stalk may produce upto 100 seed-pods or capsules; these are triangular in cross section, about 2 to 4 inches long, and about ½ inches in diameter. Each contains about 60 to 100 flat, black and shiny seeds. The seeds are very small, and about 40,000 of them weigh only I lb. Once the inflorescence has developed, the production of new leaves ceases, and the fan dies within about a year.

Microspore mother cells undergo meiotic divisions, cytokinesis is simultaneous. Archesporial cell functions directly as megaspore mother cell. Polygonum type of embryo sac. Synergids are hooked. Polar nuclei fuse before fertilisation.


Breeding system and varietal improvement:
As the pollen of the individual flowers ripens before the stigma of the same flower is ready to receive it, self-pollination is prevented naturally.(3) Although the use of seed-grown plants is desirable for breeding of improved strains, propagation for fiber production is most satisfactorily carried on by division of the rhizomes, thus preserving the qualities of the parent plant.

Phenology:
Flowers are produced in the early summer months of November to January in New Zealand.


Pollinators:

In New Zealand, in the past the pollinating was usually done by the Tui bird (Prosthemandera novae - Zeelandiae), although now that this bird has become comparatively scarce, pollination appears to be done by bees, by other birds such as starlings and sparrows, and by the wind. The birds are attracted to the flowers by the very sweet nectar which they contain at certain times.

Floral and seed biology:
Seedling germination :

Planting is done preferably in the spring months of August to November on land which has been placed and freed of weeds. Rows are usually 8 ft. apart with the plants spaced at 4 foot intervals. Periodic cultivation between the rows during the first 18-20 months. Keeps down weeds and gives the plants a vigorous start. A single plant produces about leave on five years, but each season 3 or 4 of the older leaves die off so that at the end of the five years only 8-10 leaves are available for harvesting.Although the plant has been found to grow well in New Zealand on very acid soils that are deficient in lime, it makes very little growth unless phosphatic manures are used as fertilizers.



MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
A pest of Phormium in New Zealand is Olairus atkinsonii, but as the natural flooding which takes place in the areas where Phormium grows wild destroys this insect at the nymphal stage, the pest seems to be kept under control by natural means.
Diseases:

The most serious disease which attacks Phormium tenax is known as yellow leaf disease, and it appears to be caused by some attack by microorganisms, although the exact cause has not yet been determined. The disease shows itself by a yellowing of the leaves and the eventual death of the plant. It is stated to have much in common with 'Panama disease' of bananas.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
The most common uses of Phormium are in the making of woolpacks, matting, binder twine and lashing. In the later phormium and sisal are often combined. It is also used in smaller quantities as a binder in plasters, in paper manufacture, mattress and upholstery stuffing, and occasionally in rope, either alone or in combination with Manila Hemp. Ordinarily, the by-product tow is used in woolpacks and in products where strength is of secondary importance.

In Argentina, it is used for making the rope-soiled shoes known as alpargatas. In South Africa it is used in sack manufacture in admixture with jute. In Kenya, the fiber is used to close the mesh of sisal bags.

Parts Used:

Leaves


Harvesting Practices:
The leaves are cut off 6-8 inches above the ground with a curved hand sickle and tied into bundles weighing approximately 70 pounds Tram lines or roads are laid out at convenient intervals in the field for transporting the bundles to the stripping mill. In Southern New Zealand harvest is commonly restricted to the spring and summer months, but in the North Island it goes on the year round except for early autumn when young leaves might grow up to be at the mercy of winter frosts. On the average 30 tons of green leaf/acre is considered a good yield, though yields above 40 tons are known.

Produce and Products:


Chemistry:
Analysis of the fiber shows moisture, 10; fat and wax, 0.70, waer solubles, 2.20; lignin, 11.20; cellulose, 45.10; hemicellulose, 30.10; and pectin, 0.7%. The fiber yields upto 60% of a pulp suitable for making writing and wrapping paper and rayon. The pulp can be fermented to produce alcohol and the residue can be used as cattle feed.

Processing Techniques:
After cutting, the bundles of green leaf are hailed to the stripping mill for the first step in processing the fiber. Stripping literally beats the vegetative matter off the fiber. Unfortunately this decortication considerably weakens the fiber and beats a part of the fiber out of the leaf as well. The resulting refuse, known as "stripper slips", is reclaimed for its fiber. Following the stripping process the fiber is passed between washing drums with water flowing over them to wash away the loose vegetable matter. During the washing fiber is gripped on an endless chain and is automatically released on leaving the washer. At this stage the fiber is formed into hanks y hand and thrown over a pole to drain. Each hank represents the fiber from 12 to 20 leaves. When the hanks are fairly dry they are hauled to the sun and weather for about a week. The fiber is spread out in long rows on the ground or if the weather is rainy is hung on wire fences. During this period a partial retting occurs, in which fragments of vegetative material are loosened from the fiber. After the desired stage of bleaching is reached, the fiber is reformed into hanks by hand and either stacked in stock piles or hauled directly to scotching nulls. The purpose of scotching is to remove the dry material which has been loosened in the bleaching field. The automatic urbo-scutcher is in general use now, although hand feeding was common in the early years of the industry. Hanks of fiber are placed over an endless chain which grips the fiber and takes it through a cylindrical scotching drum. Here, revolving beaters remove residue, short fibers and rough strands. After their first trip through, the hanks are automatically released and gripped in a different place. During the second trip other parts of each hank are exposed to the scotching beaters. The short and touch fibers are reclaimed as tow. After scotching, the fibers are again formed into hanks and, if to be transported any great distance, are pressed into 400 lb bales.

In the manufacture of the fiber into twine or yarns for weaving, the hanks are drawn out to the desired thickness by combs and rollers in a standard hard fiber "goods machine". In this process a "batching oil" is added to the fiber to improve its working qualities. The strands may then be spun into a variety of weights of twines or yarns.


Other uses:

The seeds yield upto 30.7% of a brown coloured semi drying oil of potential use in the paint and varnish industry. The gm exuding from bases of leaves and the nectar of flowers are reported to be collected and eaten in New Zealand. A decoction of he roots is used as purgative and anthelmintic.
