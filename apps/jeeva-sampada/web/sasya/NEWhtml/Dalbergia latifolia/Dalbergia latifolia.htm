Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:

A large glabrous deciduous or nearly evergreen tree. Found in Sub-Himalayan tract and in tropical high rainfall areas of Western Ghats, West Bengal and Assam (wet tropical). Wood hard and heavy which sinks in water before seasoning. Tree produces a large number of root suckers from its long, horizontal, superficial root branches. It is cultivated in Bihar, Orissa, Gujarath, Maharashtra and south India.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Large glabrous tree with numerous spreading branches, forming a shady head. Leaves imparipinnate, 4-6" long, rachis straight, glabrous, much produced beyond the insertion of the uppermost pair of leaflets. Leaflets 5-7 (usually 5), firm, nearly as long as broad (the terminal slightly the largest), broadly ovate or suborbicular, rounded, sometimes emarginate at the apex. Glabrous on both sides, pale beneath, base shortly cuneate.

 

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Flowers in axillary or extra-axillary lax divaricate panicles usually shorter than the leaves, pedicels filiform, bracts minute, bracteoles membranous, very caducous. Calyx glabrous, teeth linear-oblong, obtuse, rather shorter than the tube. Corolla greenish or yellowish white, the petals with long claws. Stamens 9 in one bundle. Ovary glabrous, ovules 3-5. Pods star-shaped, slightly reticulated, glabrous. Seeds 1-3 (rarely 4).

Floral and seed biology:

Seedling Germination: Artificial propagation is possible by both direct sowing and by planting seedlings, root suckers and sections of lateral roots. Stump planting of nursery seedlings is favoured, particularly in the west coast. Weeding and cleaning are essential in all cases.

For direct sowing it is advisable to break the pods into one-seeded parts. Good results are obtained by sowing in prepared lines, c. 18 in. wide, the distance between lines being 12 ft. Seeds are sown shortly before the commencement of rains, 5 oz. Of pods being sufficient to sow 100 ft. of line.

For transplanting, seeds are sown in raised nursery beds of porous sandy loam in drills 9 in. apart or in lines 18 in. apart according as transplanting is to be done during the first or the second season. Seeds are sown in March or April and the beds are regularly watered and weeded; protection from the sun is necessary. Seedlings are transplanted early in the first rains either entire or stumps with stems pruned down to c. 2 in. and taproots to c. 6 in. Seedlings may be kept in the nursery till the early part of the second rains; in that case, they should be thinned out during the cold season. Before transplanting, the stem should be pruned down to about 2 in. and the taproot to about 12 in. The size of the stump is an important factor in stump planting. For raising exclusive plantations, seedlings or stumps should be planted 15 ft. apart, alternate saplings being removed subsequently (Cameron, 94). The plants are also propagated by cuttings obtained from one-year-old branches.

MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:


A number of fungi (Polystictus steinheilianus Berk. & Lev., Schizophyllum commune Fr., Trametes lactinea Berk. and T. Persoonii Fr.) cause white rot on the tree.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts Used:

Wood used as fuel.

Wood: Sapwood lighter in colour than the heartwood, often light grey-brown, sometimes with pale yellow cast. Heartwood brown to reddish-brown and quickly turning dark brown or red on exposure. Rather dull, very hard, very heavy, average weight 815 kg/m3 (51 lb/ft3) at 12 per cent moisture content. Specific gravity 0.97 – 1.08 air dry. Somewhat straight to interlocked-grained, occasionally showing ribbon like bands. Medium to nearly fine-textured. Rather close and even-grained. Wood usually with a faint pleasant odour.

A diffuse-porous wood, very rarely with a slight semi-ring-porous tendency. Growth rings indistinct to fairly distinct. Pores large to small, visible to the eye, few to moderately numerous, mostly solitary or in short radial multiples, often filled with dark gummy deposits; vessel lines distinct on longitudinal surfaces. Soft tissues mostly round the pores, forming eyelets, with lateral extensions, often connecting the adjacent pores by narrow, wavy or straight tangential bands particularly in the late wood and also as a fine broken or interrupted line delimiting growth rings visible only under the lens. Rays fine to very fine, distinct only under the lens as numerous, closely-spaced lines. Ripple marks distinct only under the lens, usually more prominent in the sapwood, 60-65 per cm.

The wood structure is very similar to that of sissoo (Dalbergia sissoo Roxb.) but the two timbers can be distinguished easily by their colour.

Wood is well known in Europe and America for its chief use in the pianoforte trade. It is also a valuable decorative wood suitable for carving and ornamental plyboards and veneers. It is especially useful for calico-printing blocks, mathematical instruments and screws. It is also used for gun carriage wheels, ammunition boxes and army wagons, pulleys, knaves, felloes, handles, shelves, decorative carriage parts, temple cars, boat knees, well construction, agricultural implements, combs, razor handles and brush backs.

It is too expensive for general construction work, but when available it is used for posts, rafters, floor boards, and door and window frames. Arefully selected and manufactured plyboards satisfy aircraft specifications.

In South India, liked very much for house building especially for posts, beams, rafters, door and window frames and planking; locally also used for plough-handles. It may be worthwhile to try this for turnery work.


Harvesting Practices:

Either coppicing or selection felling

Processing Techniques:

Wood can be seasoned either in the air or in the kiln with but little degrade. It darkens in colour during kiln-seasoning thereby gaining in value. It is one of the few hard woods which season better in the log or in rough squares than in planks; it attains its best colour only when seasoned in the log. The centres of logs are often faulty due to calcareous deposits and in such cases, it is advisable to box the centres during conversion.

Due to hardness, this is rather difficult to saw; reciprocating saw is necessary for obtaining satisfactory results. It can be machined without difficulty and worked to a smooth surface which takes fine polish. It can be also peeled on a rotary lathe after heating in water, to yield handsome veneers which, however, tend to develop small surface cracks. It can be bent after streaming; it is, however, not as good as sissoo wood in this respect.


Other uses:

Leaves are used as fodder. It is grown in coffee plantations as a shade tree. The bark of the tree are reported to be useful as stimulant and appetiser and also in dyspepsia, diarrhoea, leprosy, obesity and worms.
