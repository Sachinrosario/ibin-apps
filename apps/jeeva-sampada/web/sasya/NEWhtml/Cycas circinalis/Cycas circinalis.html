Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

It is a dioecious plant, 6-20 ft high. Like other cycads the queen sago resembles a palm tree with its feather-like leaves arranged in a rosette that crowns a single trunk. The queen sago's solitary trunk can grow to 20 feet in height which is twice that of the Japanese sago. In older specimens some branching may occur to produce plants with multiple crowns. It typically occurs in fairly dense, seasonally dry scrubby woodlands in hilly areas. Many trees in this habitat lose their leaves in the dry season, and C. circinalis is also facultatively deciduous in extremely dry times. Queen sago is native to equatorial Africa. C. circinalis is now known to be an Indian endemic, restricted to the Western Ghats, in the states of Kerala, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Orissa and the south of Maharashtra.

  (1, 2,7,10,11,12,14,16,17,18)

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

Stems arborescent, to 5 m tall. And leaves are bright green, semiglossy, 150-250 cm long, flat (not keeled) in section (opposing leaflets inserted at 180° on rachis), with 170 leaflets, tomentum shedding as leaf expands. Petiole 40-70 cm long, glabrous, spinescent for 90% of length. Basal leaflets not gradually reducing to spines. Cataphylls narrowly triangular, soft, thinly sericeous or lacking tomentum, 50 mm long, persistent. (1, 2,7,10,11,12,14,16,17,18)

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Plants are either male or female. Pollen cones narrowly ovoid, orange, 45 cm long, 10 cm diam.; microsporophyll lamina firm, not dorsiventrally thickened, 38-50 mm long, 12-19 mm wide, apical spine prominent, gradually raised, 25 mm long. Megasporophylls or female organ is 30 cm long, brown-tomentose; ovules 4-10, glabrous; lamina lanceolate, 74-100 mm long, 25-38 mm wide, regularly dentate, with 24 pungent lateral spines, apical spine distinct from lateral spines. 

(1, 2,7,10,11,12,14,15)

Pollinators:

In their native habit, Cycads are pollinated by wind or insects, however, in cultivation, they usually must be hand-pollinated for viable seed production. Recent studies have shown that cycads are not entirely wind pollinated, as was originally assumed. Some pollination is done by beetles. (20)


Seed Biology :

Female plants produce large orange seeds in a cone structure located in the center of the rosette of leaves. Seeds are subglobose, 25-38 mm long; sarcotesta yellow; fibrous layer present; sclerotesta smooth. Spongy endocarp absent.  (1,11,18)

Seedling Germination :

The seed will usually germinate in 8 to 12 weeks. (1,11,18)


Cultivation: 

Propagation: Propagated by seed. Press seeds to half their depth in moist sand. Keep moist and in a warm place.

Soil: Queen sago is tolerant of soils but prefers loose sandy soils with some organic matter and light bark or leaf mulch.

Light: Bright conditions are best. Can take full sun and moderate shade as well.

Moisture: Needs adequate moisture but will tolerate occasional short-lived droughts. Make sure soil is well drained or roots will rot.(1,16)

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used (1,4,5,17)

Seeds, Bark, Tender leaves, Pollen and Stem

Uses

  • A flour is obtained from the seeds. This must be thoroughly washed and processed to remove toxins.

  • The bark and seeds ground to a paste with coconut oil, are used as poultice for sores and swellings.

  • The juice of the tender leaves is said to be usefull for flatulence and vomiting.

  • The pollen is narcotic.

  • The stem yields Starch in the form of Sago and it also yields gum.


Harvesting Practices (1,5,17)

The Sago is extracted from the plant when the plant is about 7 years old (before fruiting). The plant is cut down and after removing the outer bark, the core is cut into discs, sun-dried and ground into meal. On stirring up the meal with water and settling, the starch separates out. About 5 lb. Of Sago are obtained from a stem 4 ft. long. The large, ovalish, orange-red seeds from the fruiting plant also yields annually about the same quantity of starch, and apparently, it is more economical to extract from the seed than from the felled plants.

Produce and Products (1,5,17)

-Flour: Prepared from the seed.

-Sago: Prepared from stem and the seed, used as Infant food, Pudding, Porridge, Soups and Savouries, Sago snow, Sago papad, Sago kichri, Payasam, Kesari, Uppuma, Vangibath, Curd bath and Boondi.

Jelly: The sogo is boiled with sugar to make jelly.

Processing Techniques  

For the manufacture of palm sago, the starch is obtained from the pith of the stem. The trees are cut down when they grow about 8-10 m high, stripped of leaves ,and cut into small lengths ( 1 m). The outer woody rind is slit lengthwise and the pith removed, pounded, and the starch extracted with water is filtered and allowed to settle in trays, Further purification is done by re-suspending the starch in water, filling and tabling in long troughs. Starch is dried in the sun and the partially moist cake is pressed through a coarse screen whereby pellets are formed. The pellets are given spherical shapes by subjecting them to a circular swinging motion in a hollow hammock-shaped cloth: They are sieved and roasted in shallow metal pans for partially gelatinizing and drying. A single palm is reported to yield 125-330 kg of sago. Manufacture of sago. (1,4,5,17)

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country  

Prospects for sago are particularly bright for some nontraditional uses. Several developing countries have expressed an interest in establishing industries for the bioconversion of sago starch to protein. In some countries sago palm has recently been used by local industries to produce ethanol, which can be added to gasoline to strech petroleum supplies, or, with appropriately designed engines, can be used as a transportation fuel. (1,21)
