Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

A fleshy perennial climbing orchid with stout stem and grey white adventitious roots (velamen roots) opposite to the leaves. It is a shallow rooted and surface feeder plant. Root growth is confined to the decayed organic mater applied around the plant base. Basal roots have mycorrhizal association and reach the humus or mulch layer. This exotic orchid is a highly remunerative crop. Vanilla is the source of natural vanillin sought after for its pleasant flavour. It is one of the important low volume high value spice crops and the second costliest spice after saffron (2). The name vanilla comes from the Spanish word vainilla, meaning ‘little pod’ (7).

Climate and soil
Vanilla requires a warm and moist humid tropical climate with frequent rains. Thrives well between 10 and 20 North and South latitudes from sea level to over 700 to 1500 m above sea level in a temperature range of 25 to 32C (average 27C). Ideal condition for vanilla is moderate rainfall. Annual rainfall may be 150-300 cm evenly distributed for a period of 3 months with dry spells for a period of 9 months during flowering and harvest period. It needs water and moist conditions. It requires a partial shade of about 50%. A gentle slop is ideal for cultivation of vanilla. Vanilla is adapted to wide range of soil types from well-drained sandy loam which is loose and friable with good organic content rich in humus to laterite soils with a pH of 6-7. Humus rich forest soil of Western Ghats is ideally suitable for cultivation of vanilla. Clayey soil and water logged areas are not suitable for vanilla. However, the plant cannot survive under excessive dryness, but slightly tolerant to drought conditions, usually up to three weeks. Favours slightly acidic pH and is not tolerant to frost and wind.

Availability :

Originated in humid tropical rainforests, indigenous to south-eastern Mexico, Guatemala and Central America. Important production areas are Indonesia, Madagascar, Tonga, Comores and French Oceania. Vanilla is cultivated to a limited extent in Kerala, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu (2).

Conservation/ Threat Status : Wild and cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES: A fleshy, herbaceous, perennial vine, climbing up trees to a height of 10-15 m by means of long, whitish, adventitious roots, opposite the leaves. Stem long, cylindrical, 1-2 cm in diameter, simple or branched, succulent, dark green. Leaves alternate, fleshy subsessile; blade oblong-elliptical to elongate to lanceolate, 8-25 cm x 2-8 cm, somewhat rounded at the base, acute to acuminate at top, with numerous parallel veins. 

V. planifolia is a fleshy perennial climbing orchid with sessile leaves and succulent green stem climbs up on trees or other supports by means of aerial adventitious roots (velamen roots) at the nodes. Root occurs singly, opposite the leaves and adheres firmly to the support on which the vines climb. The basal roots have association of mycorrhiza and reach the humus or mulch layer. Stem is succulent and has internodes 5-15 cm long. Leaves are 8-25 cm long and 2-8 cm broad, oblong elliptic to lens-like with a short but thick petiole (9).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR: Flowers are borne in axillary racemose inflorescence usually towards the top of the vine. Up to 20-30 flowers occur in a bunch but usually they are only 6-15. They mature from base to apex, and at any given time only one to three flowers open and they last for a day, with only 1-3 flowers opening at one time. Flowers are large, pale-greenish-yellow, about 10 cm in diameter, fragrant and have short pedicel. Sepals 3, oblong, 4-7 cm x 1-1.5 cm; 2 upper petals resembling the sepals but slightly smaller, labellum (lower petal, trumpet-shaped) 4-5 cm x 1.5-3 cm, obscurely 3-lobed at top, inside hairy at base; column 3-5 cm long, attached to the labellum, bearing at its tip 2 pollinia covered by a cap. Ovary is inferior, usually curved, and 4-7 cm long. They are oblong lens-like sepals. 
Lower parts of the labellum envelop a central structure called the column. Tip of the column bears a single stamen with two pollen masses (pollinia) covered by a cap or hood-like structure below which is the stigma. Separating the stigma from the pollinia is a flap called rostellum. Because of the rostellum, natural self-pollination is generally impossible (9).

                                                                                                                                                 
Fruit
Fruits are capsules known as the beans in trade. They are pendulous cylindrical, obscurely 3-angled, 10-25 cm long and 1.5 to 4.5 cm in girth. Fruits become aromatic up on drying and contain hundreds of minute seeds, which get liberated at maturity by longitude splitting of the capsule. Under cultivation they are harvested before they ripe.

Breeding system and varietal improvement:

Phenology:
Flowering period is from December-March and some times in October-November depending up on the altitude of cultivation. Flowering in April under high elevation at Myladumpara (10,000 m above MSL) (2). The maximum production of flowers occurs during the 7-8th year of planting. Flowering takes about 45 to 60 days from initiation of inflorescence. Generally, only one flowering occurs in a year. Inflorescences are formed in the axillary racemes of vine of one-year-old growth. Unlike in most of the orchids, flowers of vanilla lasts for only a day, only from early morning to late evening. Vanilla usually starts flowering in the third year of planting; however, it depends on the size of the original cutting used for planting. Maximum production of flowers occurs during the 7-8th year. Pinching off the top 7.5 to 10.0 cm of the vine, 6-8 months pruning off the older branches (which bore fruits the previous year) also encourages flower production (4).

Pollinators: Natural pollination is seen only in the place of origin. The natural pollinators are Melapona bees and humming birds. Hand pollination is done for commercial fruit production. Pollinate the flowers on the same day of flower opening since the flower lasts only for a day. Anthesis is in early morning hours. Pollination may be done between 6 AM - 12 PM. Pollinate only 10-12 inflorescences in a vine in order to get beans of good size. Hand pollination is very simple and can be done within seconds. Hold the flower in left hand and push the innermost petal to expose the ‘colum’. Take a splinter in the right hand and slowly lift the rostellum. Then gently press the overhanging anther to the stigma surface with the left thumb. While pressing, pollen grains will get discharged and adhere to the sticky stigmatic surface and effect pollination (2). The remaining flower buds are ripped off.

Floral and seed biology: Seeds are very minute about 0.3 mm in diameter, liberated by longitudinal splitting of the capsule (9).


CULTIVATION ASPECT:
Seed germination:
Seed propagation is very difficult. Germinate on artificial medium only.

Related taxa :
There are about 110 spp. of vanilla described, but only three species viz. V. planifolia, V. pampona & V. tahitensis are important as source of vanilla. Of these, Vanilla planifolia is the most preferred and commercially cultivated (6). In South India two species of vanilla occur mainly in the Tirunelveli forest area. They are V. wightiana, (V. aphylla) and V. walkeriae but of no economic use. Inferior vanilla yielding taxa are V. pompana Sch. (West Indian vanilla) cultivated mainly in Hawaii and V. tahitensis, J.W. Moore occurring in Tahiti. Interspecific hybrids produced between V. planifolia with V. pampona and V. tahitensis were found to be inferior in quality. 
Vanilla in India - Genetic resources: Vanilla was introduced into India about a century ago. It seems that only very few introduction only have taken place in this crop, and the genetic base in India is very narrow. Hence it is not easy to select superior vanilla improvement through hybridization. No crop improvement work has been given into this crop in India. Because of the behavioral instability of chromosomes in meiosis and at the time of pollen mitosis much variability could be expected among the seedlings. Variability enhancement through tissue culture and seeds can be utilised for widening the genetic base of vanilla population in India. Considerable scope does exist in utilising the plants for crop improvement. The germplasm repository of ICRI, Myladumpara has 22 accessions of vanilla collected from Wynad, Nagarcoil & Valparai. Collections are also available from Indonesia, Mauritius, Papua New Guinea. 

Genetic resources:
The germplasm of vanilla besides V. planifolia, includes indigenous species like V. aphylla, V. vatsalanae, V. andamanica and exotic species like V. tahitensis and V. pilifera (3).  
V. pompona Schiede, West Indian vanilla, which occurs wild in south-east Mexico and other American countries resembles V. fragrans, but has larger leaves, 15-30 cm long and 5-12 cm wide. The flowers are greenish-yellow and larger and more fleshy, with perianth lobes up to 8.5 cm long. The lip has a tuft of imbricate scales, instead of hairs in the centre of the disc. Cylindrical pods are shorter and thicker, about 10-17.5 cm long and 2.5-3.3 cm in diameter.
V. tahitensis J.W. Moore, Tahitian vanilla, indigenous to Tahiti, is less robust than V. fragrans with slender stems and narrower leaves, 12-14 cm long and 2.5-3.0 cm wide. Lip is shorter than sepals. Pods are 12-14 cm long and 9-10 mm wide tapering towards each end.

Biotechnology:
Techniques have been standardised for micropropagation of V. planifolia, V. aphylla and V. andamanica. Protocols have been developed for conversion of root meristem to shoot meristem and callus regeneration. In vitro technique for seed / ovule culture were developed. Cryopreservation and synthetic seed production were standardised (9).  

Released varieties:
No varieties have been released so far.

Chromosome number:
2n=2x=32

Selection of planting material:
Stem cuttings are used. Poor germination of seed (except in artificial medium). Production of quality beans depends on proper pollination, nutrient management and appropriate and timely plant protection measures. It can also be cultivated as an intercrop in coconut, arecanut, coffee, clove plantations. 

Land should be cleared by cutting unwanted trees and shrubs. Soil should be prepared by ploughing or digging followed by levelling. It is advisable to incorporate organic mulch and forest soil in the cleaned land. Vanilla can also be cultivated in open land by providing adequate shade. 

Vegetative (stem cuttings): Propagated mainly by stem cuttings. Usually cuttings of 60-120 cm long to be selected as planting material for direct planting in the field. Cutting less than 60 cm not to be used. Use only strong, healthy and actively growing vines for planting. Cuttings after collection should be washed in water thoroughly and dipped in Bordeaux mixture (1.0%) or copper oxychloride (0.2%) solution and keep under shade for a week for partial lose of moisture, a process which enhances rooting.

Tissue culture plantlets: Use 9-12 months old tissue culture plants hardened from secondary nursery.

Planting:
Support/Standard: Vanilla being a climber requires a support (standard) for growth and establishment. It is usually trains on low branches of the support trees. Trees with rough bark, low branching and small leaves are preferred as the standard. Glyricidia maculata which is a legume is commonly used as a universal standard for vanilla which is found to be most suitable under Indian condition. The other common trees suitable as standards are Plumaria alba, Casuarina equisettifolia, Mulberry, Erythrina lithosperma, Indian almond (Persia americana) and Cashew (Anacardium occidentale). In Kerala and Karnataka arecanut palm is also used for trailing vanilla. The best time for planting of support is on the outset of rains after the summer break. 

Obtain cuttings from healthy vigorous plants (use any part of the vine), cuttings of 60-100 cm length are preferable, strip off 3 to 4 basal leaves, dip in 1% Bordeaux mixture and keep in shade for a week to lose moisture. Cuttings are to be planted at the base of support tree horizontally. Defoliated part of the vine is laid on the loose soil surface and covered with soil. Gently press these nodes to the soil to cover the nodes with 2-3 cm soil. Care should be taken to ensure that the basal cut end portion of the cutting must be kept just above the surface soil to prevent decay. Vines are coiled around and the base portion is buried in soil. Yield may decline after about 12 to 13 years of planting. It is advised to replant at this stage during the onset of south west monsoon, i.e., in May-June. 

Management after planting:
If the sun is hot the cuttings are shaded. Tip of the cutting is to be tied to the basal part of the support tree. When the weather is neither too rainy nor dry planting of cuttings should be taken up preferably during August-November in shallow pits filled with humus and mulch. A thick mulch of green leaves should be provided immediately after planting mulching 3 to 4 times a year is needed. Cuttings sprout with in 4-8 weeks. In dry soil, a light sprinkling of water helps for easy establishments of cuttings. It takes about four to eight weeks for the cuttings to strike roots and timely irrigation must be given. Allow it to grow only up to 150 cm. Do only surface scrapping for weed control.

Vines should be looped up and down in such a way that it should be retained at a height of up to 1.5 m. This makes the pollination, harvest processing easy and to make the crop productive. The vines can also be trained from one support tree to the other in same row using horizontal poles connecting two support trees or by planting additional support trees between the standards to make the vine for trailing or coiling. Trailing and coiling is an important operation to induce flowering. Horizontal coiling is more advantageous compared to coiling on the branches of the support trees to enhance flowering. Banding and coiling of vines in this manner helps to accumulate carbohydrates and other flower forming hormones and induce flower formation at this portion of vines (2).  

Shade trees should be regularly pruned to keep shade or to induce branching. If the trees do not drop of leaves they were pruned before the commencement of heavy rains for sufficient light. The pruned leaves and branches can be used as mulch to vanilla. Inter cultivation is not generally recommended for vanilla. Occasional slashing of weeds is advised. Regular mulching with irrigation is required during summer months to enhance growth and yield. Spreading of a thick mat of dried leaves on the ground is advisable which will prevent the rise of soil temperature as well as arrest the weed growth.

Fertilizer and manure: Vanilla a surface feeder. Apply manures to the surface layer of soil. The root system is mainly confined to the surface layer of soil and in the mulch. Decomposed organic matters forms the main source of nutrients. Apply easily decomposable organic matter 2 to 3 times every year. Bone meal, dried cowdung and cowdung slury, vermicompost, oil cake, poultry manure, wood ash, or any other easily decomposable organic matter is applied around the plant base can also be used. Apply inorganic fertilizers depending on soil fertility status. The recommended fertilizer to be applied based on the nutrient status of the soil. However, the application of 40 to 60g N, 20 to 30g P2O5 and 60 to 100 g K2O per vine per year in 2 or 3 split doses is recommended besides organic manure. Foliar application of nutrients is also recommended, and is very effective (1).


MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
Insect pests: No serious insect pest damage reported but sporadic occurrence of a beetle Saulu ferruginea notice in Wynad area during October-November. The beetle cuts and eats away leaf damage may lead to leaf rotting.
Control: Weekly application of 1% Malathion at the time of infestation. 

Leaf feeding beetles and Caterpillars: Very few serious insect pests have been recorded on vanilla in India. A few species of leaf feeding caterpillars and beetles feed on leaves and tender stems. Leaf feeding caterpillars and beetles can be controlled by spraying Quinalphos 0.05%.

Sucking bug: Adults and nymphs of the sucking bug Halyomorpha sp. infest tender shoot tips and emerging inflorescences resulting in their drying and rotting. Spraying endosulfan or quinalphos (0.05% each) on tender shot tips and emerging inflorescences is effective for the management of the pest.

Snails: Snails and slugs feed and damage tender shoot tips and leaves especially in moist and shaded areas in the plantations. Hand-picking and poison baiting helps in preventing the pest infestation. No serious insect pest damage is reported. Sporadic occurrence of a beetle Saula ferruginea Gerst noticed in Wynad during October November season. Weekly application of 1% Malathion during the time, of infestation controls the pest.

Diseases:
Fusarium wilt: Caused by Fusarium oxysporum, is the most serious disease of vanilla especially during the monsoon season. 
Symptoms: The leaf axil is infected initially which spreads to the internodal region later resulting in rotting and drying of the stem above the point of infection. The root system is also infected leading to wilting (3).  
Control: Removing the affected portions and spraying 0.25% mancozeb at disease initiations. Vines affected by basal wilt have to be uprooted and destroyed (2). Spraying and drenching carbendazim (0.2%) on the affected vines and providing adequate drainage in the plantation controls the disease. 

Phytophthora rot: Phytophthora meadii causes rotting of beans, leaves and stems. In severe cases all the beans in a bunch are completely rotten. The disease is more severe during the monsoon especially in shaded plantations and poorly drained soils. Phytosanitation and spraying of Bordeaux mixture 1% and drenching of soil with copper oxychloride 0.2% is effective against the disease.

Root rot: Root rot caused by Sclerotium rolfsii is generally seen during the onset of monsoon rains. Initially, rotting of root tips occur and later the rotting extends to the whole root system followed by yellowing and wilting of vines. In severe cases the vine may also die. Drenching of affected vines with Carbendazim 0.1% is effective for the management of the disease.

Shoot tip rot: Characterized by rotting of shoot tips is caused by Colletotricum vanillae and is more common during monsoon season. Drenching of affected vines with Carbendazin 0.1% is effective against this disease.

Sclerotium rot: The disease affects leaves and beans and is caused by Sclerotium rolfsii. The beans develop reddish brown sunken lesions initially and later the entire bean rots. In severe cases all the beans in the bunch are covered with a thick white feathery fungal mat. Spraying Carbendazim 0.1% is effective for control of the disease.

Dry rot: Dry rot affects all parts of the plant and shrivelling of stems, roots and leaves followed by yellowing are the common symptoms. Though the disease occurs throughout the year, it is more severe during summer. Intermittent drying of stems and leaves occur at advanced stages of the disease. Fusarium sp. and Rhizoctonia sp. are associated with the disease.

Leaf spots and blights: Causal organism - Fusarium oxysporum
Symptoms: Necrotic brown spots (2-8mm) with central sunken area. Near by lesions join together to form bigger patches. Later form shot holes of irregular appearance severe infection causes blighting of leaves. Low temperature (18-22C and high humidity favour diseases incidence.)

Root rot and wilting: Complex disease, Causal organism - Fusarium oxysporum f. batatas var. Vanillae, Rhizoctonia sp.
Symptoms: Occurs in skin, root, shoot, tip, pods and leaves, diseases starts during rainy season as water soaked lesions surrounded by yellow hallow and later spreads to nearly are and infected portions die due to rotting, infection at collar and root causes foot rot and wilting of vine and also see vascular browning of stems in wilted plants and ultimately plants get detached from the soil.
Control: Spray Bavistin or Dithane M-45 200g/ 100L water apply one in 15-20 days intervals (2-3 sprays). Drench the base of the plant in case of foot rot and wilting. 

Brown spot (leaf spot): Causal organism - Colletotrichum gloeosporioides 
Control: Spray Bavistin or Dithane M-45 (0.2%) at, an interval of 15-20 days. Two to three rounds of application is advisable.

Storage diseases/insects: Penicillium spp. & Aspergillus spp. are two moulds affecting stored product. Mites also attack stored vanilla beams. Mould attack can be prevented by proper curing and hygienic storage condition. Mites can be controlled by fumigating the curing room by burning sulphur/drying the beams under sun.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Vanilla beans are mainly employed for food flavouring (among the food flavours, vanilla has a prime position). The essence is largely used in the preparation of ice creams, chocolates, bakery products, puddings, custards, pharmaceuticals, liquors and perfumes. With the advent of chemical technology to produce vanillin/ethyl vanillin, these synthetic substitutes have taken over the use of vanilla beans. However, natural vanillin is still the most preferred food flavour (6). Only a small portion of vanilla beans is consumed directly. 95% of the vanilla beans are processed into extracts. Dairy industry is the largest consumer of vanilla extracts and flavour. Confectionery, baking and beverage industries are the other industrial users. Ground beans are also used for flavouring confectionery products. Poorer quality is used to aromatize tobacco in Java.

Parts Used: Pod (fruit).


Harvesting Practices: The beans takes 9 to 10 months to reach full maturity after flowering and are harvested by hand picking or by cutting with knife. Best time is when the fruits are still dark green, with only the tip turning yellow and fine yellow streaks appear in the pods. Immature pods split during curing and hence not harvested. Daily picky of mature pods is essential over a period. The pods can be harvested. A good crop yields of 300-600 kg cured beans per hectare. About 6 kg of green produce yields 1kg of cured beans. It is also reported the yield of vines declines after 12-14 years. Matured bean will be 12-25 cm long with 2.5 cm circumference (5).    

                                                                                                                                                

Post Harvest Handling:
Beans are to be cured within a week after harvest. It consists of blanching, fermenting and drying, during which 70-80% of the water is lost and typical aroma develops (vanilla beans do not possess any flavour or aroma at the time of harvest). Curing involves a four phase process. A long term process involving cessation of vegetative life, sweating, rapid drying/slow drying and conditioning are required to produce quality beans. The whole process may take 2-3 months as per the traditional system. It is during curing that the beans undergo enzymatic reaction responsible for the characteristic aroma and flavour of vanilla. Curing should preferably begin immediately after harvest, but beans can be stored for 3-5 days. The important methods of curing vanilla are Mexican process, Peruvian process, Bourbon process, Indonesian process, Tahiti process and Guinea process. In Mexican process, killing is done by exposing the harvested beans directly to the sun for about 5 hours, which produce the optimum percentage of vanilla content. Of this Bourbon method is most commonly practiced. Which is very simple, gives the best quality beans and followed in most of the producing countries. Curing involves four phases.

Killing or wilting: Beans washed and cleaned of any material and sorted according to length based on maturity, size and splits. Load batch of 25 to 30 kg into cylindrical baskets.
* Stops vegetative development of fresh beans.
* Initiate enzymatic reaction responsible for aroma.
* Dip in hot water at 62-65C for 2-3 minutes or expose to sun for 4-5 hours.
* Dark brown colouration indicate the killing.

In the modified Bourbon process baskets with the beans are immersed in hot water (63-65C) for 3 minutes. When the beans are still hot they are taken out and wrapped in a woolen cloth (sweating box) or in dark cotton cloth lined sweating box or in an airtight wooden box for 24 hrs. Beans become chocolate brown colour the following day. Instead of hot water dipping, hot water vapour (steam) treatment also is practiced. Steam treatment is carried out for 12 hrs and this is reported to give a more uniform produce, but is more cumbersome.

Sunning and sweating: Beans are to be taken out from sweating box after 24 hrs and spread evenly in the blanket or on dark coloured cotton cloth for 3 to 4 hours under direct sunlight and later rolled up to retain heat and stored in wooden boxes. This process is repeated for 6 to 8 days during which beans lose some weight and become very supple. This sunning and sweating process has to be continued for one week to 10 days depending upon atmosphere temperature and humidity conditions and by the end of this process the beans will attain chocolate brown colour.

* Raising temperature of killed beans and allowing to sweat.
* Enhancing rapid drying to avoid harmful fermentation to promote enzymatic reaction. 

Slow drying: Beans are to be spread on wooden or steel racks in well ventilated rooms for 2-3 months. Process usually lasts for 15-20 days. But in method modified for our condition this period is much shorter than in Madagascar were slow drying is for about 7-10 days under a relative humidity higher than 70% for slow drying. After slow drying the beans become supple, dark brown with oily appearance, by which the beans become pliable or flexible and rolled on to fingers in a coil. Moisture is to be kept around 30% without any fungal attack in storage for beans for home consumption. Extraction industries prefer beans & with maximum moisture content of 20%, properly dried beans are kept in closed containers where the fragrance is fully developed. 

Conditioning and grading: Straighten bent beans by drawing against fingers. Sort the beans based on length and classify into unsplit, split and cut beans. Finally they are graded according to the size. Tie beans with black string into bundles of 50 and store in paraffin paper lined metal or wooden boxes. When properly cured and sun dried the vanilla beans will be almost black and supple enough to the bent without breaking and vanillin crystallize over the beans. Properly cured vanilla beans contain about 2.5% vanillin with moisture content of 20 to 25%. Bourbon vanilla beans are classified into six grades: prime, firsts, seconds, thirds, fourths, and splits. Prime beans are the perfect ones (moisture: 37.2%). Firsts are a degree lower in appearance. Seconds contain 25.5% moisture and are sweet flavoured. Thirds have about 13% moisture with good flavour but with blemished surface. Fourths are rich in resin, low in vanillin content. Splits are shrivelled and dry beans.
Note: Store beans in closed cloth lined boxes for 3 months to permit development of aroma and flavour.

Vanilla beans during curing and after curing have to be stored properly; else the quality will be affected. 
Spoilage of vanilla
* Penicillium and aspergillus infect during conditioning and storage.
* Infection starts from stem and to beans.
* Immature harvest, improper killing and excess moisture cause infection.
* Mites, Tyrophagus sp. infest and impart disagreeable odour.
* Limited infestation may be cured by alcohol treatment.

In the international market the quality is determined by way of vanilla is cured, thus the stage if largest and the method of curing play a major role in determining the quality of the produce. Aroma/flavour is the primary quality requirement. Other quality indicators include general appearance, flexibility, size and vanillin content. Appearance flexibility and size are directly related with aroma/flavour. Highly aromatic long, fleshy, supple, dark brown beans with oily appearance and a moisture content of 30-40% is considered as top quality beans (5).


Produce and Products: Today, all over the world, almost 90% of vanilla essence used is synthetic vanillin. The product extract of natural vanilla are: 
Vanilla extracts: Hydroalcoholic solution within minimum 35% ethyl alcohol.
Vanilla flavouring: Similar to vanilla extract, with less than 35% alcohol.
Vanilla tincture: Contain at least 38% alcohol (for pharmaceutical use).
Vanilla oleoresin: Semi-solid concentrate without solvent.
Vanilla powder: Pure mixture of vanilla powder or oleoresin with sugar, food starch and gum acacia.
Concentrated vanilla extract and concentrated vanilla flavouring: prepared by stripping off a part of the solvent from solvent extracts to concentrate the vanilla constituent content.
Perfumery vanilla tincture: maceration of vanilla beans with perfumery alcohol; ethyl alcohol content is 90%.
Vanilla absolute: obtained by direct alcohol extraction of beans followed by alcohol washing of an oleoresin. Most concentrated form of vanilla aroma (7-13 times stronger than good-quality beans!) (9).

Major types of vanilla:
1. Whole beans
2. Splits – Whole beans partially split open longitudinally from the blossom end owing to dehiscence.
3. Cuts – beans that have been cut into short lengths to accelerate the curing process, or portions of cured beans remaining after cutting away mouldy or damaged sections. Also include very small whole beans (9).

Quality Product: The Bourbon vanilla ranks tops in quality terms with following specifications :
Colour : Dark Brown to Black shining Red/Brownish to dark Brawn. 
Quality : Whole, Split 
Aspect : Oily, Sapple 
Length : 10 cm up to 12 cm: low grade 
13 cm up to 22 cm: Standard 
Above 22 cm : Top grade
Cuts - Chopped according to buyer demand. 
Vanillin Content : 1.8% to 2.4% 
Moisture Content : 16% to 28% (6).  

Major Product: 41 types of vanilla beans are there in the world market which differ in flavour profile, organaleptic and analytical properties due to growing conditions, harvesting and earning process. Among these the Bourbon vanilla stood top in terms of quality.
1. Bourbon vanilla (produced from Madagascar, Comoro & Reunion).
2. Java vanilla (produced from islands of Java in Indonesia).
3. Bourbon like vanilla (produced from islands of Bali in Indonesia).
4. Mexican vanilla (produced from Mexico).


Chemistry: Unique fragrance and flavour of vanilla is due to vanillin. Several secondary aromatic compounds along with vanillin are: volatile oil, fixed oil, resins, organic acids, tannins, sugar gum, wax, cellulose and water (9). Vanillin (C8H8O3) is the main chemical constituent responsible for the aroma and flavour. Properly cured beans will yield 2-2.5% vanillin. Vanilla flavour produced by vanillin is enhanced and modified by secondary aromatic compounds - consisting of a complex mixture of aromatic aldehydes, alcohols and esters. Vanilla beans from Tahiti contain heliotropin, which gives the distinctive flavour of thin vanilla. 

The four most abundant constituents are vanillin (about 2%), p-hydroxybenzaldehyde (0.2%), p-hydroxy benzyl methyl ether (0.02%) and acetic acid (0.02%). Schulte-Elte et al. (1978) have reported identifying two diastereoisomeric vitispiranes (2, 10, 10-trimethyl 1-6-methylidene-1-oxaspiro (4.5) dec-7-enes) at 1 ppm level in oleoresin and stated that these two were important contributors of vanilla aroma. 

The aromatic carbonyls are: vanillin, anisaldehyde, acetophenone, benzaldehyde, piperonal, and salicylic aldehyde. The aromatic alcohols are: anisyl alcohol, benzyl alcohol, coniferyl alcohol, phenyl ethyl alcohol, p-hydroxy benzyl alcohol and vanillyl alcohol. The aromatic acids include: anisic acid, cinnamic acid, salicylic acid, vanillic acid, etc. Aromatic esters include: anisyl acetate, benzyl acetate, benzyl cinnamate, furfuryl acetate, methyl benzoate, methyl vanillate, etc. Phenols and phenol esters include: anisole, p-cresol, eugenol, vanillin 2,3-butylen glycol acetal etc. Aliphatic alcohols include: Butan-2.3-diol, nonan-2-ol, dedecan-1-ol, heptan-1-ol etc. Aliphatic carbonyls include: acetaldehyde, diacetyl, heptan-2-one etc. The other constituents are aliphatic acids, aliphatic esters and lactones, aromatic hydrocarbons, terpenoids, aliphatic hydrocarbons and heterocyclics (9).  

The following names are also found in the literature for vanilla: 4-hydroxy-3methoxy-4-hydroxybenzaldehyde, methyl protocatechuic aldehyde, vanillic aldehyde

Appearance: White to off-white crystalline powder

Melting Point: 80-81˚C
Boiling Point: 285˚C 
Solubility: 1% in water at 20˚C, soluble in ethanol, DEP, Benzyl benzoate, aliphatic and aromatic esters, chlorinated solvents, acetic acid.

Other uses:

Medicinal uses
Traditionally used as carminative, aphrodisiac, emmenagogue and stimulant. Said to reduce or cure fevers, spasms and caries. Vanilla extracts are used in pharmaceutical preparations such as syrup, primarily as flavouring agent.
