Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Aleurites montana is a decidious tree, native to Subtropical parts of Southren China, Myanmar, Thailand and Indochina (type), also cultivated in Java and other tropical regions. Like Aleurites fodii this is also hill side species, but it can thrive in warmer climate and can stand heavier rainfall, provided the area is well drained. Grows well in alluvial soils and is not very exacting in its soil requirements, but in richer soils the growth is more vigorous. A slightly acid soil is preferable. pH of 5.5–8.0. Maximum temperature 35.5°C, minimum temperature 6°C. It is frost-tender, and does not require a low temperature (below 3°C) as tung-oil trees (A. fordii) do, so can be grown in warmer regions. In Assam, grown where rainfall is 175–275 cm annually, in Karnataka at elevations of 800–1,000 m with annual rainfall of 150 cm. Experimental cultivation in different parts of India has shown that this species is better suited to the Indian climate than A. fordii . Aleurites montana has been found to thrive better in Assam, Bengal and in the area around Ranchi. This species also gives a much better yield of fruits than A fordii.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A moderate sized up to 15-20 m tall, much-branched, partially deciduous tree. Leaves simple, spirally arranged, ovate or more or less cordate, acutely acuminate to slightly cuspidate, without a gland at the top, about 12 cm long, 10 cm broad, sometimes larger and 3-lobed, leaf-blade with 2 large, conspicuous glands at base, petiole up to 24 cm long. Petioles longer than lamina, lamina ovate, 5-7 veins from cordate or trunctate base, 2 stalked concave green nectaries at junction of petiole and lamina. Fruit is a drupe, flattened, ovoid to subglobose with rounded-truncate base, apiculate at the apex, surface wrinkled with 3 distinct longitudinal grooves and ridges and a few transverse ribs, 4-6 cm in diameter, sparingly pubescent, yellow. Tree posses white latex in all its parts. Generally two types of trees are recognised.

1. A-type clones in which the main trunk increases steadily in height as it develops succeeding tiers of primary branches, one above the other at some distance apart, takes 3-5 years to come into bearing.

2. B-type clones in which the main trunk loses its identity after producing first or second whorl of branches, secondaries arise at short intervals, trees low, dense and bushy in habit, a precocious bearer, coming into bearing in third year and giving high yields in first 6 years. Both types are relatively surface rooting.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Inflorescence usually unisexual, staminate ones many-flowered, 15-25 cm, pistillate inflorescence significantly smaller, 4-15 cm. Flowers very fragrant, white, reddish brown in the centre, fragrant, calyx green at the base, otherwise deep pink to pinkish yellow, lobes 10-15 mm, petals narrowly obovate-spathulate. Staminate flowers 15-35 mm in diameter, pedicel 7-12 mm long, disc glands subulate or cylindrical-fusiform, slenderly triangular in outline, very long and acute, 3-4.5 mm, somewhat fleshy, stamens 10-12, filaments pale light greenish to faintly, anthers 2-2.5 mm, light yellow. Pistillate flowers 20-30 mm in diameter, pedicel rather short, stout, 3-6 mm long, disc glands shorter and wider as in the staminate flowers, ovary obovoid or ellipsoid and angular, 3 locular, 4-5 mm, styles 5-10 mm long. Three types Inflorescences are found Viz.,

1. Male which emerge at tips of branches simultaneously with new leaves, corymbose, large with about 200 male flowers trees with this type are predominantly male and bear little fruit.

2. Female; in racemes at end of short leafy shoots of new season's growth, smaller and denser than male, about 20 flowers.

3. Mixed of 2 types:

-- on predominantly male trees with a few female flowers borne at end of mainly male inflorescence.

-- mainly on 'bearer' trees with predominance of female flowers and a few males in a subsidiary position. In both male and female flowers, males being smaller.

Breeding System :
Flowers are protogynous, female flowers opening before male on same tree, cross-pollinated, Predominantly male trees have a longer blossoming period than bearers, are usually the first to begin flowering.

Phenology :
Flowering starts from February-March and Fruits are produced from late September-early November.

Pollinators:
Entamophillous in nature. Honey bees are needed to transfer pollen from anthers to pistil. When staminate and pistillate flowers are on separate trees.

Seed Biology :
Fruits egg-shaped, 3-lobed, wrinkled, about 5 cm in diameter, pointed at summit, generally with 3 or 4 seeds, the pericarp thick, hard and weedy. Seeds broadly obovate in dorsal view, pointed at the micropylar end, 20-30 mm, conspicuously warty with rough testa, endosperm white and ridged dorsally and ventrally. Each seed consists of a kernel surrounded by a thin hard shell. The fruit consists of 4-5 seeds, each of which is enclosed by a thin inner hull and the whole surrounded by a fibrous outer hull.

Seedling Germination :
Fresh seeds usually give 75-90 per cent germination, which is reduced on storage unless stored stratified in moist sand in a cool place. On germination the radicle emerges and, when hypocotyl loop appears above surface, seedlings should be transplanted from germination beds to the nursery. Hypocotyl on straightening then pulls cotyledons above surface. Approximately 140 seeds per lb.

Cultivation:

Trees are propagated from seeds or by budding from high yielding clones. Seeds are usually planted in nursery and may take 2 to 3 months to germinate. When seedlings are about one year old, they are planted out, spaced 6.6 x 6.6 m or more. Cultural practices are similar to those for A. fordii. As soon as the seedlings emerge, a side dressing of fertilizer (5-10-5) of nitrogen and phosphorus along with commercial zinc sulphate should be applied. Fertilizer is applied at rate of 600 kg/ha, in bands along each side of row, 20 cm from seedlings and 5–7.5 cm deep. Other fertilizers may be needed depending on the soil. Most successful budding is done in late August, by the simple shield method, requiring a piece of budstick bark, including a bud, which will fit into a cut in the rootstock bark. A T-shaped cut is made in bark of rootstock at point 5–7.5 cm above ground level, the flaps of bark loosened, shield-bud slipped inside flaps and the flaps tied tightly over the transplanted bud with rubber budding strip 12 cm long and 0.6 cm wide. After about 7 days, rubber strip is cut to prevent binding. As newly set buds are susceptible to cold injury, soil is mounded over them for winter. When growth starts in spring, soil is pulled back and each stock cut back to within 3.5 cm of the dormant bud. Later care consists of keeping all suckers removed and the trees well-cultivated. Trees may be planted 125–750/ha. When trees are small, close planting in rows greatly increases the bearing surface, but at maturity the bearing surface of a crowded row is about the same as for a row with trees further apart. However, it is good to leave enough space between rows for orchard operations. In contour-planting, distances between rows and total number of trees per hectare vary; rows 10–12 m apart, trees spaced 3.3–4 m apart in rows, 250–350 trees/ha. Tops of trees must be pruned back to 20–25 cm at planting. As growth starts, all buds are rubbed off except the one strongest growing and best placed on the tree. A bud 5 cm or more below top of stump is preferred over one closer to top.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Similar to those that of Aleurites fodii

Pests:

Several species of scale insects particularly cottony cushion scale (lcerya purchasi) and oleander or ivy scale (Aspidiotus hederae), bollworm (Heliothis armigera) and coffee bean weevil (Araecerus fasciculatus) attack tung.

Diseases:

Tung is subject to a variety of leaf spotting diseases. One of the most serious is angular leaf spot, the parasitic stage Cercospora aleuritidis producing the spots. The saprophytic stage develops on the dead leaves and infection is reduced by destroying these. Thread blight (Corticium stevensii) can seriously reduce yields in infected orchards. Nut rot (Botryosphaeria ribis) attacks the fruits early in the season. Black canker rot (Physalospora rhodina) occurs principally on young trees. It is not advisable to replant tung where trees have been killed by Clitocybe root rot (Clitocybe tabescens).

Pruning out and burning of diseased branches is recommended.

Economic Potential and Uses

Aleurites montana seed oil is not at present a commercial product though it has the properties similar to those of Aleurites fordii. Tung oil which is usually a mixture of Aleurites fordii and Aleurites montana (about 10%) trade does not make any distinction between these due to their similar properties.

Kernels yield a valuable drying oil, used for caulking boats and junks, used in painting of wood work, in the admixture with natural resins in preparation of varnishes and linoleums. Used locally for illumination and lacquer-work. Varnish made from this plant possess a high degree of water-resistance, gloss and durability. It is also used for water proofing wood, paper and fabrics. At present it is extensively used, after suitable treatment, in the manufacture of paints, enamels, etc. It has become an important raw-material in the linoleium and water proofing industry and in the manufacture of electrical insulating varnishes and in the treatment of aeroplane fabrics and in the automobile industry (brake linings). The oil when burnt in an insufficient supply of air, yields carbon, an ingredient of Indian or Chinese ink.

Parts used

Seeds

Uses

The seeds have drastic purgative properties and can cause severe poisoning if eaten raw. The seed remnants are used as manure in China. Seed cake remained after extraction contains 20-25% protien and is used as fertilizer. Hulls are used in the field as mulch for tung or other crops e.g coffee, or in the pulvirized from used as a substiute for peat in potting composts. Used as a conditioner in mixed fertilizers.

Harvesting Practices
Trees begin bearing 2–5 years after transplanting with maximum production reached in 8 years and continuing for 40 years. In northern Burma, it has been observed to be more vigorous and disease-resistant than A. fordii. Fruits mature and drop to ground in late September to early November. At this time they contain about 60% moisture. Fruits must be dried to 15% moisture before processing. Fruits should be left on ground 3–4 weeks until hulls are dead and dry. Fruits are gathered by hand into baskets or sacks. In USA, Clean cultivation and mechanized harvesting is practiced. The harvesting machine sweeps or rolls fallen fruits into windrows, and then collects them by a pick up mechanism which includes suction device. A. montana is reported to give much higher yields of fruits than A. fordii. The percentage of kernels in the seeds is about 56%, and of oil in the kernels, about 59.3%.

Produce and Products

Oil of Industrial importance is obtained from the seeds. It is widely used as a dryer in varnishes and paints as tung oil polymerizes to a hard, waterproof gel that is highly resistant to acids and alkalies. It is also used as a component of insulating compounds and in the manufacture of linoleum and oilcloth.

Chemistry

Tung oil is composed primarily of eleostearic (elaeostearic) acid, with smaller amounts of oleic, linoleic and palmitic glycerides. Eleostearic acid is a crystalline unsaturated fatty acid that exists in 2 stereoisomeric forms: An alpha acid occurring as the glycerol ester especially in tung oil, and a beta acid obtained from the alpha acid by irradiation (9, 11, 13-octadecatrienoic acid) and this type is found in Aleurites montana. The chemical structure of tung oil eleostearic acid:
CH3(CH2)3CH=CHCH=CHCH=CH(CH2)7COOH

Processing Techniques

Pre-treatment:

When the fruit falls from the tree its moisture content is about 65%. It should be allowed to dry in the orchard to 25% the safe level for storage. The dried fruits are mechanically de-hulled. In China the fruits are kept moist in piles to loosen and soften the hulls, after which the nuts are removed by hand and sold, usually without drying.

Oil extraction:

Traditional methods of oil extraction in China involve drying over slow fires followed by milling in primitive stone mills. The meal is then steamed over hot water for a short while before being moulded into large cakes which are then pressed in a wedge press. The pressed meal is usually re-pressed after further grinding and heating.

In the USA the fruits are mechanically de-hulled and after drying passed to continuous oil expellers. The efficiency of extraction depend on the moisture content of the nuts and the percentage of shell present. The best yields are obtained with 4.2% moisture and 20% shell.

The crude oil is filtered, but is not usually subjected to alkali refining.

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

Tung oil has wide range of industrial significance. In India it has not attained much importance and is grown only in limited area. It can be alternatively used with edible oils so as to reduce the pressure on edible oils. Major producing countries are mainland China and South America (Argentina and Paraguay). United States and Africa much less than the others.

Miscellaneous Information

Improvement: The main aim is to increase the yield of good quality oil per acre. High yielding clones, which can be vegetatively propagated, have been selected from seedling material in Malawi and have given 100 per cent increase in yield over unselected seedling material. Hybridization of high yielding clones has been done to produce legitimate seedling families and selections from these are being made. Hybrids between A. montana and A.fordii have been produced but shown little merit.
