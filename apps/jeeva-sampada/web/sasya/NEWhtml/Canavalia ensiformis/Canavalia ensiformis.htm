HABIT & HABITAT
A genus of slender vines or stout lianas, found along the eastern part of India from Himalaya to Ceylon and Siam, wild or cultivated. Also distributed in the tropics and sub-tropics. About eight species occur in India of which two (Jackbean and Swordbean) are cultivated to some extent as vegetable, pulse and fodder. Swordbean is generally known under the botanical name C. ensiformis which includes the jackbean.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
An erect bushy annual, 1-2 m in height, native to. South and Central America, introduced and cultivated to a small extent in some parts of India. Leaves trifoliate, shortly hairy, leaflets up to 20 cm long, slightly coriaceous, ovate-elliptic, obtuse but often with a very short acute apex; flowers in racemes, rose or violet, fading towards the base; pods stout, straw-coloured, slightly compressed, pendent, 20-30 cm long, 2-2.5 cm broad, 8-20 seeded; seeds white, oblong, moderately compressed, hilum 8 mm long, running to almost half the length of the seed.

Cultivation:
Jack bean is occasionally cultivated in kitchen gardens in almost all parts of India except in the MSL above 1,200 m, for vegetable or pulse. It is allowed to trail over the roofs of huts and dwellings. Sometimes it is grown on borders of fields also. Its cultivation is recommended for rehabilitating exhausted soils, and as a cover crop to prevent soil erosion and leaching of the nitrates from the top soil. The dead leaves make good compost; the crop is also grown for green manure.
Sowing and Season
The seeds are sown direct in any good garden soil in broad pits, 7.5 cm deep and 30 cm apart, and with 1.5 m between rows. Sowing in the plains as well as in the hills is done from the middle of April to the end of June. Manuring is not essential, but if some decomposed manure is dug into the ground before sowing, it benefits the crops. The plants may be watered once in 10 days in the dry weather. Inoculating the crop with nodule bacteria is beneficial in tropical regions.
When the plants are a few centimeters high, the ground should be weeded, and strong bamboo sticks inserted on either side of the rows for the support of the vines. Later they may be trained over a pandal or a roof. No further attention is needed except weeding between the rows occasionally and replacing such supports as may give way during the course of the season. The crop is ready in 3-4 months. It bears abundantly from the middle of the rainy season through winter.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:  
The vegetable mite, Tetranychus cucurbitae Rahman & Sapra, and aphids infect the jackbean 
Diseases:
Several fungi have been reported to parasitize the jackbean. Sclerotium rolfsii Sacc. causes a collar rot and pink rot while Colletotrichum capsici (Syd.) Butler & Bisby causes seedling blight which may be controlled by spraying with Bordeaux mixture. Cercospora canavaliae Syd. infects the leaves. Fruit rot caused by Fusarium sp. and Trichothecium roseum Link ex Fr. And dark grey spots on fruits due to Alternaria tenuis Necs and Coleophoma empetri (Rostr.) Patrak have been recorded on jackbean. Besides, mottling of pods caused by sword bean mosaic virus has been reported in Marathwada. Glomerella lindemuthianum. Shear causes the anthracnose disease. Using healthy seeds, practicing crop rotation, growing resistant varieties and spraying 0.2 per cent Ziran are some of the control measures to check this disease.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Pods, Seeds, Whole plant, Bark.

Uses
  1. The young pods and immature seeds of jack bean make an excellent vegetable. They can also be pickled. As a vegetable, they are a substitute for French beans and are eaten with meat
  2. The dried ripe beans are used as a pulse and are a rich source of proteins but their use as human food is limited because of the presence of some growth inhibiting factors. These growth inhibiting factors are destroyed on heat processing thereby improving the biological value of the proteins. 
  3. The beans can be used for the production of protein concentrates which may find use in the preparation of several food formulations. In Africa, the dried roasted seeds are used as coffee-substitute.
  4. Bark is used by the Santals in fever.

Harvesting Practices
The crop is ready in 3-4 months. The pods are generally in best; condition for table during Oct.- Nov. The crop is harvested for pulse when the pods are brown and mature. The crop can be cut with mower and windrowed, but generally the pods are picked by hand or the plants pulled and stacked in piles to dry. Later, threshing is done by beating the dried plants or pods, and flailing out the seeds.
Yield
The yield of seed in India is 800-1,000 kg/ha whereas in Zimbabwe it is 2,000- 2,500 kg/ha. The crop yields 16-20 tones/ha of green matter when grown for green manure.
