Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

A tall hardy annual cereal grain, of the grass family, resembling wheat and barley. Rye is native to temperate Eurasia where it is mostly used as a bread grain (mixed with other grains) and as a livestock feed. And in India it is cultivated for grain to a small extent in Ladakh. Lahul and other north-western Himalayan areas and as host for the cultivation of medicinal ergot in Kashmir.

Distribution: Probably native to southwestern Asia, but now widely cultivated in the temperate regions of the world.
(1, 5,6)
Availability :  

Status : Cultivated.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

Tall, hardy, tufted annual grass, 1–1.5 m tall, with blue-green cast, culms slender, erect, glabrous overtopping the foliage, glaucous, except pubescent near the spike, the spikes are 3 to 5 inches long, slender and awned; leaves many, soft, 1.2 cm or less broad, smooth or slightly scabrous, long-pointed; leaf-sheaths long and loose; ligule short and jagged. (1, 5,6)

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

The rye plant is characterized by slender seed spikes which is terminal, 7.5–15 cm long, curved, much-awned, slender, compact and consist of spikelets with two or more florets, spikelets generally contain two fertile flowers. The florets, which have rows of barbs and long terminal beards, or awns, lemmas long and narrow, tapering into awns, Prominently keeled. (1, 5,6)

Breeding System :

The cultivated ryes are thought to have been derived, by selection from wild perennial weed types which arose by hybridization in central and south-western Asiatic regions between two or more ancestral species like S. segetale, S. ancestrale, S. anatolicum and S. montanum. Interspecific hybridization with other Secale species is fairly easy and the fertility of resultant hybrids is relatively high. The objectives of such hybridization have been the breeding of hardy perennial types giving a higher yield and better quality grain and fodder. (1, 5,6)



Phenology :

Rye is essentially a cold weather crop. It is the most adaptable of the cereals and produces satisfactory crops in regions of severe winter temperatures and at high altitudes. It can be grown as a winter crop in the North Indian plains where the average winter temperature does not go above 25° C. Trial cultivation in the environs of Calcutta shows that it can tolerate humid conditions. (1, 5,6)

Pollinators:

It is a cross-pollinated crop and the pollinators are the insects. (1)

Seed Biology :

Seeds are enclosed in the palea and lemma, as in wheat, but tend to protrude when near ripe, so are less completely enclosed than in wheat. kernel oblong, 0.8 cm long, light brown, narrowly grooved on face, short-pointed, glabrous. (1, 5,6)

Seedling Germination :

Cultivation:

Soil: Among cereals, rye is the least extracting in its soil requirements and can thrive well on light sandy or loamy soils that are too poor for any other crop, but it yields best on more fertile soils. It requires adequate drainage and can withstand considerable soil acidity and alkalinity and can, therefore, fill a gap in marginal cereal soils.

Planting Season: In the northern plains of India, like Jammu, rye can be planted along with wheat and barley. As a grain crop it can be planted at any time between the last week of September and the last week of November, but for production of ergot it is sown from the last week of September to last week of October. In the temperate climate of Kashmir valley it is planted during October- November, and the germinating seed remains dormant till March, when the crop starts growing again to be ready for harvest in July.

 

Sowing method and Seed rate: Seed is broadcasted or drilled at seed rate of 40–68 kg/ha.

 

Irrigation: Two irrigations are required in the conditions of Jammu area but no irrigation is required in the Kashmir valley.

 

Nutrition: Responds to fertilization, but will grow on soils of limited fertility. Nitrogen and phosphorus required on sandy soils. Nitrogen at 40-60 kg./ha. is applied in two split doses, one at planting and the other during growth of the crop.

 

Cropping system: Intercropped with wheat in some countries. (1, 5,6)

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests: (1, 5)

Some of the more important insect pests of rye include: Barley thrips (Limothrips denticornis), Aster leafhopper (Macrostoles fascifrons), Armyworm (Pseudaletia unipuncta), and Apamea sordens.

Diseases: (1, 5)

  • The most serious disease of rye is ergot (Claviceps purpurea).

  • Blight caused by Gibberella saubinetii (Mont).

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used and Uses  

Grain: Baked products made with rye flour have a distinctive flavor. As feed, rye is not relished by livestock, so rye grain is usually fed in mixtures with other cereals. Substantial quantities of rye are also used for making distilled alcoholic beverages. Roasted grains substitute for coffee.

Leaves and stem: Rye is also used as hay, straw, silage and as green manure; Straw used as packing material for nursery stock, bricks and tiles, for bedding, paper manufacture, archery targets, and mushroom compost. (1, 5,6)

Harvesting Practices

The crop is harvested when the grains harden and the straw becomes dry and brittle. The rain-fed crop reaches the harvest stage much earlier than the irrigated crop. Most of the harvesting in India is done with the sickle. The grain is generally threshed by treading with cattle on a threshing-floor. The use of a stone roller, saw threshers and tractors expedite the operation. Winnowing is done generally with the winnowing baskets.  (1, 5)

Produce and Products  (1, 5, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 16, 17, 18, 26-30)

  • Flour: Used in making roti/chappati from atta.

  • Alcoholic beverages: rye whisky is made by fermenting rye grains.

  • Rye starch: Extracted from rye grains as done for other cereals.

Processing Techniques   

The cereal starches, such as maize, wheat, rice and sorghum, are recovered by several processes, of which the wet-milling is by far the most important. Other processes commercially employed in the manufacture of unmodified starches are the alka1i process.


In all the above processes, starch is recovered in five stages: (i) softening or steeping of the grains, after thorough washing in water, (ii) glinding and sieving of the steeped grains, (iii) tabling or centrifugation of the ground mass, (iv) dewatering and drying of the final products, and (v) the recovery of important byproducts.


Wet-milling process-This process is universally adopted for the manufacture of all types of cereal starches. It makes possible a complete separation of the major components of the cereal kernels.

The raw material comes to the wet-milling plants in the form of shelled grains. The grains are screened to remove all large and small pieces of grains, chaff, sand and other foreign matter.


The screened grains are steeped in steeping conical staves in which water and sulphur dioxide are sent in. Sulphur dioxide helps in the prevention of the growth of putrefactive organisms, dispersion of proteins and in maximizing the recovery of starch. During steeping, washwater from a previous operation, concentrated to attain a soluble level of 0.10-0.20 per cent, is sent counter-currently through the steeping staves. The steep water coming out of the staves contains 40-80 g/l of total solubles and is sent to concentration plants for preparing animal feeds, mixed feeds, or fermentation nutrients.

 

After steeping, the grains are crushed in attrition mills and the pulverized grains are sent through a set of hydroclones under pressure. The heavier endosperm and fibre particles pass out of the bottom of the hydroclones, while the lighter germ is drawn off the top of the vortex.

 

The underflow from the hydroclones is sized in screens and ground in Buhrstone or Entroleter impact mills. The pulverized material is passed through screen pumps where fibres are removed as continuous mats which are washed with water, slurried, squeezed and dried. The defibred pulp, known as mill starch, is concentrated in centrifuges where protein is removed as a overflow and starch is obtained from the underflow. The starch stream is passed through a series of hydroclones to reduce the protein content to the minimum level of 0.3 per cent. In the last hydroclone, the starch is washed with softened fresh water. The starch milk from the cyclones is dewatered on moving belt filters or in basket centrifuges, and dried in flash driers.


Alkali process- In this process, the proteins in the cereal grains are dispersed by an aqueous solution of caustic soda or ammonium hydroxide. All cereal starches can be manufactured by this process. The cereal grains are crushed and sieved, the flour is slurried with an aqueous solution of ammonium hydroxide, or the grains themselves are directly soaked in a dilute aqueous solution of caustic soda in large vats. The alkaline slurry is screened in vibrating screens, the underflow is fed into a series of centrifuges where the separation of starch from the proteins occurs. The starch milk from the centrifuges is washed with water, filtered in vacuum filters and tunnel-dried. The protein solution from the centrifuges is dried in spray driers.

 

Martin process- It is primarily meant for producing starch from wheat flour and is in use in some of the European countries and in USA. Low grade wheat flour is made into a stiff dough by mixing it with water in proper proportions (5 wheat: 2 water) and the dough is hydrated for an hour in hydrating tanks. The hydrated dough is either extruded or rolled through a series of fluted rolls into flat ribbons or kneaded in a trough with reciprocating rolls under a spray of water. The extruded ribbons of the dough are cut to small pieces, the pieces sized in shaking screens under a continuous spray of water. Alternatively, the kneaded dough is washed with water, screened and slurried in Water. The starch slurry, obtained from both the cases, are purified in gravity separation tables or continuous centrifuges of the modern design. The centrifuged starch is then washed with water, filtered and dried in the, conventional equipments. It is reported that about 45-55 per cent of first quality starch, 10-20 per cent of inferior grade starch, and 10-15 per cent of gluten on the weight of the wheat fed are obtained in this process.

Batter process -This process is also employed essentially for the manufacture of starch from wheat flour. Wheat flour and water in proper proportions are sent through a series of ribbon blenders and from where an elastic, free-flowing, smooth batter or dough is obtained. The batter is mechanically broken down by hot water at 48-57 °C and sent to cutting pumps where the starch is washed with cold water. A partial separation of starch and gulten is achieved in the cutting pumps. The partially separated mass sent through a set of vibrating and gyrating screens under a spray of water. More starch is separated by this operation and the gluten is obtained in the form of curds. The starch slurry is then sent through several centrifuges in which complete separation of starch and gluten is achieved. .The starch mass is washed with water, dewatered and dried In tunnel, or roll, or spray or flash driers. The gluten fraction is washed with water, dewatered, cut into small pieces and dried in vacuum ovens or flash dryers.


MODIFIED STARCHES

Natural starches are the basic source material for the manufacture of all types of modified starches. The modified starches may be classified into innumerable groups, such as thick boiling starches and thin boiling starches; or acetylated, esterified, etherified and hydrolyzed starches; or textile, laundry and paper starches and so on. The modified starches are categorized here into (i) dextrins, (ii) acid-modified starches, (iii) oxidized starches, (iv) pregelatinized starches, and (v) starch derivatives.


Dextrins

Known popularly as starch gum, white dextrins, canary dextrins, or the British gum, the dextrin is an intermediate colloidal substance formed during the hydrolysis of starch. It is marketed as a yellow or a ; white powder, or as granulated particles, or as thick brown coloured liquids, or as a white, viscous paste. Dextrins are prepared from unmodified starches and are named after the starch from which they are derived.

The dextrins are commercially prepared by two methods, viz. dry process and wet process. In the dry process, raw starch is directly heated to 200-250°C In steam, oil, or fuel heated roasters. During dextrinization, the colour of the original starch changes to yellow and brown and finally the entire mass is charred. At 150-80°C, a series of low viscosity canary dextrins are obtained, and at above 195°C a series of British gums are produced. In some manufacturing plants, small quantities of hydrochloric acid are added to the raw starch for bringing down the temperatures of dextrinization. In the wet process, dextrins are produced by acid hydrolysis, enzymic action, or by the action of Bacillus macerans on the raw starch. In actual operation, an aqueous suspension or a paste of starch is heated with a trace of acid or an enzyme and after sufficient degree of conversion is accomplished the excess acid is neutralized, the mixture filtered and the product dried. Only hydrochloric acid, nitric acid and formic acid are commercially used in this process. The roasted or the dried product is milled, sieved and packed.


Acid-modified starches

These starches are prepared by treating an aqueous suspension of starch with a dilute solution of an acid at subgelatinizing temperatures (50-55°C). The acids used for the hydrolysis of starch are hydrochloric, nitric, sulphuric, formic, acetic, propionic, and phosphoric acids. In modern industrial practices, it is usual to employ a mixture of hydrochloric and sulphuric acids. The raw Starch is treated with the acid until the desired viscosity change is obtained. The resulting slurry is then neutralized with sodium carbonate, and the acid modified starch is recovered by filtration, washing and drying.


Oxidized starches

These starches are produced by treating raw starch with oxidizing agents such as sodium hypochlorite, periodate, nitrogen dioxide, chlorine, potassium permanganate, potassium dichromate, and ozone. Commercial oxidized starches have been produced mostly from sodium hypochlorite-starch reaction, or to some extent by periodate or nitrogen dioxide-starch reaction. The effect of the other oxidizing agents on starch has been studied only at the laboratory levels.


Hypochlorite oxidized starches

A slurry of refined starch at a concentration of 20-24° Be is introduced into a reaction tank equipped with impellers for mixing the starch with chemicals. A dilute solution of sodium hypochlorite is let into the tank until the reacting solution has a pH of 8-10. As the reaction proceeds, the value of pH goes down and more quantities of the hypochlorite solution are added to maintain the original level of pH. The temperature of reaction is maintained in the range of 21-38°C. The reaction tanks are provided with a mechanical cooling device since the reaction is exothermic. When the desired amount of oxidation has been achieved, the oxidized starch is neutralized or made slightly acidic (pH 6-6.5). A solution of sodium bi-sulphite or sulphur dioxide gas is added to the treated starch to remove free chlorine present in the reaction system. The oxidized starch is finally washed in continuous vacuum filters and dried in hot air driers.


Periodate oxidized starches

These starches, known commercially as dialdehyde starches, are produced by the oxidation of the reducing and non-reducing endgroups of the starch by periodic acid. Periodic acid is manufactured by electrolysing an aqueous solution of iodine in caustic soda in electrolytic cells containing iron cathodes and lead dioxide coated lead anodes. The periodic acid is reacted with starch in polyethylene, or PVC, or glass-lined reactors. After the desired level of oxidation is achieved, the oxidized starch is washed with water, centrifuged and dried. The spent oxidants from the centrifuges are again recycled into the electrolytic cells, generating the periodic acid.


Nitrogen dioxide oxidized starches

These are prepared by reacting raw starches with liquid nitrogen dioxide in carbon tetrachloride at room temperature, or with the vapours of nitrogen dioxide, or with a mixture of sodium nitrite and nitric acid. Low temperature oxidation with a high concentration of nitric acid promotes carbonyl group formation, while the exactly opposite conditions give rise to non-uronyl carboxyl group formation.


Pregelatinized starches

This group of modified starches includes a variety of starches obtained by precooking natural, modified or derivated starches on hot rolls with or without the addition of chemical reagents. The starches have the common property of marked swelling on contact with cold water, and form colloidal dispersions reflecting the characteristics of the raw materials from which they are derived.

Starch derivatives .

The term starch derivatives is applied to compounds evolved by the reaction of starch with other chemicals. The derivatives of starch outlined in this section include acetates, phosphates, carbamates and sulphonates of starch, hydroxyethyl starch, other types of esters and ethers, cationic starches, and cross-linked starches. (2,3,9,10,12,14,31)
