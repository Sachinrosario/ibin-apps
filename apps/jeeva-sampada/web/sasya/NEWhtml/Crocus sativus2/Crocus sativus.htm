Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

A short perennial herb with globular underground corms and six or more radial narrow and linear leave. Saffron, the most expensive spice in the world, is derived from the dry stigma of the saffron Crocus sativus L. 

Climate and soil
In India saffron is grown under rain-fed conditions in Kashmir, with an annual rainfall of 1000-1500 mm, an altitude of 1600 m above MSL and in rich well drained sandy or loamy soil. Spring is the favourable season for corm production while the rain immediately before flowering encourages high flower yield (1).

Availability :

Cultivated in temperate regions of Asia, South and Central Europe, South America and southern hemisphere (New Zealand).

Conservation/ Threat Status : Cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

A short perennial herb of 10-30 cm with globular underground corms and six or more radial narrow and linear leaves and has a corm-tunic finely fibrous of 1.5 to 5 cm diameter; the fibres reticulate. Corm which attains the size of 1.5 to 5 cm in diameter. It is raised from corms and no seed production. Plant remains dormant from May to August. Mother corms reproduce annually and give rise to 3 to 7 cormlets. Corms formed in a year produce flowers during the following year. The mother corms provide food to the newly developing corms die finally replacing the old corms every year. It starts flowering between the last week of October and 1st week of November.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Pungent smelling bluish violet flower are borne singly and their strikingly dark red or orange trifed funnel shaped stigma is used as spice. Pistil bears elongated pale yellow exerted filiform style, divided at the top in the brilliant orange red trifed stigma of 25-30 mm length. It is a sterile triploid cultigen, possibly selected from C. cartwrightianum Herbet, of Greek origin. CUTLIVATION ASPECT:
Chromosome number
Autotriploid with irregular meosis. 2n=24.

Selection of planting material:
Corms are the planting material. Corms for planting to be 1.5cm above in diameter clean the outermost loose covering of the corms before planting and should have 15% moisture. Dip in 5% copper sulphate solution before planting to protect from soil borne disease.

Planting: 
Two rows of corms are planted in furrows of 12-15 cm depth. The distance between furrows is 25-30 cm. The space between the corms is 10 cm and between rows is 8-10 cm. Planting season is July-September. Large amount of organic manure are incorporated into the soil before planting the corms (1). Farming operation at Pampore starts in August-September where bulbs are transplanted in raised plots in the pulverized loose loamy soil and surrounded by drains. In Kishtwar, where the soil is relatively lighter and well drained, the bulbus are planted on flat ground. The bulbs help in vegetative propagation. The crustiy soil around each bulb is loosened some time in the spring itself to facilitate the emergence of the stalk, bearing the large, scented lavender blue or lilac crocus with its delicate petals encircling three yellow stamens and three red styles with stigmas. By late autumn, as the neighboring Himalayas receive the first sprinkling of snow, the costliest crop in the world is ready for harvest. At Pampore, as elsewhere in the world, the purple flowers of saffron bloom from late October till mid-November. The flowers are similar to those of the common spring crocus, and the stigmas, protruding from the perianth, are flaming orange-red. The flowers rarely bear fruit.

Relatively low annual rainfall of about 35-45 cm is desirable heavy rain at bloom time or an early autumnal frost can be disastrous. The crop is, therefore, successful only in relatively cold regions with a warm or subtropical climate. Interestingly, the bulbs continue the cycle for 10 to 15 years, new bulbs being produced annually while the old ones rot. About 125 mounds of bulbs are required to plant one hectare. In well-conditioned soils they multiply to about 165 mounds in three years and to almost double their weight in about 10 years. Following a rather sluggish period in the first two years the crocus growth reaches its peak by the third year and after and after six year the bulb is uprooted for subsequent planting elsewhere, leaving the ground fallow for a considerable length of time (almost five years). The overall yield per hectare is relatively small at Pampore (it is even smaller at Kishtwar) – just about 160 kg of fresh flowers, corresponding to 5 kg of dried saffron. However, in some countries the yield is as high as 12.5 kg a hectare, depending on the crop management. Generally the yield will be the highest between the third and the third and the sixth year. corms planted once, last for six to seven years before become uneconomic. Afterwards the corms are removed screened and a fresh planted for new crop (3).

Management after planting:
Fertilizers used are mixture of potassium sulphate and ammonium nitrate. If planting is in dry season, irrigation is provided in March, April and August, as practiced in Spain (dry farming) (1). Requirement of FYM is highly essential for the crop. 15-20 t/ha of FYM is required for higher floral production. FYM is to be incorporated to soil during ploughing which in turn improve the soil texture and water holding capacity under rainfed condition. The estimated fertilizer request of saffron is 20 kg N, 80 kg P and 30 kg K per hectare, given as basal where Nitrogen is applied in two equal spits viz. fist ½ in the first week of September and the other half in 3rd week of November (2).

Weeding and inter-cultivation: Usually the planted fields are undisturbed till following June. First hoeing is carried out during June. The hoeing operation is very essential to provide soil aeration and highly essential for proper development of corms. Seed hoeing is done in early September and is accomplished by light dressing of FYM @ 4t/ha. In the subsequent years as long as the crop remains to promote growth of flowery shoots.
Inter-cropping and rotation: In Kashmir saffron field are profitably inter cropped with almonds in the juvenile stage because of the feature of leaf shady by almonds before saffron put forth new growth which will not hinder the sunlight to saffron plants. As soon as the saffron is harvested before mid-November, crops like about, linseed or others may be planted in saffron fields which are harvested invariably by mid June so as to facilitate first dissay of saffron crop with out affecting the saffron yield (3).

MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:

Vole (field mouse) and rabbit in some countries. Controlled by fumigation of their burrows and using traps (1).

Diseases:
Fungi such as Rhizoctonia and Sclerotinia (Phoma) are disease causing organisms. Controlled by dipping corms in fungicide before planting and using raised beds to improve drainage (1).

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
In ancient times saffron was an important dye, but nowadays used mainly in cooking and colouring foods, especially Spanish rice (paella), bouillabaisse and in Cornwall, traditional saffron cakes, loaves, cream or cottage cheese, chicken and meat, fish dishes, rice, mayonnaise, continental dishes, particularly French bouillabaisse and the rice chicken dishes, liquors and cordials. Spanish, French and Italian cuisines favour use of saffron. Christmas breads and buns using saffron are traditional in Sweden. Saffron cakes are of specialty in England. It is an essential commodity in high-quality, milk-cream-based confectioneries and Mughlai dishes in India, imparting a rich colour and distinctive flavour. Average use of this spice in weddings in Rajasthan and Gujarat is about 250 g (middle class family). The yellow pigment of saffron crocin is an excellent colouring agent which makes it a popular ingredient of chews, pudding, confectionery, a host of Indian and continental dishes and even in medicines. Saffron oil obtained by distilling the dried stigma is the most expensive of all essences. Slightly bitter saffron powder has been used in certain cakes and buns and in some alcoholic beverages, various tobacco preparations pan masalas besides Unani and Ayurvedic medicines. In India saffron is usually available as fine loose threads, but occur in the form of cakes consisting of the stigmas and parts of the styles which have been sweated and pressed and as hay consisting of dried stigma alone.

The high price of saffron has paved way for use of synthetic dyes instead of the crop. Saffron is also used in perfumery and in cosmetics. It is used together with sandalwood to produce the “Zaafran Attar” in the Middle East. It is a perfume ingredient in many famous perfume brands and also used in some types of incense (1).

Parts Used: Dried stigma


Harvesting Practices: Hand harvested at the flowering season (end of October or beginning of November). The stigmata are picked on the same day of harvest (1). Flowers remain open only during the first half of the day and the picking should start early. The job requires a high level of skill and is indeed laborious as about 1.5 million flowers are required for a kg of the spice. Hand picking is only a part of the process (2).

Post Harvest Handling
The delicate styles and stigmas are to be carefully dried in the mild autumn sun; and some times various remaining parts of the flowers are preserved individually for highly lucrative adulteration later on, usually justified on the grounds of producing relatively less expensive grades to satisfy market demands. The major elements for adulteration are the styles, stamens, anthers and parts of the corolla or as in saffron cake, some times safflower florets, particles of coloured wax, corn silk, mari gold petals and even shredded meat. But despite wide spread adulteration, the ever-growing demand for the exotic spice is nowhere on the wane. Finest quality of saffron, “shahi saffron” is obtained from the red tips or the stigmas borne on the styles. Stigmas are sun dried or less desirably, dried over low charcoal fire. After this the product is packed immediately, preferably in tin containers. Instead of drying the stigma alone, whole flowers are also dried in the sun for three to five days and then beaten lightly with sticks. Entire material is then passed through course sieves into water. Heavier stigma products sink to the bottom and when dried subsequently constitute the best quality Mogra saffron. Process is repeated several times and the sunken product is retrieved and dried before being packed in tin containers for marketing. Floral parts that remain and are subsequently retrieved from water are relatively inferior and less expensive. But even these are much more expensive than many other spices. Quality and quantity of the saffron (or Kong, as it is locally called) varieties vary with the method of extraction and treatment. Pick the flowers on alternate days during hours. Stigma along with style is pinched out from the flowers and is placed for immediate air drying. About 1.5 million flowers are required to produce one kg of good quality of dried saffron. A kg of the spice has a retail price of about Rs. 12, 0000 (3).

Drying stigmata by gently toasting in a silk sieve over the embers of a charcoal fire, is also practiced. Dried stigmata are stored in paper, cloth or plastic containers (McGimpsey 1993; Alonso et al. 1998) (1).  
ISI Specification: ISR recognizes two grades of saffron as “Special” and “Studad” based on colour, floral waste content and foreign matter.
Special – Deep red colour, floral waste content – 5%, foreign matter - 0.5%.
Studad – Light reddish to bright red, floral waste content – 10 %, foreign matter – 1.0%.


Produce and Products: Dried stigma and style. The trifed stigma along with style constitutes the saffron of commerce. Saffron oil is also a product obtained from saffron. 


Chemistry: Major components for colouring saffron are cis and trans crocins. Crocins are unusual water soluble carotenoids. With sulphuric acid the red colour changes to blue (polychroit). The molecular formula of crocin (digentiobiosyl ester of crocetin) is C44H64O24, which is a bis-(6-O-β-D-glucopyranosyl-β-D-glucopyranoside) ester of crocetin (= di-(β-gentiobiosyl)-crocetin), C20H24O4 a carotenoid 8,8’-diapo-Ψ,Ψ’-carotendioc acid (trans crocetin). In addition there are some other esters (all-trans and 13-cis isomers) of crocetin like Crocin A, Crocin B, Crocin C, Crocin D, Crocin E, Crocin F, Crocetin and Dimethylcrocetin. Crocins are produced from the glucose derivative of zeaxanthin (all-trans-β-carotene-3, 3’-diol, C40H56O22) named protocrocin, the substance responsible for the bitter taste of saffron.

Picrocrocin or saffron bitter C16H2607 (R-4-(β-D-glucopyranoxyloxy)-2,6,6-trimethyl-1-cyclohexene-1-carboxaldehyde), is responsible for the bitter taste of saffron. Safranal (C10H14O), principal component responsible for the aroma, is obtained by hydrolysis and dehydration of Picrocrocin. Safranal corresponds to 2,6,6-trimethyl-1,3-cyclohexadiene-1-carboxaldehyde (=β-dehydrocyclocitral). The essential oil obtained by hydrodistillation of saffron contains safranal as the main constituent and many other cyclohexane derivatives. Enzymatic hydrolysis of picrocrocin with a β-glucosidase, produces 4-OH-β-cyclocitral that gives safranal by dehydration. The other components are protein (10-14%), sugars (13-14%), starch (6-7%), gums and dextrin (9-10%), pentoses (6-7%), ash (5-8%), fibre (4-5%), volatile oil (0.8%), fatty oil (8-13%) with glycerin esters of palmitic, stearic, lauric and oleic acids; 56-138 γ/g vitamin B2, 0.7-4.0 γ/g vitamin B1, xanthophylls, carotenes (α, β and γ), lycopine and zeaxanthin; Ca (111 mg), Fe (11.1 mg), P (252 mg), Na (148 mg), K (1724 mg). Saffron is the richest known source of vitamin B-2 (Alonso ad Salinas 1994; Duke 1985). Low temperature (0˚C and -17˚C) and low moisture (0%) are favourable for saffron storage as picrocrocin and crocin are easily oxidized by direct contact with air. The colouring strength and bitter taste of dehydrated saffron are 5 times more than fresh saffron (Alonso et al. 1990; 1993) (1).

Quality: According to ISO standards, the maximum values obtained from spectrometric studies for saffron are: colouring components (crocins at 440 nm), bitter constituents (picrocrocin at 257nm) and volatile fragrances (safranal at 330 nm). Fine or superior saffron is defined as follows: stigmata much longer than the united styles, with an intense red colour and maximum floral waste matter 4%. The specifications according to ISO standards: total ashes (w/w) - 8%, moisture and volatile oil (w/w) – 12%, insoluble ashes in acids (w/w) – 1.0 and 1.5%, water solubility (w/w) – 65%, crude fibre (w/w) – 6%, total nitrogen (w/w) – 3.0% (1).

Other uses: Medicinal uses
Ebers papyrus (1550 BC) mentions saffron as an ingredient in a cure for kidney problems. Hippocrates, Theophrastus and Galen considered it to be an appetite and stimulant, an aid for easing digestive disorders and praised its calming effect on infants (Basker and Negbi 1983). It often a quoted folk remedy for various types of cancer (Duke 1985). Extracts of saffron inhibits cell growth of human tumor cells. Crocetins reduce the cholesterol levels in blood. In small doses it is considered as anodyne, antihysteric, antiseptic, antispasmodic, aphrodisiac, balsamic, cardiotonic, carminative, diaphoretic, ecbolic, emmenagogue, expectorant, nervine, sedative, stimulant and stomachic (Duke 1985). In India it is used in ayurvedic medicines which heal a variety of diseases ranging from arthritis to impotency and infertility. It is employed to cure asthma and cough, useful in colds, treat alcoholism, acne and skin disorders. It is also used for baldder, kidney and liver aliments and also for cholera (Baby brand saffron 1999 - http://www.Babysaffron.com/gis.htm). It is mixed with ghee for diabetes. In Indian Unani medicine it is used to reduce inflammation, for treatment of enlarged liver and infection of bladder and kidneys. Useful in menstrual disorders, strengthening heart and is a refrigerant for brain. It is diuretic if soaked overnight with water and administered with honey. It is mixed with cinnamon, orange peel, rose petals, honey and egg yolk and employed to restore strength of the body. Overdoses (>5g) are narcotic, can cause apoplexy and extravagant gaiety and corms are toxic to young animals (Baby brand saffron 1999; Litter 1975; Boisvert and Aucante 1993; Duke 1985) (1). Useful in hepatomegaly, vitiated conditions of kapha in children, epilepsy, inflammations, vitiated conditions of vāta and pitta, nephropathy, splenopathy and skin diseases. As a facial cream it is very specific for decolouration of the skin (5).

Economic potential to the country

Economic potential:India is a major saffron cultivating country along with Spain, Iran, Greece, China and Morocco. In Kashmir the yield amounts to 1.5-3.0 kg/ha (average) (1).  
Area under cultivation: 5707 ha.
Major states where the crop cultivated: Kishtawar of district Doda in Jammu and in the district of Pulwama, Srinagar, Budgam and Anantnag in Kashmir.
Production: 16 t dry saffron.
Productivity: 2.8 kg/ha (source: Spices Statistics, Spices Board, 1996-97).

Export Potential:
The competing countries are: Iran, Spain, France, China, Turkey, Sicily, Italy, Greece, Austria, England, France, India (Kashmir) and China. More than half of the world’s saffron is grown in the La Mancha region of the central Spain. Small quantities of the Indian varieties are exported to Saudi Arabia, Bahrain, Myanmar and Hong Kong.
Export: 2.13 t (source: Spices Statistics, Spices Board, 1996-97).
