DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Leaves pinnate : leaflets 7-15 pairs, oblong to oblong-lanceolate, flowers white to pink, crowded in compound corymbs; fruits globose, c. 1 cm. across, bright red or sometimes nearly white. The tree is often cultivated as an ornament but is valued for its fruit known as Rowan berries in Europe.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used:
Fruits, Leaves and Bark.

Uses:

  1. Ripe fruit is used in the preparation of extracts, syrups and juices; the extracts find use in brandies, liquors. lemonades, marmalades, confectionery, and in food and coffee additives as a flavouring agent. 
  2. In times of scar- city dried fruit is ground into a meal and made into bread. 
  3. Preserves of the fruit are a good low cost source of vitamin C. Candied fruit may contain 30-40 mg./l00 g. of vitamin C, and concentrates up to 240 mg./l00 g. of the vitamin. A concentrate prepared from the fruit, particularly of var. moravica Dippel, which bears larger fruits of agreeable flavour, is said to be valuable in infant nutrition

Chemistry
 The berries of mountain ash contain : sugar, 3-1-8-9; malic acid, 1 .9-3.9%; vitamin C, 39-74 mg.;carotene, 6-2-9-8 mg./l00 g. Sorbitol, sorbin and sorbic acid, pectin, tannin, bitter substances, a cyanophoric gluco- sidc, volatile oil, wax and a red colouring matter in the skin are also reported to be present. A bitter substance isolated from the fruit in an yield of 0-8 per cent is made up of equivalent amounts of glucose and ^-sorbic acid.
Amygdalin (34-27-61-70 mg./l00 g.) and hydrocyanic acid (2-02^3-72 mg./l00 g.) are reported to be present in frozen fruit.

Fruit is said to be depurative, astringent, diuretic, cm- menagogue, aperient and antiscorbutic. An infusion of the fruit is said to be useful in haemorrhoids and strangury. An extract of the fruit is used in gall-bladder irritations and digestive disturbances. A preparation of the fruit administered in the form of pills containing vitamin P (50 ing.) and vitamin C (25 ing.) normalized capillary resistance in patients sutlering from myocardial infarction and angina pecloris and treated with anti-coagulating agents to reduce capillary resistance. In homoeopathy a strong decoction of the berry is employ-ed against peripneumonia in cattle.

Seeds contain 22 per cent of a fixed oil, and detailed seeds 0-073 per cent hydrocyanic acid. Leaves, buds, young twigs and bark contain amygdalin. In addition, tannin (4-47%), four triterpenoids, sorbose, sorbitol and sorbic acid have been reported to be present in the bark 

An infusion of the leaves is slightly laxative and used as a pectoral in cough and bronchitis. Leaves and flowers are used in tea mixtures. A decoction of the bark is given in diarrhoea and as a vaginal injection in leucor- rhoea. It is said to produce irritation of the alimentary mucous membrane and a reflex nervous irritation .
Chemical composition of wood:
Bark is said to tan leather a good brown colour. Analysis of wood gave the following values : pentosans, 29-53; Koenig lignin, 46-44; cellulose, 46-44; hemicellulosc, 33.48; and ash, 0-63%. Two phenolic derivatives occur in the wood. Wood (wt., ,690-740 kg./cu.m.) is yellowish white, very close- and even-grained and soft. It is easy to work, and finishes smoothly, but its durability is low.
