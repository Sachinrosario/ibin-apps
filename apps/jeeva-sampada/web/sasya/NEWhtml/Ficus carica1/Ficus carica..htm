Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
The common fig (Ficus carica) is a small- to moderate-sized deciduous tree, 6-8 m high which has been under cultivation since antiquity in the eastern Mediterranean region. It is thought to be a native to southern parts of Arabian peninsula, Italy, the Balkan peninsula and the former. According to El-Ray and Llacer , the fig originated in the East Mediterranean region (Turkey, Syria, Saudi Arabia), from where its cultivation expanded to the whole of the Mediterranean region. It was perhaps first brought into cultivation in the southern parts of the Arabian peninsula by at least 3000 BC. It later spread into what is now Iran, Syria and Turkey and into all the Mediterranean countries. 

Availability :  
It is Cultivated in many parts of India, especially in the North-Western Provinces, the Punjab, the Western Himalaya, Sind and Beluchistan.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
The common fig (Ficus carica) is a small- to moderate-sized deciduous tree, 6-8 m high with a short twisted trunk, crown with irregular branches; frequently shoots develop at the base of the trunk. The bark is pale grey. Terminal buds are short and stout. Leaves are broad, ovate or nearly orbicular, more or less deeply 3-5 lobed, rough above and pubescent below; long stalked, leaf blade 10-25 cm long, dark green with pronounced venation. The female fig plants have larger leaves and denser, more spreading crowns than males. Leaf fall begins earlier in males. The leaves and the spongy parenchyma tissues are thicker in females. The leaf thickness is 125.5 m in females and 111.8 u.m in males. Fruits are mostly solitary, axillary, green or yellow, brown, purplish or even black depending on cultivar, more or less pear-shaped with either a velvety or glabrous skin, in certain cultivars up to 6 cm in diameter, but normally of moderate size. The fruit consists of a fleshy hollow receptacle with a narrow aperture at the tip and numerous small flowers lining the inner surface. The edible fig is a multiple fruit. Botanically it is known as syconium which consists of a fleshy hollow receptacle with a narrow aperture at the tip and numerous small flowers lining the inner surface. The true fruits are the tiny drupelets inside the cavity of the fused peduncle.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Ficus carica is a gynodioecious species, having two distinct forms of trees; the capri- fig is monoecious, and the common fig is pistillate. Flower buds which are spherical, have 3-5 scales, with the inflorescence axis in the internal cavity enclosing female primordia differentiated to varying degrees depending on the position of bud on the shoot. These buds produce parthenocarpic fruits only in cv. Petrovaca.
Mixed buds are conical, made up of 5-8 scales, and inflorescences on shoots arising from these buds produce fruit in both cultivars.
Vegetative buds, made up of 3-5 scales, which usually remain dormant and only rarely produce shoots.

Breeding System :
The figs commonly grown in India are parthenocarpic in nature and do not need any cross-pollination with wild fig (caprifig), which is a very common practice in other countries. The improvement is being brought about by breeding methods like Selection, Hubridization and also by Biotechnology.

Phenology :
The trees continue to bear good crops for 30-40 years before they begin to decline. The fruits start ripening from March-May in western India and May-June in northern India. In South India, the fig bears twice a year - once in July-September and again in February-May. But some reports says that flowering occurs in different periods in different places like September-November
March-August.

Seed Biology :

Seed Germination:
Seed - sow spring in a warm greenhouse. Prick out the seedlings as soon as they are large enough to handle and over winter the young plants in a greenhouse for at least their first year. Plant out in late spring after the last expected frosts and give some protection for their first winter outdoors.

Cultivation:
 Requires a well-drained medium to light loam and some lime rubble incorporated into the soil. Succeeds in dry soils. A heavy wet soil tends to encourage excessive plant growth at the expense of fruit production. Prefers a very sunny position but tolerates part-day shade when grown on a warm wall. Land must not be water logged. The crop requires a mixed manure, about 10-12 cart-loads for the first year. The plants are raised from cuttings 1/2 to 1 inch thick and a foot and half a long, planted in rows 10-12 inches apart in a richly manured and watered plot. The cuttings should be put into the ground in June after the mansoon has set in and should be watered regularly. In two months they began to through roots and shoots and few leaves. If they are properly taken care they become fit for transplanting after a year. Best season for transplanting is July-August. It requires careful watering. In the fruiting season, the failure of a singly weekly watering reduces the outturn.
'Brown Turkey' is the cultivar most commonly grown in Britain and is probably the most suitable for this climate. 'White Ischia' is a dwarf cultivar (though it can still be 5 metres tall and wide) and is ideal for pot culture. It produces an abundance of green-white thin-skinned fruits. Up to three crops of fruit a year can be obtained in some countries. If plants are grown in pots in a conservatory or cold greenhouse, two crops of fruit can be obtained, one in early summer and one in late summer to autumn. Pinch back the new shoots to about six leaves in order to encourage the second crop. It is a good idea to restrict the roots of fig trees on most soil types in order to discourage excessive vegetative growth at the expense of fruit production. This can be done by root pruning, but it is easier to place some kind of permanent restriction around the roots - planting into a large tub that is then buried into the ground is one method. It is important to make sure that the tree still gets ample moisture, especially when the fruits are ripening.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES
Pests:
There are several pests of fig. The major pests are stem-boring beetles (Batocera mfomaculata, B. rubus), leaf defoliators (Adoratns duvauceli, A. versutus). Scale insects (Aspidiotens lataniae} and fig flies (Lonchaea anstella).
Stephanoderes rulgaris is one of the most common borers on fig branches and stems. Larvae and adult beetles construct their distinct galleries beneath the bark. Severe infectation may cause drying and death of weak trees.
The fly (Lonchaea arisfella) which lays its eggs on green fruits 30-40 days before they mature, can cause 40-70 per cent losses. It may be controlled by spraying with fenthion during growth and deltamethrin at harvest. The fig scale (Ceroplastes rusci) may be controlled by oleoparathion and by natural predators. In addition, fig jassids (Vehtcaricae) and fig midge (Udumhana naimenris) also cause some damage to the crop.
Fig leaf roller (Phycodes mma) has been reported to occur in Haryana; excellent control of this pest was obtained with tnchlorphon at 0.05 per cent and chlorpyrifos at 0.03 per cent. 
Diseases:
1) Fungal diseases
Rust caused by Cerotelium fid is an important disease of fig in all the fig growingcountries including India. Rust affected plants show small, round, brownish to black eruptive lesions on the leaves mostly on the lower surface. The rust causes heavy defoliation of leaves and consequently reduces the yield. It affects all species of Ficus The disease can be controlled by dusting with sulphur or spraying with zincb (0.15 to 0.20 per cent). It is also found 0.2% mancozeb at monthly intervals as the most effective fungicide for controlling fig rust followed by tridemorph and wet sulphur.
A leaf spot disease of fig caused by Cylwdrocladium scoparium has been reported from Uttar Pradesh. The affected leaves show minute, brown spots, which enlarge into uniform, prominent, reddish-brown lesions with dark brown margins. The lesions mostly coalesce at a later stage onto irregular patches.The centre of the lesion is often dropped out. A cobweb-like mycelium spreads over the lower surface and subsequently becomes powdery. The affected leaves abscise 20-30 days earlier than the healthy ones.
Anthracnose of fig (Sphaceloma fici-caricae) is a common disease in certain fig erowmg areas of India. The disease can be controlled by spraying aureofungin at 40 ppm in soap solution + 20 ppm CuS04. 

2) Viral diseases
Figs arc susceptible to viruses which occur in all the fig-growing countries. In India, a severe fig mosaic has been reported from Himachal Pradesh and Punjab. The symptoms appear as yellowish-green spots scattered over the lamina. The spots sometimes coalesce to form bigger spots of various shapes and sizes. Occasionally, the leaves develop white mottle and are deformed. The diseased plants do not attain normal size and bear very few fruits. The fig mite, Aceria ficus serves as a vector of the virus causing fig mosaic. The virus can infect other species of Ficus. 

3) Root knot nematodes:
Figs are also susceptible to root-knot nematodes which can be controlled by fumigation with DD mixture. Nematode species associated with fig crops in Brazil are Aphelencho.des sp., Aphelenchus avenae, Criconemella onoensis C sphaerocephala. Helicotylenchus dihystera. Heterodera fid, Pratylen- chus brachyurus, P. zeae. Meloidogyne arenaria, M. incognita and M. Javamca. 

Storage diseases-1
Among storage rots, rhizophus rot (Rhizopus stolonifer) is quite common. It can be controlled bv dipping the fruits in a solution ofaureofimgm at 40 ppm in soap solution + 20 ppm copper sulphate. Rhizopus rot thrived when temperatures were between 20°C and 25°C, and with a relative humidity higher than 90% and twice as many fruits showed rots under cover than when grown in the open. When figs were grown under PVC, sprays of 1000 ppm captan WP, 333 ppm iprodio^e w.p. and 400 ppm chlorothalonil WP gave promising results against Rhizopus rot. Bagging did show promising control of Rhizopus rot. Drosophila flew to fruits in which the sarcocarp had softened and the peri- carp had changed from green to yellow, and especially to ripe fruit. In orchards using PVC covers, a method of cultural control which involved removing diseased fruit from the orchard every day, the incidence of rots was reduced to 20% of the previous level within 4 days.

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Fresh fruits and Dried fruits.

Uses

  1. Figs are consumed fresh or dried, preserved, candied or canned. Fresh figs are very delicious, wholesome and nutritious and used as dessert or for making jam. The great bulk of fruit is consumed as dried fruits. When dried and ground it can be used as substitute of coffee.
  2. Fruit - raw or cooked. Sweet and succulent, a fully ripe specimen is an exquisite fruit that almost literally melts in the mouth. The fruit is often dried for later use and this dried fruit is a major item of commerce. Figs are usually pear-shaped and up to 5 cm in diameter.
  3. The latex from the sap can be used to coagulate plant milk. 

               

Harvesting Practices
For best flavour, the fruits should not be picked until they are soft and wilt at the neck, hanging down from their own weight. They are then fully mature and ripe and ready to be eaten. Milky latex exuding from the stem when the fruit is pulled off indicates that the fruit is still immature.
The fruits of cv. Bursa Siyahi from the first, second and third nodes had the greatest diameter. TSS content was highest (17.7%) in fruits harvested on the second harvest date (20 September) compared to those harvested at other dates from August to October.
As fresh figs are extremely perishable, fully ripe fruits cannot be transported to distant markets; for that purpose it is better to harvest the crop when slightly inunature. Ripe fruits are picked either from the tree by twisting the neck at the stem-end or by cutting it or gathered after they drop on the ground. Harvesting may start from the middle of February and continue till June; fruits are picked every 2 to 4 days.

Produce and Products
Jam, dry fruit.

Chemistry
Fresh figs generally consist of 84 per cent pulp and 16 per cent skin. The average composition of fig is : protein 4 g, calcium 200 mg, iron 4 mg, vitamin A 100 IU and thiamine 0.10 mg/100 g of edible portion (Platt, 1962); its calorific value is 260. Fig is a poor source of vitamin C, but is high in sugar, Ca, Fe and Cu (Mortensen and Bullard, 1968). Yahata and Nogata (1999) observed that glucose and fructose were the major sugars, whereas sucrose was a minor one in second crop fruitlets of 10 common figs (Ficus carica var. hortensis) and of first crop fruitlets of 3 San Pedro-type figs (F carica var. intermedia) and 1 common fig. In the fruitlets and fleshy peduncular tissue of the second crop syconia of cultivars Masui Dauphine and Houraishi (common figs), glucose and fructose contents increased rapidly 2 weeks before harvest. While the percentages of glucose to total sugar contents decreased, those of fructose increased. Investigation on 10 fig cultivars revealed that leaf Mn was highly correlated with fructose, a-glucose and total sugar content, possibly due to its role in carbohydrate metabolism (Hakerlerler et al., 2000).

Processing Techniques
There are many fruit products of fig; dried figs being the most important. Before drying the fruits are subject to sulphur fumigation and then dried in an electric drier at a temperature of 70-72°C. In another method, fruits are first soaked in boiling salt water for half-a-minute, then dried for a few hours in the sun and for 8 days under shade. At the end of the process the weight is reduced to a little over one-thirds of the fresh weight (Samson, 1980). Duyar (1998) suggested that apart from specific temperature and humidity requirements, quality of dried fig production depends on wind factors.

Other Uses

1.     Wood - pliable but porous and of little value. It is used for hoops, garlands, ornaments etc . When saturated with oil and covered with emery is used as a substitute for a hone .

2.     The fruit, fresh or dried, is valued for its laxative property.  Its applied for boils and other skin infection.

3.      In some places its latex is used to coagulate milk.

4.     Fig leaves are used medicinally for their diuretic, demulcent, emollient and antihelmintic properties. Three new constituents viz., calotropenyl acetate, lupeol acetate and oleanolic acid were isolated. A decoction of the leaves is stomachic. The leaves are also added to boiling water and used as a steam bath for painful or swollen piles .

5.     The latex from the stems is used to treat corns, warts and piles. It also has an analgesic effect against insect stings and bites.

6.     The fruit is mildly laxative, demulcent, digestive and pectoral. The unripe green fruits are cooked with other foods as a galactogogue and tonic . The roasted fruit is emollient and used as a poultice in the treatment of gumboils, dental abscesses etc .Syrup of figs, made from the fruit, is a well-known and effective gentle laxative that is also suitable for the young and very old.

7.     A decoction of the young branches is an excellent pectoral.

Miscellaneous Information

Breeding and improvement :Selection
Although fig has been under cultivation for many centuries and is now distributed in many countries of the world, relatively little work has been done for its improvement.The common fig (Ficiis carica) is diploid (2n=26), while F. elastica cv. Decora is triploid in nature (Ohri and Khoshoo, 1987). Most of the fig cultivars cultivated today are selections made by unknown persons in Asia and Europe in the past centuries from wild seedling trees and chance seedlings. Since then these have been maintained clonally by rooted cuttings and in course of time, have acquired names.
Hybridization
The objectives of present-day fig breeding are : (i) development of high yielding cultivars, (ii) improvement in fruit quality, (iii) elimination of caprification, and (iv) transfer of nematode and insect resistance characters of wild fig varieties to high yielding good quality cultivars. After separating the seedlings into a caprifig or a common fig type, the occurrence of parthenocarpy in the first or second crops was investigated. In the first crop of caprifig type, a high frequency of parthenocarpy was found in progenies from San Pedro-type cultivar, whereas the common-type cultivar set a moderate number of parthenocarpic fruit in the first crop. In the second crop, the common-type cultivar produced proge- nies with a high frequency of parthenocarpic fruit. Almost no parthenocarpic seedlings were produced when a non-parthenocarpic Smyrna-type cultivar was used as a seed parent.
Biotechnology
Apart from standardization of protocol for micropropagation, biochemical and molecular markers have been used in the identification of genotypes of fig.

Disorders-2
1) Sunburn
Sunburn is a serious problem of newly planted young trees The affected parts crack and the bark peels off. Affected trunk or branches may be a centre for infection with fungi. Heavy pruning exposing the trunk and branches is responsible for sunburn. Whitewashing of exposed parts can prevent this malady.

2)Fruit splitting or cracking
Fruit splitting usually occurs if there is a shower during ripening of fruits. Fruit spli- tting damages the fruit, making it completely unfit for consumption as it invites infection of rot organisms. 

Types and cultivars
Depending upon the nature of flowers and the method of pollination, there are four pomologically distinct classes of fig : (i) Common fig or Adriatic fig, (li) Caprifig, (iii) Smyrna fig and (iv) San Pedro fig.

Common Fig
In this type the individual flowers are pistillate and the fruits develop without the stimulation of pollination and fertilization. Some cultivars of this type are Kadota (Dottato), Mission, Adriatic, Brown Turkey, Celeste and Conadna.

Caprifig
It is the most primitive cultivated type with short-styled pistillate flowers and functional staminate flowers. Most caprifigs are not edible but are grown because they harbour a small wasp, Blastophaga psenes, which is necessary for pollination and setting of fruits.

Smyrna fig
In this type, the fruits do not develop unless the flowers are pollinated with pollen carried from the male flowers of the caprifig by the tiny Blastophaga wasp. Calimyma is the most common and widely grown cultivar of Smyrna type.

San Pedro fig
It is an intermediate type in which the first crop known as 'breba' crop, is completely parthenocarpic and does not require pollination and fertilization of flowers, but the second crop develops only if the flowers are pollinated as in the case of Smyrna type. San Pedro, King and Gentile are common cultivars of this type. Of these four types, Smyrna fig is commercially most important and is extensively grown in Europe and USA.
