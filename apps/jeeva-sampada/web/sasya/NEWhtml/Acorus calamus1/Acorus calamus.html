Other Biological Details

HABIT AND HABITAT
It is a herbaceous plant, it is commercially cultivated. It is distributed very widely across the Northern Hemisphere. It inhabits in the stagnant pools. It comes up well in clayey loams, sandy loams and light alluvial soils of river banks. It prefers an area having well distributed rainfall throughout the year (>800 mm) and temperatures ranging from 10°C to 38°C. There should be plenty of sunshine available to the plant during its growth period. In India, it is recorded from North-Western Himalayas (Jammu & Kashmir and Himachal Pradesh) to Eastern Himalayas (Sikkim and Arunachal Pradesh) and also other N.E. states (Assam, Manipur, Nagaland). It occurs in most other states of India. Thus this species is found almost throughout India upto an altitude of about 2000 m, growing chiefly in marshy or moist situations. It is also grown in North America, Europe and Asia. In Karnataka, it is being grown in Koratagere Taluk of Tumkur district in an area of 200 acres and annual production is 440 tonnes of dried rhizomes. Since the last 10 years, it is also being cultivated using the urban sewage water in Tumkur city (140 acres).BioticZone: Himalaya,none,

Other Species
Acorus is a small genus of herbs, comprising 2 species.
1. Acorus gramineL is Soland
It is a Japanese species, occasionally encountered in Sikkim (up to
1 800 m) and the Khasi hills (1 200-1 400 m). It is used for the same medicinal purpose as A. calamus. Here, the mid-rib of the leaf is absent. It is a semi-aquatic, perennial herb with creeping and many-branched aromatic rhizomes of spongy texture, from 1.5 to 2.5 cm thickness and sometimes several metres long. The leaves are 10-22 cm in width and 0.60-1.8 m in length. The rhizome will be brown outside and white and sponge-lined inside.

2. Acorns calamus L.
It is a semi-aquatic perennial herb with a stout midrib, with a creep ing and many branched aromatic rhizome. It is found growing wild or cultivated throughout India and Sri Lanka ascending to 1 800 m in the Himalayas. The plant thrives in marshy places and moist situations, like the edges of lakes and banks of streams and rivers. It is plentiful in the marshy tracts of Kashmir and Sirmoor in Manipur and the Naga Hills. It is also grown in North America, Europe and Asia.

Status : Domesticated, and it also found in wild.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Sweet flag is a monocot plant. The plant is a herb,  and has a long branched, rhizome which contains aromatic oil. This rhizome is light brown or pinkish brown externally, white and spongy within. Leaves are bright green, arranged alternately in two vertical rows on the rhizome, of about, 0.75-1.5 m, long leaves. and the breadth of the leaf is 2-4 cm and resembles the leaves of an iris. The leaves are bright green, thickened in the middle and the margin is wavy. The spathe is 6-30 cm long and the spadix is slightly curved. The fruit is a berry, yellow-green in colour, angular and 1-3 seeded.

Cultivation:
Soil requirement:
Sweet flag is cultivated in almost the same way as rice and may be grown in any part of the country where suitable irrigation facilities are available. Although calamus in the wild state grows in water, it may be cultivated in almost any good, but fairly moist soil. It is usually grown in areas where paddy can be grown. It is generally grown in the clayey loam and light alluvial soils of riverbank.
    The field is laid out exactly as for rice, irrigated sufficiently and after ploughing twice are watered heavily and again ploughed. The field is then left for a few days and ploughed to puddle again. The field is then leveled with the leveling board.
Climate:
    Sweet flag is a hardy plant and thus found growing from tropical of sub-tropical climates. However, it prefers an area having a good and well distributed rainfall all throughout the year. There should be plenty of sun shine available to the plant during its growth period and also after harvesting, for drying the rhizomes.
Cultivation:
Planting material: It is mainly propagated vegetatively through rhizomes.
    Rhizomes obtained from earlier planting are kept preserved in the soil, which is constantly kept moist. After emergence these rhizomes are cut into small pieces and planted. Rhizome cuttings of 2 to 3 nodes are planted during June-July in sunken moist beds of clayey loam to light alluvial soils. They are regularly watered by flooding the bed. Nursery beds in the open are preferred. In about a month, 90 percent of the rhizomes sprout and about 90 percent successfully establish in the field.
Transplanting and after care:
    Sprouted rhizomes are picked up from the nursery and planted directly in marshy open field at a spacing of 30 x 30 cm and pressed into the ground at a depth of about 5 cm inside the soil. Periodic manuring is important. The field is kept irrigated regularly with 5 cm of water left standing in the field at the earlier stage; later water level is increased to 10 cm as the plant grows. The field is carefully and regularly weeded. Plants should be protected from mealy bugs. The rhizomes will be ready for harvesting after a year.
Planting season:
    But the best planting season is spring (March-April) but, the crop may be planted almost at any time of the year taking into consideration the fact that plenty of sunshine is available at harvest time to dry the rhizomes.
    The recommended spacing for sweet flag is 30 cm x 30 cm, the rhizome pieces are pressed into the mud at a depth of about 5 cm inside the soil. The rhizomes are planted in such a way that the plant in the second row comes in between the first row and not opposite to them. Around 1,12,000 sprouted rhizome pieces are required for planting an area of 1 ha.
Manure and fertilizer recommendation:
    The field should be manured with green manure (10 to 12 t) and compost (15 t/ha). Along with this, 100 kg ammonium sulphate, 300 kg super phosphate and 100 kg muriate of potash should be added, or otherwise 125 kg N, P and K/ha is recommended to be applied in 3 split doses.
Irrigation and weeding:
    The field is kept regularly irrigated. About 5 cm of water is left standing in the field in the beginning. Later water level is increased to 10 cm as the plant grows and maintained till the harvest. Weeding should be done regularly and carefully, till the completion of the crop eight weeding are required and at each weeding the exposed erect rhizomes are pressed down into the soil.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES
Pests and diseases:
    This crop is practically free from the attack of any pests or diseases of serious nature, except mealy bugs, which infest the shoot and roots. These insect can be controlled by spraying the shoot and drenching the roots of grown up plants with 0.1 per cent methyl parathion or 0.2 percent quinolphos. And it is infected by fungal diseases like leaf spot which can be effectively controlled by spraying captan (10 gm/10 liters of water).  
Harvesting Practices:
    After about a year, the crop is ready for harvesting. Partially the field should be dried, so that sufficient moisture is retained to facilitate the necessary deep digging. The leaves start turning yellow and dry, indicative of maturity. The rhizomes will be at a depth of 60 cm and are about 30-60 cm long. Therefore, harvesting should be done carefully. The rhizomes, after harvest, are cut into short lengths of 5-7.5 cm and all the fibrous roots are removed. The pieces are then washed thoroughly and dried in the sun. The dried material is put into gunny bags and rubbed to free them of leafy scales. 
Yield:
    The yield of rhizomes is about 10 t/ha., the fresh rhizomes yield about 1.2% of dry rhizomes and 3-3.5% of essential oil, which is bitter. The fresh aerial parts yield 0.125% oil. The rhizomes should not be peeled prior to drying, because peeling results in considerable loss of oil through evaporation and resinification. The highest yields of oil are obtained from dried, unpeeled rhizomes that have been granulated immediately before they are put into the still. Peeled, dried European calamus roots yield from 1.5-1.8% oil, whereas the Japanese variety yields up to 5% of oil.  The Indian roots growing in the plains yield about 3.1% and those growing in the Kashmir Valley yield not more than 1.4% of oil.

Chemistry
The plant is aromatic on account of the presence of an essential oil, which contains the glucosidic bitter principle,  'Acorin'. It contains a volatile, yellowish-brown oil of peculiar, but  pleasant, slightly sweet odour, that can be isolated by steam distillation. The yield and physico-chemical constituents and, consequently, the composition of the 'calamus oil' depend upon the source from which the rhizomes are obtained. The European and American oils are similar in composition, consisting of low amounts of ‘asarone’, which can be compared to the Indian oil. The rhizomes contain a high percentage of starch.

Other Uses:
  1. It has an expectorant action due to the presence of the essential oil and is commonly used as a remedy for asthma. 
  2. It is also used as a remedy for chronic diarrhoea. 
  3. The rhizome is an appetizer and is also useful in the treatment of epilepsy, delirium, hysteria and loss of memory. 
  4. In Unani literature, the rhizome has been described as having a very bitter taste and is said to be useful as a brain tonic. 
  5. The powdered rhizomes have been employed in combination with Rauwolfia serpentina and other drugs in the treatment of neurosis, insomnia and hysteria.  Because of the pleasant aromatic smell, the rhizomes are extensively used as an aromatic agent in agarbathies, dhoops and havana samagries.
  6. The powdered rhizome and the essential oil can be used as a safe insecticide against flies, mosquitoes, bed-bugs, moths and lice. This property is due to the presence of the 'trans-isomer of asarone'. The leaves and rhizomes are used to control lice. It's essential oil is used in flavouring liquors, beer, gin, vinegar, snuff and various other preparations. The "bach" of commerce, which has many medicinal uses is prepared from the rhizomes. The fresh rhizomes are used in confectionery and also as a substitute for ginger. In moderate doses, the oil produces antispasmodial action on the involuntary muscle tissue, inhibiting the excessive peristaltic movements of the intestine. The rhizomes are being used in hair powders, flavouring liquors, vinegar and in snuffs.
Economical Potential to the country

Economics of Sweet flag Cultivation:
    A study was conducted by collecting field data from thirty farmers cultivating sweet flag in Tumkur district of Karnataka covering both ground water (fresh water) and sewage water conditions respectively during 1997-98.
    In sewage Sweet flag, 52 per cent of the total cost of production is contributed by human labor, followed by seed cost, which forms 10 per cent of the total cost. In groundwater Sweet flag, 47 per cent of the total cost of production is contributed by human labor, followed by the imputed cost of electricity to lift ground water which forms 8.5 per cent, followed by the cost of seeds, which forms 8 per cent.  In sewage Sweet flag, the major portion (35 per cent) of labor cost is towards harvesting, followed by labor for irrigation (29 percent).
    In groundwater Sweet flag, the major portion (35 per cent) of cost is towards labor for irrigation followed by labor for harvesting (26 percent). The farmers using sewage water use mechanized processing of the Sweet flag rhizomes in hand operating drums. But farmers using ground water, process the Sweet flag by hand beating the long rhizomes and rubbing them by hand on the ground.
    Another difference is in the expenditure on manure. The ground water using farmers incur an expenditure of Rs.1298 per acre on FYM and Rs. 1523 on green leaf manure. Whereas farmers growing Sweet flag using sewage water incur an expense of Rs. 220 on FYM and apply no green leaf manure.
Out put and returns per acre:
    The average yield of Sweet flag per acre was 23.04 and 16 quintals in sewage and ground water farms respectively. The higher yield on sewage water farms is due to the use of nutrient rich sewage water. Sewage water farmers got a gross return of Rs.54, 374 while it was Rs.45, 152 on ground water farms. The output price was Rs.2, 822 and Rs.2, 360 per quintal on ground water and sewage water irrigated conditions respectively. The difference in gross return is due to lower yield of Sweet flag on ground water farms uncompensated by the higher price of Sweet flag. The higher price for ground water Sweet flag is due to the (i) higher thickness of the rhizome from ground water and (ii) higher oil content (of 1.85 per cent) (called Calamus oil). The oil content in sewage grown Sweet flag is 1.1 per cent. The oil content in the Sweet flag leaves is estimated to be 0.1 per cent for ground water Sweet flag and 0.07 per cent for sewage water grown Sweet flag.
    This study indicates that cultivation of sweet flag under sewage water condition is (twice) profitable than the cultivation under ground water situation. However, cultivation of the crop under marshy or water logged conditions is suitable. This crop can be cultivated where ever the seepage/ water logged conditions are predominant through out the year. Alternatively, we can also encourage cultivation of this crop near the cities, which enables better economic utilization of sewage water since the crop comes up well with out harm full effects and also creates a good environment. Commercial cultivation of sweet flag conserves biodiversity (in the wild) and reduces burden on foreign exchange and for meeting the domestic requirements of the country. Thus, cultivation of sweet flag using ground water is advantageous in terms of quality, as rhizome may not contain any chemical residues.
