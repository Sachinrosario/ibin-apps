Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:

Large deciduous tree suitable for growing in marginal land and village commons, often with crooked trunk. Occurs throughout the sub-Himalayan tract from Ravi to Assam, ascending up to 5,000 ft., tropical medium rainfall areas of Indo-Gangetic Plains and the Peninsula (moist tropical), dry areas of Rajasthan and Peninsular India (dry tropical). It grows gregariously in alluvial forests characteristic of the riverbeds of these regions and also comes up naturally on freshly exposed soils. Sissoo does not grow healthy in soils of heavy texture. The species can withstand high soil moisture under good drainage conditions. It can not withstand water-logging even for a short period but can sustain periods of drought.

In natural state, fairly drought resistant and frost hardy. A strong light demander. Seedlings sensitive to drought. Susceptible to browsing damage. Cannot withstand fire.

It occurs throughout the sub Himalayan tract from Ravi to Assam, ascending up to 5,000 ft.; it grows gregariously in alluvial forests characteristic of the river beds of these regions. It is extensively cultivated in Punjab, U.P., Bengal and Assam. No other timber tree except teak is cultivated to a greater extent.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Tree often with crooked trunk and light crown. Under favourable conditions attains height of about 100 ft., a girth up to 8 ft. and a clear bole up to 35 ft. Young parts pubescent or tomentose; branches numerous, spreading. Leaves alternate, bifarious, imparipinnate; leaf rachis zigzag; petioles terete, very downy when young; stipules lanceolate, caducous. Leaflets 3-5, firm, the terminal the largest and the lowest the smallest, distant, alternate, suborbicular, conspicuously and abruptly acuminate, puberulous when young, soon glabrescent, base narrowed or cuneate

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Flowers sessile or nearly so, small, copious, in terminal or lateral panicles, rachis and branches of the panicle densely hairy. Bracts small, sub-persistent, bracteoles usually minute. Calyx companulate, hairy, teeth short, ciliate, the two upper connate except at the tip, the lateral linear, obtuse, the lowest the longest. Corolla exserted standard broad with a long claw, wings oblong, keel obtuse, its petals joined at the tip. Stamens 9 all in a bundle, slit only at the top. Anthers minute, basifixed, with cells back to back, dehiscing usually by an apical (rarely a longitudinal) slit. Ovary stalked; ovules 2-4; style incurved, short, stigma small, terminal. Pod oblong or strap-shaped, usually thin and flat, indehiscent. Seeds 1-4, reniform, flat-compressed.

Floral and seed biology:

Seedling Germination: Grown by entire transplanting, root and shoot cuttings and direct sowing.

The tree sheds its pods during December-April and seeds germinate at the commencement of rains, though in riverain regions, floods stimulate earlier germination. The factors favouring seedling development are full light, porous soil with sufficient moisture and freedom from weeds.

Artificial propagation is possible by both direct sowing and by planting seedlings, root suckers and sections of lateral roots. Stump planting of nursery seedlings is favoured, particularly in the west coast. Weeding and cleaning are essential in all cases.

In direct sowings, seedlings attain a height of 6-9 in. at the end of the first rains and 3-4 ft. after the second rains. Direct sowing may prove successful if the soil is favourable and sufficient moisture is available. For seedling transplantation, only tender plants with small taproots should be used. The method is employed for filling gaps in direct sowing and for road side planting. Planting of root suckers has given satisfactory results in dry climates.

The usual method of raising sissoo is stumps planting, widely employed in irrigated plantations in Punjab and U.P. Trenches are dug about 5 ft. apart and the dug up earth is thrown a little away from the trenches and the berms used for sowing pod segments. Sixty seers of pods are required for sowing an acre of nursery with sowings done on both sides of the trenches. March and the middle of June, earlier sowing being preferred. Plants are big enough by the beginning of next season to yield stumps. Plants are pulled out and stems and roots chopped off leaving 1.5-2 in. of the former and 9-14 in. of the latter; the lateral roots are also removed. Stumps thicker than 1 in. and thinner than 0.75 in. diam. At the collar are rejected. The yield of stumps is 40,000 per acre. For transport over long distances, stumps are made into bundles, wrapped in leaves or grass, sprinkled with water, and carries in gunny bags.

The best time for planting is spring, not earlier than the third week of March. Planting may be carried out in April, but in no case should it be cut off to August-September. Where the subsoil is low or the rainfall is poor and uncertain, irrigation is essential.

For raising plantations without irrigation, pod segments are sown directly in lines. Good results have been obtained by sowing seeds along the centre of lines which are 2 ft. wide. Sailaba plantations (on alluvial ground thrown up along banks of rivers and subject, in part at least, to occasional flooding) are best raised by transplanting seedlings or by line sowing.

Spacing adopted in sissoo planting varies in different areas. Spacing exceeding 8 ft. X 8 ft. is too wide and leads to low branching and crooked growth. A spacing 5 ft. apart in lines 10 ft. in between has proved satisfactory. In Punjab, stump planting 6 ft. apart on berms of trenches 10 ft. apart is the standard practice for stocking plantations.

All sissoo plantations should be thinned out when young and the area sown or planted with other species with only a certain proportion of sissoo trees standing. Mulberry is suitable for inter-planting in sissoo plantations. Sissoo trees should be removed at any sign of unhealthiness. Raising of pure crops is recommended.

MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:


Sissoo trees are susceptible to wilt disease due to Fusarium vasinfectum Atkinson in the sapling and pole stages in the taungyas of north India. The mortality is more pronounced if pure strands of sissoo are raised or when the strands are in admixture with other susceptible species such as khair, babul and siris.

The trees of advanced age are attacked by Ganoderma lucidum (Leyss.) Karst. and Polyporus gilvus Schwein. The former cause root disease and die back ; the latter produces stem canker in trees growing naturally in the terai region, outskirts of villages and canal banks in Punjab and Uttar Pradesh where the roots are exposed and damaged by soil erosion. (Bagchee, Indian For., 1945, 71, 20; Khan, Indian For., 1923, 49, 503.)

The mildew fungi, Phyllactinia subspiralis (Salmon) Blumer and Uredo sissoo Syd. Attack leaves, but they have not been observed to cause any serious injury. Seedlings are subjected to collar rot due to Rhizoctonia (Corticium) solani Kuhn. Dendrophthoe falcata (Linn. F.) Ettingshausen syn. Loranthus longiflorus Desr., an angiospermous parasite, is found on sissoo in some localities and trees often succumb to it. (Mahmud & Nema, Indian For., 1951, 77, 149; Indian J. agri. Sci., 1950, 20, 107).

Sissoo wilt is caused by Fusarium solani (Mart.) Appel & Wr.f. dalbergiae Gordon (Bakshi and Sujan Singh 1954).

Serious wilt of sissoo seedlings in nurseries due to Fusarium oxysporum Fr. Which also causes seedling wilt in many other forest species and Rosellinia aquila (Fr.) de Not. are reported from Pakistan. Web blight caused by Pellicularia filamentosa (Pat.) Rogers causing considerable damage to sissoo seedlings and other tree species is reported from India and Pakistan. (Browne 1968).

Cercospora sissoo Syd. attacks leaves of sissoo, mostly on the lower surfae, producing yellowish to greyish-green discolourations.

Colletogloeum sissoo (Syd.) Sutton, causing imperceptible spots and recorded from Varanasi (U.P.) on sissoo leaves, is similar to Cercospora sissoo from which it differs only in size and number of septation of conidia (pavgi and Singh 1971).

Phyllachora dalbergiae Niessl attacks upper surface of leaves and produces shining black, cushion like stromata which occur scattered or in clusters. The fungus is recorded from Calcutta (West Bengal) and Pusa (Bihar)(Saccardo 1883).

Phyllachora spissa Syd. forms densely aggregated, dot-like dark stromata on irregular brownish infection spots in sissoo. The parasite is recorded from Wynaad (South India) and Khasi and Jaitia Hills (Assam) (Saccardo 1926).

Phyllosticta sissoo Died. produces round to irregular, grayish-brown infection spots which sometimes cover the entire leaf surface in sissoo.

Mycosphaearella dalbergiae Muller & Ahmed causing leaf spot in sissoo is reported from Pakistan (Browne 1968).

Maravalia achora (Syd.) Arthur & Cumm. attacks leaves and juvenile twigs of sissoo and rosewood seedlings in nurseries.

Uredo sissoo Syd. & Butler attacks sissoo of all ages in nurseries, plantations and natural forests.

Tolypanthus involucratus (roxb.) v. Tiegh. (=Loranthus involucratus Roxb.) attacks branches and twigs. Cuscuta reflexa Roxb., Dendrophthoe falcata (L.f.) Ettingsh. and Loranthus pulverulentus Wall. are also recorded on sissoo in India. other records include Tapinanthus dodoneifolius (DC.) Dans. from Nigeria and Tapinanthus sp. from Sudan (Browne 1968).

Glomerella cingulata (Stonem.) Spaulding & Shrenk and Septothyrella dalbergiae Pavgi & Singh are recorded on pods from Varanasi (U.P.) (Pavgi and Singh 1971). A species of Phoma is common on pods of sissoo forests in western U.P.

Other disease records are heart rot due to Fomes robiniae (murr.) Sacc. & D. Sacc. And Ganoderma applanatum (Pers. Ex Wallr.) Pat. from India, branch camker due to Hypoxylon hypomiltum Mont. from Pakistan, horse hai blight caused by Marasmius equicrinis F. Muell. from India and sooty ,ould due to Meliola bicornis Wirt. from Ghana (Browne 1968).

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts Used:

Calorific value of wood: sapwood 4,908 cal, 8835 B.t.u. ; heartwood 5,181 cal, 9,326 B.t.u. Sissoo wood classed among excellent fuels burn like charcoal. Eminently suitable for making charcoal.

Wood: Sapwood distinct from heartwood. Sapwood white to pale brownish white; heartwood golden brown to dark brown with deep brown streaks, becoming dull on exposure, strong and durable. Wood hard, heavy, average weight 780 kg/m3 (48 lb/ft3) at 12 per cent moisture content. Specific gravity 0.82; wt. 50-53 lb/c ft., narrowly interlocked grained and medium textured.

A diffuse-porous wood, very rarely with a slight semi-ring-porous tendency. Growth rings indistinct to fairly distinct. Pores large to small, visible to the eye, few to moderately numerous, mostly solitary or in short radial multiples, often filled with dark gummy deposits; vessel lines distinct on longitudinal surfaces. Soft tissues mostly round the pores, forming eyelets, with lateral extensions, often connecting the adjacent pores by narrow, wavy or straight tangential bands particularly in the late wood and also as a fine broken or interrupted line delimiting growth rings visible only under the lens. Rays fine to very fine, distinct only under the lens as numerous, closely-spaced lines. Ripple marks distinct only under the lens, usually more prominent in the sapwood, 60-65 per cm.

The wood structure is very similar to that of sissoo (Dalbergia sissoo Roxb.) but the two timbers can be distinguished easily by their colour.

It is an important timber for gun carriage parts. It also makes good veneers.

Used for railway sleepers, musical instruments, charpai legs, electric casing, hammer handles, shoe heels, hookah tubes and tobacco pipes.

Suitable for all types of construction work, door and window panelling, electric casings, railway carriages and wagons, lorry bodies, tool handles, agricultural implements, carving, engraving, shoe-lasts, walking sticks.

Suitable for marine and aircraft grade plywood and is in demand for veneers.

Carefully selected and manufactured sissoo plywood logs satisfy he specifications prescribed for air-craft, and for this purpose, the wood from trees growing on canal banks and in plantations considered to be the best.

Paper made from the pulp has satisfactory strength properties.

High-class furniture and cabinet wood. Due to its great strength, elasticity and durability, highly valued as constructional and general utility timber.

It can be worked into intricate, deep and delicate ornamental carvings. Wood is used to make fancy trinket boxes.

Processing Techniques:

Air or kiln seasoned with little degrade. Kiln seasoning enhances the value of timber as it darkens the colour. Difference between light and dark bands gets intensified. For good scantling centres should be boxed during conversion.


Other uses:

Leaves used as fodder. Decoction of leaves said to be useful in gonorrhea.

Leaves and roots medicinally useful. Leaf mucilage mixed with sweet oil is applied in excoriation. The roots astringent and the wood useful in cutaneous affections.

Heartwood yields oil, suitable as a lubricant for heavy machinery.

Given irrigation, it is a successful species for reclaiming sandy wastes and converting them into valuable plantation areas.

Miscellaneous Information

 It grows well on porous soil containing sand, pebbles and boulders. It reaches its finest development in the riverain tracts of Bengal Duars. On clayey soil, growth stunted.

Plants in copper mine dump sites showed colonization of VAM fungi especially Glomus mosseae.
Tree is a nitrogen fixer.
Plantlets have been regenerated in vitro from roots, cotyledons and hypocotyl.
