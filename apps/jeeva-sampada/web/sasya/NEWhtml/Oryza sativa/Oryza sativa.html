HABIT & HABITAT
Rice (Oryza sativa) belongs to the genus Oryza. There are 18 valid wild species distributed mainly in Asia, Africa and America. Of the two cultivated species Oryza sativa and Oryza glaberrima, the former is cosmopolitan and the latter is confined in Africa. The Asian rice (O. sativa) evolved from the ancestral wild progenitor over a broad region stretching from the Gangetic plains below the Himalayan foothills across north-east India, upper Burma, northern Thailand, Laos, Vietnam and south China. It is generally felt that the domestication had occurred independently, and India is one of the oldest regions where domestication began. There is mention of nivara, a wild form of rice, in Vedic literature. 'Atharva Veda' mentions vrihi, probably meaning wild rice. (6,7)

Rice is an higly tillering erect growing annual belonging to the family Gramineae. Its cultivation in the world extends from 39 degrees S latitude (Australia) to 45 degrees N latitude (Japan) and 50 degrees N latitude (China) in India, it stretches from 8 degrees N latitude to 34 degrees N latitude. Rice is also grown even in areas below the sea-level, as in Kuttanad region of Kerala. It is also grown well at altitudes above 1,979 m, as in parts of Jammu and Kashmir. It is cultivated as a purely rain-fed upland crop in West Bengal, Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, etc., where the monsoon is precarious and its distribution is often erratic. On the other hand, in parts of West Bengal and Bihar, shallow and deep water (4.75-6 m) rices are also grown. Most of the present day cultivars of rice are photo-insensitive and can be grown round the year in a wide variety of soils with pH range of around 5.5 to 6.6 (1).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
An annual or perennial grass without a rhizome leaves long and narrow, 30-5O cm. x 1.2-2.5 cm., slightly pubescent with spiny hairs on the margin. Inflorescence a terminal panicle varying from close land compact in some to loose and spreading in others, spikelets generally single, but in some in clusters of 2-7; number of spikelets varying from 50-60 to 200-300, large numbers being usually associated with smaller size and a densely packed arrangement; lemma and palea surrounding the kernel, variously coloured, golden yellow, red, purple, brown or smoky black, becoming straw or light yellow when the grain ripens; grain varying in size from 5 to 14.5 mm. long and 1.9 to 3.7 mm. broad, the length/breadth ratio defining size and shape of the grain. Kernels most commonly white occasionally red, purple or brown (1).

Leaves alternate in two ranks with a single leaf at each node, number of nodes and leaves greatest on the main culm and number declines progressively with the rise in tiller order. The first leaf at base of main culm and each tiller a bladeless prophyll, all the others have a sheath and lamina. Sheath split down whole length and encircles internodes, those at base longer than internodes, thus overlapping sheath above; after tenth node sheaths become progressively shorter than internodes. Sheath finely ribbed, glabrous, with large airspaces in mesophyll, pigment may be present at base or throughout its length and is dominant to no pigmentation. Ligule a triangular membrane, 1.0-1.5 cm long, usually colourless, tending to split with age. Auricles, when present, falcate, 2-4 mm long, usually fringed with long hairs, pigment when present correlated with that of pulvinus (12).

Glumes two, an outer lower glume and inner upper glume, of equal length and usually about one-quarter length of lemma, 2-3 mm long, lanceolate, leathery, shiny, whitish, yellow, red or black

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Inflorescence of rice is termed as panicle and it is branched. Terminal panicle, 14-42 cm long, each with 50-500 spikelets attached to rachillae, usually about 100, may be open or compact, erect or drooping. Base of peduncle enclosed in sheath of flag leaf, at base a ciliate ring at junction with rachis, distance from top of leaf sheath (juncture) and ciliate ring determines the degree of exsertion . Main axis or rachis bears a variable number of primary branches, one to several at each node; angle at which they are borne determines the compactness of panicle. Each secondary branch bears one or more spikelets. The length of the panicle and its density have a definite relationship to seed yield. The spikelets are single flowered enclosed by lemma and deeply kneeled palea, which later on form husk. Rice flower comprising of 6 stamens in two whorls of three each. The filaments are very slender and delicate and anthers are linear. The pistil has a single ovary with two styles each with a plumose and laterally excerted stigma (12).

The opening of the spikelets/anthesis starts shortly after the emergence of panicle from leaf sheath. Generally the spikelets at the top of the panicle open first and the anthesis proceeds down to the base of the panicle in an irregular fashion. All the spikelets in a panicle open in 7-8 days depending upon weather conditions. The maximum anthesis takes place on the second, third and fourth day after commencement of anthesis. In most of the cultivated varieties, maximum blooming of occurs between 8.00 AM to 12.00 Noon. Along with lengthening of the filaments, the stigmas also protrude and remain receptive from the time of anthesis upto three days. In most cases the plumose stigmas are dusted with pollen when they emerge as the dehiscence of anthers takes place in many varieties just at the time of flower opening (1,13).

Breeding System :
It is self-pollinated, nitrogen positive and a short day plant. Though self-pollination is the rule natural cross pollination also occurs varying from 0.1 to 4.0 per cent sometimes it may go upto 10 per cent. Spikelet opening begins either on the day of panicle emergence, but, more usually, on the second day. The panicle completes its exsertion from the flag or boot on the third day. Blooming reaches its peak 2-4 days after emergence and is completed in 6-10 days. Spikelets at the apex of the panicle open first and proceed downwards and also from the apex of the branches downwards. In the tropics blooming is most prolific between 9-12 a.m., with a peak of about 11 a.m., but may be delayed during wet weather. The spikelets remain open for 30-90 minutes. Anthesis takes place either immediately before, simultaneously with, or more usually immediately following spikelet opening. The pollen can remain viable from five minutes up to 50 hours and germination begins within three minutes of being deposited on the stigma. Fertilization is effected about three hours after pollination. (12,13)

Phenology:
A rapid growth in height of the tiller in the later stages foretells the onset of the flowering phase. A difference of two to three weeks in the ages of tillers gets reduced to a few days only in earhead formation. since the ear formation is independent of the tillering phase. It takes about 5 weeks for the ear primordium to develop into a full ear and come out of the enclosing leaf sheath. Two days after the panicle emerges out of the leaf sheath, the spikelets start opening. A plant takes five to seven days to complete heading. irrespective of the number of tillers. F1owering and anthesis begin on the same day or the day after the heading of the panicle. (12)

Pollinators:
Rice is mainly self-pollinated crop, wind and insects are the chief agents effecting natural cross pollination.

Seed Biology:
The rice grain is caryopsis. The kernel is enclosed by the husk or hull or lemma and palea. The outer layer of the kernel is the pericarp, which may be white or colored. The pericarp consists of the epicarp, the mesocarp, and the cross layer. Attached to the pericarp and on its inner side is thin layer of testa and below it is the aleurone layer. The bulk of the kernel is constituted by the endosperm, which may be glutinous or starchy. The embryo situated at the lower end of the ventral side of the kernel consists of the radicle, the plummule, the epiblast and the scutellum. Rice shows considerable variability in grain size from 9 to 14 mm. It may be round, oval or elongated. The color of the hull varies from straw to dark purple, brown or black, with various intergrades. (1,6)

Seedling Germination :
Rice grain can germinate either immediately after harvest or may require a period of dormancy. Those with a short maturation period of less than 120 days do not generally have a dormant period, while varieties which have a long maturation period of 160 days and more require usually a resting period of 2-4 months. The period of dormancy can be broken by hulling or treatment with chemicals. The optimum temperature for germination is 30°-35°C. The seed absorbs water to about 25 per cent of its dry weight and, under suitable conditions, the first indication of germination is detectable after about two days. Rice seeds can germinate when submerged due to their low oxygen requirement, but germination is more rapid in moist soil. When grown in well-drained soils the coleorhiza with the radicle is the first to emerge, under inundation the colleoptile precedes the coleorhiza. The radicle breaks through the coleorhiza, soon to be followed by two secondary seminal roots. These function for a limited time and are replaced by adventitious roots from the lowest stem nodes. The coleoptile appears as a colourless or pale green hollow cylinder, enclosing the rudiments of the main culm. It is carried upwards by the growth of the mesocotyl. On appearance above the ground, the coleoptile ruptures at the top and the first leaf or prophyll emerges; subsequent leaves are differentiated into sheath and blade (12).

Cultivation:

There are two main seasons for growing rice in India, though three crops are taken in Tamil Nadu and Kerala. The kharif season is characterized by a gradual fall in temperature, more numerous cloudy days, low light intensity, a gradual shortening of the photoperiod, high relative humidity and cyclonic weather. During rabi, there is a gradual rise in temperature, bright sunshine, near-absence of cloudy days, a gradual lengthening of photoperiod, and a lower relative humidity.

The systems of rice cultivation in various rice growing areas of the country are largely dependent upon the rice growing conditions prevalent in the respective regions. The principal systems followed in India are 'dry', 'semi-dry' and the 'wet'. The dry and semi-dry systems of rice cultivation are mainly confined to tracts which depend on rains and do not have supplementary irrigation facilities. The wet system is practiced in areas assured of rainfall or by irrigation. In some areas, e.g. Assam, parts of Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal and Bihar, flooding is common during the rice growing season

Dry and Semi-Dry Systems: Basically, the steps involved are essentially the same under both these systems of rice-culture. The fields are ploughed and harrowed in summer for achieving the required-tilth. Farmyard manure is uniformly distributed 2-3 weeks before sowing. The seed is sown directly with the onset of the monsoon showers, either by broadcasting, dibbling behind the country plough or by drilling in lines. Line-sowing is preferable, as it ensures an adequate stand establishment and facilitates easy weeding and interculture. The reduced seed-rate requirement is another advantage. The row spacing may be suitably adjusted from 20 to 25 cm.

Under the semi-dry system, the rain-water is impounded when the crop is about 1-1/2 - 2 months old and thereafter it is converted into a wetland crop.

Wet System: Under this system, the land is ploughed thoroughly and puddled with 3-5 cm of standing water in the field. The optimum depth of puddling is found to be around 10 cm in the clay and clay-loam types of soils. The primary objective is to obtain a soft seedbed for the seedlings to establish themselves faster, to minimize the leaching losses of nutrients and thereby increase the availability of plant nutrients by achieving reduced soil conditions which facilitates a better availability of nutrient elements, to incorporate the weeds and stubble into the soil and to minimize the weed problem.

Manural application: Incorporate about 7.5 tonnes of farmyard manure/compost per ha three weeks before transplanting. At the time of Sowing apply 50- 60 kg nitrogen, 50-60 kg P205 and 50-60 K20 per hectare as basel dose a day prior to transplanting. After application of inorganic fertilizers do not allow water to flow from one plot to another. Top dress the crop with 25-30 kg. N, 25- 30 days after transplanting and another 25-30 kg N, is applied at panicle initiation stage, drain the water in the field before top dressing

Transplanting: The seedlings are ready for transplanting in kharif within 20 to 25 days (4-5-leaf stage), whereas in rabi, it may take 30 to 40 days. Two to three seedlings are planted at 20X10 cm or 20X15 cm spacing in levelled fields. In regions of low fertility, and for late planting, closer planting is advocated.

Interculture And Weed Control: First 10 days after transplanting limit the level of water to 2.5cm and increase the water level to 5cm during the rest of the growing period till 10 days before harvest, stop irrigation 10 days prior to harvest. Weed out seed plots atleast twice, before flowering, once prior or at the time of first top dressing and the second weeding at 30 days later. In addition to this chemical weed control is also suggested i.e., application of 2,4-0 ethylester 5 per cent granules 5-7 days after transplanting. After application of the chemical weedicides water should not be allowed to move from one plot another plot atleast for 24 hours. Passing of weeder 3 times at 2nd, 7th and 9th week after transplanting is also recommended for Killing of weeds, Root pruning which stimulates new rooting, Loosens the soil, facilitates easy rooting and better aeration (1, 6, 8, 9).

Diseases:

Blast: Pyricularia oryzae Cav.

Symptoms: Brown spindle-shaped lesions on leaves, lesions on stem and grain darker, rotting of neck resulting in dropping of ear, nodes disooloured. Affected grains partially filled

Control measures: Treat seed with Ceresan or Agrosan GN before sowing @ 2-2.5 g/kg; grow tolerant varieties like IR-8, Jaya; burn affected straw and stubble; spray with Bordeaux mixture 5 : 5 : 50 or Hinosan 50 E. C. @ I litre per 1,000 litres of water or Zineb (or Dithane Z-78, Dithane M-45) @ 2.5 kg! 1,000 litres of water.

Brown spot: Helminthosporium oryzae

Symptoms: Innumerable, dark-brown, eliptical spots on leaves, stem and glumes, inf1oresoence in a mature plant becomes distorted; grains fail to form.

Control measures: Treat seed with Ceresan or Agrosan GN (@ 2-2.5 g/kg); or 75% Thiram (0.25% ) grow resistant varieties.


Irregular stem rot: Helminthosporium sigmoideum var. irregularae

Symptoms: Pale, olive-brown patches on lower internodes; diseased culms hollow; a dark grey weft of mycelium and black solerotia found on the inner surface of the culm.

Control measures: Avoid the continuous flooding of fields; grow resistant varieties.


Stem rot: Leptosphaeria salvinii

Symptoms: Usually the base of the culm is affected, stem breaks, grains partially filled, numerous black sclerotia found all over the greyish weft of mycelium within the hallow culm in the basal node.

Control measures: Apply organomercurial @ 4 kg/ha at the base of the plants, burn straw and stubble, grow resistant varieties.


Sheath blight: Rhizoctonia solani

Symptoms: Lesions, white with irregular dark-brown margin on leaf sheath at the water level; leaf blade of affected sheath dries up from tip downwards; poor filling of grains.

Control measures: Seed treatment with 75% Thiram (@ 0.25%); soil application, 25 kg/ha of Thiram or PCNB before transplanting ; reduce nitrogen and spacing; tolerant varieties 'Krishna', 'CR.44-11', 'Saket.1'


Root rot: Rhizoctonia spp.

Symptoms: Restricted to nursery and occurs in patches; stunted growth of seedlings and yellowing of leaves; roots show brown discoloration and rotting

Control measures: Flood the nursery-beds with water; practice the rotation of nursery-beds.

Foot rot: Gibberella fujikuroi

Symptoms: Affected plants stunted; roots become reddish brown and rot; grains shrivelled; some of the diseased plants appear grow resistant varieties taller than others.

Control measures: Treat seed with Ceresan or Agrosan GN (@2-2.5 g/kg) or 75% Thiram (0.25%); Grow resistant varieties


Bacterial blight: Xanthomonas oryzae

Symptoms: Water soaked, translucent lesions first appear on edges near the tips of leaves, which turn yellow to white; edges of the lesions characteristically wavy, with yellow margin; leaf sheath also affected; finally a systemic infection known as Kresek occurs.

Control measures: Use seed from disease-free crop; treat the seed by soaking it for 12 hr in a mixed solution of Streptocyc1ine (0.015%) and wettable Cerasan (0.05%), followed by hot-water treatment at 52°-54°0 for 30 minutes; drain away the water from the fields as frequently as possible. Grow tolerant varieties.


Udhbatta disease: Ephelis oryzae

Symptoms: Infected ears look like straight spikes, as they emerge from the leaf sheath; covered with a white mycelium which is later transformed into a black, hard, dotted sclerotium-like body

Control measures: Hot-water treatment of seeds at 54°C for 10 minutes; solar treatment is also effective; grow resistant varieties.

Economic Potential and Uses

Rice has pherhaps the highest percentage of starch (87.2-93.5 %).

Parts used

Rice, Paddy husk, Rice bran, Rice straw, Rice flour

Uses

Rice is-the staple diet of over a third of the world's population and is consumed principally in Asia. It is a starch-rich food, cooked by boiling in water and eaten mostly with cooked pulses, vegetables, fish or meat. Several types of dishes are prepared from rice flavoured with spices and other ingredients. It is used for making fermented preparations like idli and dosa, popular in South India. Paddy is used to a limited extent for feeding livestock. Broken grains obtained during milling are used as human and cattle food, in making alcoholic beverages, and as a source of starch and flour. In India, they are used mainly for human consumption, only small quantities being utilized, along with inferior rice, for feeding animals.

Rice flour is used in confectionery, ice creams, puddings and pastry and in cosmetics. Rice starch, besides being used for food and laundering purposes, finds several applications in industry. Because of the short- age of rice and its high prices, only small quantities are used in India for non-food industrial purposes. Paddy husk is utilized mainly as fuel. It may be used in building material, for making hardboard, and as a raw material for alcohol and furfural. The bran serves as a valuable cattle feed. Rice starch is used as food in puddlings, ice cream, pies and custard powders. Rice straw is largely used for feeding cattle. It is also used in the manufacuture of straw boards, thatching and making hats, mats, sacks, ropes and baskets.


Harvesting Practices
Timely harvesting prevents losses in yield because of the shedding of grians. The later stage of grain-ripening is a dehydration process and maturity is hastened when water is withdrawn from the field at the hardening stage of the grains. The timely draining away of water makes the field hard to facilitate mechanical harvesting wherever this system is practiced. The harvesting of early and medium varieties, 25-30 days after flowering and 35-40 days in the case of late varieties is desirable. Filed paddy should be harvested when the moisture content of the grain is about 20-25 per cent. This moisture content is to be reduced to 13 to 14 per cent before milling. The direct drying under the sun leads to an increased breakage of the grains during milling. Gradual drying in the shade is essential for better recovery in the mills (1).

Produce and Products

Rice starch obtained by the wet milling process. Unmodified starches finds it's application as textile sizing and adhesives, and is useful as a wet adhesive in the paper industry. It is used in oil field applications (in its pregelatinized form) where it functions as a suspending agent and maintains drilling fluid viscosity and decreases fluid loss. In the pharmaceutical industry starch is used as a tablet binder, However, rice starch especially is used in cosmetics it is employed as a dusting powder. The starch is used exclusively as a human food, largely for baby foods and also in extruded noodles.

The produce after harvesting is known as rough rice, paddy or grain. The rough rice is milled before it is consumed. The husk or hull, the highly milled rice is white, translucent or opaque and is classified as head rice, brokens, screenings or brewers' rice, according to the size of the kernels.

The most common rice products are Parched rice, Parched paddy, Rice flakes which are prepared mainly on cottage industry basis. Rice starch is prepared from the broken grains. Rice is also used in the manufacture of Alcoholic beverages. The bran is the most important byproduct of the rice milling industry and is valued as cattle feed, it also yields edible oil. Rice bran wax is recovered during the extraction of the oil.

Brown rice syrup is an extremely versatile and relatively healthy sweetener which is derived by culturing rice with enzymes to break down the starches, then straining off the liquid and cooking it until the desired consistency is reached. The final product is roughly 50% soluble complex carbohydrates, 45% maltose, and 3% glucose. The glucose is absorbed into the bloodstream immediately, the maltose takes up to one and a half hours to be digested, and the complex carbohydrates take from two to three hours, providing a steady supply of energy. Rice syrup has a shelf life of about a year, and once opened, should be stored in a cool, dry place.

Processing Techniques

The cereal starches, such as maize, wheat, rice and sorghum, are recovered by several processes, of which the wet-milling is by far the most important. Other processes commercially employed in the manufacture of unmodified starches are the alka1i.

In all the above processes, starch is recovered in five stages: (i) softening or steeping of the grains, after thorough washing in water, (ii) glinding and sieving of the steeped grains, (iii) tabling or centrifugation of the ground mass, (iv) dewatering and drying of the final products, and (v) the recovery of important byproducts.


Wet-milling process-This process is universally adopted for the manufacture of all types of cereal starches. It makes possible a complete separation of the major components of the cereal kernels.


The raw material comes to the wet-milling plants in the form of shelled grains. The grains are screened to remove all large and small pieces of grains, chaff, sand and other foreign matter.


The screened grains are steeped in steeping conical staves in which water and sulphur dioxide are sent in. Sulphur dioxide helps in the prevention of the growth of putrefactive organisms, dispersion of proteins and in maximizing the recovery of starch. During steeping, washwater from a previous operation, concentrated to attain a soluble level of 0.10-0.20 per cent, is sent counter-currently through the steeping staves. The steep water coming out of the staves contains 40-80 g/l of total solubles and is sent to concentration plants for preparing animal feeds, mixed feeds, or fermentation nutrients.


After steeping, the grains are crushed in attrition mills and the pulverized grains are sent through a set of hydroclones under pressure. The heavier endosperm and fibre particles pass out of the bottom of the hydroclones, while the lighter germ is drawn off the top of the vortex.


The underflow from the hydroclones is sized in screens and ground in Buhrstone or Entroleter impact mills. The pulverized material is passed through screen pumps where fibres are removed as continuous mats which are washed with water, slurried, squeezed and dried. The defibred pulp, known as mill starch, is concentrated in centrifuges where protein is removed as a overflow and starch is obtained from the underflow. The starch stream is passed through a series of hydroclones to reduce the protein content to the minimum level of 0.3 per cent. In the last hydroclone, the starch is washed with softened fresh water. The starch milk from the cyclones is dewatered on moving belt filters or in basket centrifuges, and dried in flash driers.


Alkali process- In this process, the proteins in the cereal grains are dispersed by an aqueous solution of caustic soda or ammonium hydroxide. All cereal starches can be manufactured by this process. The cereal grains are crushed and sieved, the flour is slurried with an aqueous solution of ammonium hydroxide, or the grains themselves are directly soaked in a dilute aqueous solution of caustic soda in large vats. The alkaline slurry is screened in vibrating screens, the underflow is fed into a series of centrifuges where the separation of starch from the proteins occurs. The starch milk from the centrifuges is washed with water, filtered in vacuum filters and tunnel-dried. The protein solution from the centrifuges is dried in spray driers.


MODIFIED STARCHES

Natural starches are the basic source material for the manufacture of all types of modified starches. The modified starches may be classified into innumerable groups, such as thick boiling starches and thin boiling starches; or acetylated, esterified, etherified and hydrolyzed starches; or textile, laundry and paper starches and so on. The modified starches are categorized here into (i) dextrins, (ii) acid-modified starches, (iii) oxidized starches, (iv) pregelatinized starches, and (v) starch derivatives.


Dextrins

Known popularly as starch gum, white dextrins, canary dextrins, or the British gum, the dextrin is an intermediate colloidal substance formed during the hydrolysis of starch. It is marketed as a yellow or a ; white powder, or as granulated particles, or as thick brown coloured liquids, or as a white, viscous paste. Dextrins are prepared from unmodified starches and are named after the starch from which they are derived, e.g. Barley dextrin.

The dextrins are commercially prepared by two methods, viz. dry process and wet process. In the dry process, raw starch is directly heated to 200-250°C In steam, oil, or fuel heated roasters. During dextrinization, the colour of the original starch changes to yellow and brown and finally the entire mass is charred. At 150-80°C, a series of low viscosity canary dextrins are obtained, and at above 195°C a series of British gums are produced. In some manufacturing plants, small quantities of hydrochloric acid are added to the raw starch for bringing down the temperatures of dextrinization. In the wet process, dextrins are produced by acid hydrolysis, enzymic action, or by the action of Bacillus macerans on the raw starch. In actual operation, an aqueous suspension or a paste of starch is heated with a trace of acid or an enzyme and after sufficient degree of conversion is accomplished the excess acid is neutralized, the mixture filtered and the product dried. Only hydrochloric acid, nitric acid and formic acid are commercially used in this process. The roasted or the dried product is milled, sieved and packed.


Acid-modified starches

These starches are prepared by treating an aqueous suspension of starch with a dilute solution of an acid at subgelatinizing temperatures (50-55°C). The acids used for the hydrolysis of starch are hydrochloric, nitric, sulphuric, formic, acetic, propionic, and phosphoric acids. In modern industrial practices, it is usual to employ a mixture of hydrochloric and sulphuric acids. The raw Starch is treated with the acid until the desired viscosity change is obtained. The resulting slurry is then neutralized with sodium carbonate, and the acid modified starch is recovered by filtration, washing and drying.


Oxidized starches

These starches are produced by treating raw starch with oxidizing agents such as sodium hypochlorite, periodate, nitrogen dioxide, chlorine, potassium permanganate, potassium dichromate, and ozone. Commercial oxidized starches have been produced mostly from sodium hypochlorite-starch reaction, or to some extent by periodate or nitrogen dioxide-starch reaction. The effect of the other oxidizing agents on starch has been studied only at the laboratory levels.


Hypochlorite oxidized starches

A slurry of refined starch at a concentration of 20-24° Be is introduced into a reaction tank equipped with impellers for mixing the starch with chemicals. A dilute solution of sodium hypochlorite is let into the tank until the reacting solution has a pH of 8-10. As the reaction proceeds, the value of pH goes down and more quantities of the hypochlorite solution are added to maintain the original level of pH. The temperature of reaction is maintained in the range of 21-38°C. The reaction tanks are provided with a mechanical cooling device since the reaction is exothermic. When the desired amount of oxidation has been achieved, the oxidized starch is neutralized or made slightly acidic (pH 6-6.5). A solution of sodium bi-sulphite or sulphur dioxide gas is added to the treated starch to remove free chlorine present in the reaction system. The oxidized starch is finally washed in continuous vacuum filters and dried in hot air driers.


Periodate oxidized starches

These starches, known commercially as dialdehyde starches, are produced by the oxidation of the reducing and non-reducing endgroups of the starch by periodic acid. Periodic acid is manufactured by electrolysing an aqueous solution of iodine in caustic soda in electrolytic cells containing iron cathodes and lead dioxide coated lead anodes. The periodic acid is reacted with starch in polyethylene, or PVC, or glass-lined reactors. After the desired level of oxidation is achieved, the oxidized starch is washed with water, centrifuged and dried. The spent oxidants from the centrifuges are again recycled into the electrolytic cells, generating the periodic acid.


Nitrogen dioxide oxidized starches

These are prepared by reacting raw starches with liquid nitrogen dioxide in carbon tetrachloride at room temperature, or with the vapours of nitrogen dioxide, or with a mixture of sodium nitrite and nitric acid. Low temperature oxidation with a high concentration of nitric acid promotes carbonyl group formation, while the exactly opposite conditions give rise to non-uronyl carboxyl group formation.


Pregelatinized starches

This group of modified starches includes a variety of starches obtained by precooking natural, modified or derivated starches on hot rolls with or without the addition of chemical reagents. The starches have the common property of marked swelling on contact with cold water, and form colloidal dispersions reflecting the characteristics of the raw materials from which they are

derived.


Starch derivatives .

The term starch derivatives is applied to compounds evolved by the reaction of starch with other chemicals. The derivatives of starch outlined in this section include acetates, phosphates, carbamates and sulphonates of starch, hydroxyethyl starch, other types of esters and ethers, cationic starches, and cross-linked starches.

Seeds are used in folk medicine for breast cancers, stomach indurations, other tumors, and warts. Reported to be antidotal, aperitif, astringent, demulcent, diuretic, excipient, larvicidal, refrigerant, stomachic, tonic, and vermifuge, rice is a folk remedy for abdominal ailments, beriberi, bowels, burns, diarrhea, dysentery, dyspepsia, epistaxis, fever, filariasis, flux, hematemesis, inflammations, jaundice, nausea, ophthalmia, paralysis, piles, psoriasis, skin ailments, sores, splenosis, stomach ailments, and swellings. Miscellaneous Information

Bran oil contains 88 to 89% neutral lipids, 6 to 7% glycolipids and 4.5 to 5% phospholipids. Among the neutrallipids triacylglycerols account for 80 to 85%, and monoacylglycerols 6 to 6.5% of bran oil. The major phospho-lipids are phosphatidyl choline (35%), phosphattidyl ethanolamine (27%), phosphatidyl inositol (23.3%) and phosphatidic acid (9.2%). Among the glycolipids, acylated stearyl glucoside (51 %), and digalactosyldiacylglycerol (43%) are the major components, with minor quantities of monogalactosylmonoacylglycerol. It contains over eight different sterols, among which ß-sitosterol (50-60%), campesterol (15-25%) and stigmasterol (10-13%) are the major components. Fatty acid composition of bran oil is oleic (40-50%), linoleic (20-35%), and palmitic (12-20%) are the major fatty acids in bran oil making up over 75% of the acylglycerols. Minor amounts of stearic (1-2%), linolenic (1-2%), arachidic and myristic acids are so present. Thus, bran oil is typically an oleic-linoleic-type oil (3, 16).
