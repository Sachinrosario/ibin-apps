Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:
A handsome, aromatic, more or less deciduous shrub or a small tree, up to 6 mt in height and 15-40 cm. in diam., found almost throughout India and Andaman Islands up to an altitude of 1500 m. It is commonly found in forests, often as gregarious under growth. It is much cultivated for its aromatic leaves and for ornament throughout India. It is very common in India, as the leaves are an important flavouring in curries and chutneys. The species grows naturally in the Konkan, Western Ghats, Deccan, northern Circars and along the foot hills of Kumoan to Sikkim, up to 5,000 ft. It is widely cultivated in home gardens. A related species, Murraya paniculata (L.) Jack (=Murrya exotica L.) has similar medicinal uses, but not used in food.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES                                                             
Bark dark brown or almost black; leaves imparipinnate: leaflets 9-25. ovate, lamceolate or some what rhomboid, irregularly crenate, dentate, acuminate, obtuse or acute, base usually oblique, almost glabrous above, pubescent beneath, gland-dotted, strongly aromatic, the leaves have a slightly pungent, bitter, and feebly acidulous taste. They retain their flavour and maintain it even when moderately dried. The inflorescence is terminal, pedunculate, many white flowered, compact, corymbiform, cymose panicle; berries subglobose or ellipsoid, purplish black when ripe, 2-seeded. Wood is greyish white, hard and durable, weight 43 lb per cubic feet.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY                                                             

Breeding System :

Phenology :
Though there is stray flowering and fruit set throughout the year, the main season of availability of curry leaf fruits is July-August. Within 3-4 days of collection of fruits, the seeds should be pulled and sown in nursery beds or poly bags filled with soil and farmyard manure. If sowing of seeds is delayed there will be drastic deterioration in the germination of seeds, Each fruit contain 2-3 seeds.

Pollinators:                                                                                    
Bees.

Seed Biology :

Seed Germination:
Propagation in by seeds, which germinate freely under partial shade; a spacing of 15-20m ft. has been suggested for planting seedlings.

Cultivation:
Though curry leaf can be cultivated in a wide range of soil conditions, red sandy loam with good drainage will be ideal for its normal and fleshy growth which will result in better leaf yield. To certain extent it can tolerate drought also. Heavy clay with poor drainage will not be suitable for its cultivation. It can tolerate maximum temperature from 26 deg. to 37 deg C. But when the temperature falls down below 16 deg C. the vegetative buds become dormant arresting new growth of the plant. However, this varies with variety to variety. Curry leaf is propagated through seeds. Raising seedlings in nursery and planting in the main field is the normal practice being followed. For raising seedlings, good and well ripe fruits should be harvested from high yielding mother trees. Nursery beds are prepared in a well drained area since water stagnation will severely affect the germination. Beds of 1mt by1mt size with 30 cm height are formed. Adequate farmyard manure should be applied before preparing the beds. Lines are drawn at a spacing of 10 cm and seeds are sown in these lines. The seeds will germinate in about three weeks time. Seedlings can also be raised in polythene bags. One year old seedlings are the best suited for planting. in the main field pits of size 30 by 30 by 30 cm are dug at a spacing of 1.2-1.5 m one to two months before planting the seedlings. In soils of very fertility a spacing of 90 by 90 cm will be enough. Before planting the pits are filled with soil mixed with farmyard manure. One seedling is planted at the centre of the pit.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES
Pests: Among the pests attacking the curry leaf, the caterpillars of citrus butterfly is very important. Early instar larvae is dark with white patches resembling the droppings of birds. When grown up, it turns deep green in color, stout and cylindrical in shape. Hand picking and destruction of the larves can be done whereever possible. If the population is heavy, an insecticide like Malathion can be sprayed @ 1ml/lit which is less mammalian toxicity. The branches affected by psyllid bug and scale insect through the sucking of sap have to be removed then and there. If necessary a systemic insecticide like Dimethoate(Rogor) can be sprayed @ 1 ml/lit of water. But leaves should not be harvested atleast for 20-25 days.

Diseases:The plant is subjected to white sap root caused by Formes pectinatus Klotszch: a collar rot of seedlings, caused by Rhizactonia solani Kuhn. has been reported from Nagpur. To control leaf spot disease, carbendazim(Bavistin) can be sprayed @ 1ml/lit of water. Spraying sulphur compounds should be avoided since sulphur compounds spray results in leaf shedding.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used :
Leaves, root, Bark, Fruits, Wood.

Uses: 

  1. Curry leaf is a spicy leafy vegetable used in our day to day culinary preparation mainly to increase the flavour and taste of food. Without realising its significant nutritive value it is seldom consumed along with food but discarded after having used as a spice. One hundred grams of curry leaf contain 6.1 g of protein, 1.0 g fat, 18.7 g carbohydrate, 830 mg calcium, 57 mg phosphorus, 0.93 mg iron, 7.56 microgram carotene (VitamineA) and 4 mg vit C.
  2. The fruit is edible. It yields 0.76% of yellow volatile oil with neroli like odour and pepper like taste, accompanied by an agreeable sensation of coolness on the tongue. 
  3. The fruits are edible.

Harvesting Practices
At the end of first year 250-400 kg of leaves/ha can be harvested. In II year: Once in four months every time 1800 kg/ha which would work out to 5400 kg/ha per year and III year: yields 5400 kg/year. In IV year: 2500 kg/ha once in three months which would work to 10, 000 kg/ha/year. V year onwards: 5000 kg/ha once in three months which would workout to 20.000 kg/ha.

Produce and Products :
Fresh leaves on steam distillation under pressure yield 2.6% of a volatile oil which may find use as fixative for heavy type of soap perfume.

Chemistry
 The leaves of the plant are extensively employed as flavouring in curries and chutneys. Analysis of leaves gave the following values: moisture, 66.3; protein, 6.1; fat, 1.0; carbohydrate, 16.0; fibre, 6.4; and mineral matter, 4.2%; calcium 810 mg; phosphorus, 600 mg; and iron, 3.1 mg; carotene(as vit A), 12,600 i.u; nicotinic acid, 2.3 mg.;and vitamine C4 mg/ 100 g.; thiamine and riboflavin, absent. the leaves are fair source of vitamine A; they are also a rich source of calcium, but due to the presence of oxalic acid in high concentration, its nutritional availability is affected. The free amino acids present in leaves are: asparagine, glycine, serine, aspartic acid, glutamic acid, threonine, alanine, proline, tyrosine, tryptophan, gamma aminobutyric acid, phenylalanine, leucine, isoleucine, and traces of ornithine, lysine, arginine and histidine. The leaves also contains crystalline glucoside, koenigin, and a resin. twigs and leaves contain 0.8% of potash.

Processing Techniques : 
Distillation at ordinary pressure gives very poor yields of oil, while distillation with superheated steam (temp., 220 deg.) yields a dark coloured, foul smelling oil. Rectified curry leaf oil is deep yellow in color with a strong spicy odour and pungent clove like taste,

Other Uses
Curry leaf is used in many of the Ayurvedic and Unani medical prescriptions. 
  • The leaves and roots are slightly bitter, acrid, cooling, alexeric, anthelmintic and analgesic. Leaves and roots are reported to cure piles and allay heat of the body, thirst, inflamation and itching. Bark and roots are used as stimulants. The green leaves are said to be eaten raw for the treatment of dysentery. An infusion of toasted leaves is used to stop vomiting.  The powdered leaf is used to aid in the healing of fresh cuts, and a decoction of the leaves is drunk for dropsy.
  • The tribal peoples of India, especially the mundas, use its ground bark for a drink, and they rub the bark on the bitten limb as a snakebite remedy. 
  • The root is eaten and rubbed on for body ache.
  • The leaves, root and bark are considered tonic, stomachic and carminative. Leaves are used internally in dysentery and diarrhoea, and also for checking vomiting; they are applied externally to bruises and eruptions. Aqueous extracts of leaves, when administered parenterally to female guinea pigs, not only rise the phagocytic index but also mobilise a greater number of leucocytes to take part in phagocytosis; the effect does not last long. The juice of the root is taken ti relive pain associated with kidney.The wood greyish white, hard, even and close grained and durable. It is used for agricultural implements.
  • The plant in general is a tonic, stomachic and carminative.
  • The bark of the root is a stimulant, used externally to cure eruptions and venomous bites. Juice of the root is given to relieve renal pains.
  • An essential oil, a glucoside and koeinigin are reported from the species. Girinimbin was found in the bark. The essential oil is used in soaps and perfume industry.
