HABIT & HABITAT
A shrub or small tree, 5-10 m tall, considered to be a native of Iran, Afghanistan and Baluchistan, foundgrowing wild in the warm valleys and outer hills of Himalayas between 900 and 1800 m and cultivated throughout India for its delicious fruits.

Altitude: up to 1 500 m Mean annual temperature: 20 deg C Mean annual rainfall: 1 000 mm Soil type: Prefers well drained, heavy, light and medium soils. Will also do well on calcerous soils.

P. grantum grows wild in and probably originates from the Iran-Afghanistan area. The tree has been in cultivation in the Arabian countries for over 4 000 years. The tree was introduced from Syria to Egypt about 1 600 B.C. then naturalized in the Mediterranean region. The Romans named the fruit ‘malum punicum’ with regard to their suspicion the tree’s origin was Carthage, a Phoenician colony in Northern Africa. Pomegranate is thought to be the "Tree of Knowledge" mentioned in the biblical garden of Eden. P. granatum is now a widely cultivated and popular fruit all over the near and middle east, Africa and Europe. There is a continuing loss of genetic variability of cultivated stock largely because crop improvement in pomegranate has for centuries depended on the selection of spontaneous seedlings and their clonal propagation. Selection of seedlings from open-pollinated or artificially-pollinated mother trees is more recent.

Growth Habits: The pomegranate is a neat, rounded shrub or small tree that can grow to 20 or 30 ft., but more typically to 12 to 16 ft. in height. Dwarf varieties are also known. It is usually deciduous, but in certain areas the leaves will persist on the tree. The trunk is covered by a red-brown bark which later becomes gray. The branches are stiff, angular and often spiny. There is a strong tendency to sucker from the base. Pomegranates are also long-lived. There are specimens in Europe that are known to be over 200 years of age. The vigor of a pomegranate declines after about 15 years, however.

Pomegranates prefer a semi-arid mild-temperate to subtropical climate and are naturally adapted to regions with cool winters and hot summers. A humid climate adversely affects the formation of fruit. The tree can be severely injured by temperatures below 12° F.  The tree adapts well to container culture and will sometimes fruit in a greenhouse.

The pomegranate thrives on calcareous, alkaline soil and on deep, acidic loam and a wide range of soils in between these extremes. In northern India, it is spontaneous on rock-strewn gravel

Availability :  
The pomegranate is native from Iran to the Himalayas in northern India and was cultivated and naturalized over the whole Mediterranean region since ancient times. It is widely cultivated throughout India and the drier parts of southeast Asia, Malaya, the East Indies and tropical Africa. 

Native: Afghanistan, Iran, Libyan Arab Jamahiriya, Tunisia

Exotic: Egypt, Greece, India, Indonesia, Israel, Italy, Kenya, Morocco, Pakistan, Russian Federation, Saudi Arabia, Spain, Sri Lanka, Tanzania, Turkey, Uganda, United States of America

Status : Not threatened.  Cultivated in orchards and houses for edible fruits.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

Pomegranate is a small tree or shrub, with spinescent branchlets. The leaves are opposite, oblong, inclining to lanceolate, entire, smooth, with no marginal vein, 2 or 3 inches long, by 5 or 10 lines wide, obtuse, deciduous, shortly petioled, rarely verticillate or alternate, and often axillary and fascicled. The flowers are large, red, 2 or 3, nearly sessile, on somewhat terminal branchlets. Calyx turbinate, 5-cleft, thick, pale, and succulent; aestivation valvate. The corolla consists of 5 much crumpled, membranous petals. The stamens are numerous, inserted on the calyx, filaments distinct; anthers yellow. The ovary is roundish and inferior; the style simple and filiform; the stigma globular and capitate. The fruit is a large, globose pericarp, the size of a small muskmelon, leathery, crowned by the prominent hardened tube of the calyx, divided horizontally into 2 parts by a very irregular, confused dissepiment; the lower division 3-celled, the upper 5 to 9-celled; dissepiments membranaceous; placentae in the lower division at the bottom; in the upper stretching from the side of the fruit to the middle. The seeds are numerous, angular and covered with a bright red, succulent, acrid coat. Embryo oblong; radicle short and acute; cotyledons foliaceous and spirally convolute (L.—W.).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Breeding System :
This is a hermaphroditic species. Flowering is observed from mid-April to May in India. Fruiting begins in the 7th or 8th year and fruits take 6-7 months to mature. The number of fruits may vary from 20-25 for young trees to 100-150 for 10 year old trees and even 200-250 fruits for older trees. Yield varies with tree size.
Phenology :
According to seasonal changes, there are 3 flowering seasons in Deccan viz. June-July coinciding with the break of Monsoons. Feb.-March, and September. Normally, it starts fruiting in about 4 years after transplantation. Fruits ripen in about 6-7 months  from the time of flowering.

Pollinators:

The pomegranate is both self-pollinated and cross-pollinated by insects. There is very little wind dispersal of pollen. Self-pollination of bagged flowers has resulted in 45% fruit set. Cross-pollination has increased yield to 68%. In hermaphrodite flowers, 6 to 20% of the pollen may be infertile; in male, 14 to 28%. The size and fertility of the pollen vary with the cultivars and season.


Seed Biology :
Seed viability is for 36 months.  Moist chilling ie., after soaking for 24hrs, pack the seed with moist sterile material and store for 30-60days at 4° C will improve the storage life.  There are about 31 000 seeds per kg. Average germination rates between 30-70% are recorded. No seed pretreatment is required.  
Seedling Germination :

P. granatum is grown mainly from rooted and hardwood cuttings, direct seeding and air layering. Direct seeding is discouraged because of segregation. Mature stem cuttings of size 20-30 cm are planted for rooting in polybags of 20 x 13 cm with 300 gauge thickness containing a mixture of red soil, sand and well decomposed compost in equal proportions. Farm manure is applied at the rate of 10 kg per seedling. The rooted cuttings are ready for planting after 60 days in pits measuring 60 x 60 x 60 cm dug 3-5 m apart. Irrigation is vital after planting. Trees should be manured annually and trained with a single stem up to 30-45 cm or as a bush with 3 or 4 main stems. The seedlings stay for 12-18 months in the nursery.
The pomegranate can be raised from seed but may not come true.   Seed germination takes around 14-30days.  Cuttings root easily and plants from them bear fruit after about 3 years. Twelve to 20 inches long cuttings should be taken in winter from mature, one-year old wood. The leaves should be removed and the cuttings treated with rooting hormone should be inserted about two-thirds their length into the soil or into some other warm rooting medium. Plants can also be air-layered but grafting is seldom successful.

Cultivation:

Generally, P. granatum is slow growing. Lopping, pruning and coppicing are recommended management practices. Thinning should be done occasionally to ensure good aeration and interception of sunlight. Periodical spraying with 2 g mancozeb/litre of water is enough. Fruit cracking damage due to severe drought and boron deficiency is a serious problem tackled by irrigation and spraying 0.25% Boron solution. Plants should be protected from browsers. The fruit borer is controlled by removal of affected fruits and use of insecticides. Generally most pests are managed by hygienic orchard management and insecticides. Root pruning limits fruiting. When harvested unripe fruits may be stored for 5-6 months.

Location: Pomegranates should be placed in the sunniest, warmest part of the yard or orchard for the best fruit, although they will grow and flower in part shade. The attractive foliage, flowers and fruits of the pomegranate, as well as its smallish size make it a excellent landscaping plant.

Soil: The pomegranate does best in well-drained ordinary soil, but also thrives on calcareous or acidic loam as well as rock strewn gravel.

Irrigation: Once established, pomegranates can take considerable drought, but for good fruit production they must be irrigated. To establish new plants they should be watered every 2 to 4 weeks during the dry season. The plants are tolerant of moderately saline water and soil conditions.

Fertilizing: In the West, the trees are given 2 to 4-ounce applications of ammonium sulfate or other nitrogen fertilizer the first two springs. After that very little fertilizer is needed, although the plants respond to an annual mulch of rotted manure or other compost.

Pruning: Plants should be cut back when they are about 2 ft. high. From this point allow 4 or 5 shoots to develop, which should be evenly distributed around the stem to keep the plant well balanced. These should start about 1 ft. from the ground, giving a short but well-defined trunk. Any shoots which appear above or below should be removed as should any suckers. Since the fruits are borne only at the tips of new growth, it is recommended that for the first 3 years the branches be judiciously shortened annually to encourage the maximum number of new shoots on all sides, prevent straggly development and achieve a strong well framed plant. After the 3rd year, only suckers and dead branches are removed.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:  

  1. Foliar damage by white flies, thrips, mealybugs and scale insects.
  2. The roots are seldom bothered by gophers but deer will browse on the foliage
  3. Most common pest is the fruit borer  i.e butterfly Virachola isocrates Fb.
  4. Stem borer is another common insect found in neglected orchards. Caterpillar makes a hole and  bores through the branches.  

Diseases: 

  1. Fruits are subject to fruit rot especially during the rainy season. They get soft in some portions and rot. Fruit rot can be controlled by spraying with Bordeau mixture .
  2. In Punjab, it suffers from the dieback of twigs, caused by Pleuroplaconema punicae Petrak. Aspergillus castaneus Patterson causes discolouration of fruits and seeds, disfigurement of the fruit is caused by Sphaceloma punicae.
  3. Dry rot is caused by Zythia versoniana Sacc.
  4. Other  minor diseases are leaf and fruit spot caused by species of Cercospora and Gloeosporium. Besides these, it also suffers from canker and dieback caused by Ceuthospora  phyllosticta and leaf spot  caused by Pestalotia sp.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Fruit, seeds, flowers

Uses

Dye & Tan: Dry rind contains 18-22% tannin, Stem bark contains 10-25%, leaves about 11% and root about 28%. 

  • The roots of the plant yield a yellow and red dye which is used for colouring cotton, woollen and other fabrics.
  • The flowers also yield a fleeting dye of a light-red colour.    In India it is used for dyeing clothes. 
  • Dry rind contains 18-22% tannin and it tans leather soft and greenish brown.  With other tans, it gives alight grey colour.  It is  frequently employed as an auxiliary to other colouring agents (generally turmeric or indigo) in dyeing. Fruit rind gives yellowish brown to khaki shades and is used for dying wool and silk. It is, however, generally used with some other dye as a concentrator, in which case the pulverized rind is boiled along with the dye.  Alone, it imparts to cloth the greenish colour known in the North-West provinces as kakrezi.  When used for this purpose the rind is boiled in water till three-fourths of the latter has evaporated and the cloth then dipped in concentrated infusion. 
  • Bark is similarly used as a dyeing auxiliary, but its chief value is as a tan and dye for leather.  It is largely employed in preparing the Morocco leather of Tangiers.  
  • A tannin content of no more than 0.25% in the edible portion is the desideratum. Many studies have shown that tannin is carcinogenic and excessive ingestion of tannin from one or more sources, over a prolonged period, is detrimental to health. 


Harvesting Practices
Usual harvesting season is October -December. Fruits are harvested when the skin turns slightly yellow and the fruit gives out a metallic sound when tapped. Each tree bears about 100 fruits. Harvested fruits can be stored for a few weeks and they show improvement  on keeping.

Produce and Products
Anardana, juice, syrup, oil, tannin, dye

Chemistry

Bark and the rind of the fruit contain 22.25% tannin. Rootbark contains punico-tannic acid 20-25%. Other constituents present are mannite, sugar, gum, pectin ash 15%. Active liquid alkaloids pelletierine and isopelletierine are present. Also traced are two sugars.

Rind was found to contain a moderate amount of yellow colouring matter, readily given up to boiling water, which gave colours varying from a dull-yellowish green to a bright reddish-drab with tussur and corah silk, and cotton.  The rind contains around 8% Ellagic acid and 10% tannins.

Flowers contain a pigment called Pelargonidin 3,5-diglucoside.

Other Uses
  1. In northern India, a major use of the wild fruits is for the preparation of "anardana"–the juice sacs being dried in the sun for 10 to 15 days and then sold as a spice.
  2. Seeds yield an oil which possesses antibacterial properties.
  3. Fruit - good source of sugar and vitamin C and fair source of iron. 
  4. Fruit juice - used for the production of wine.
  5. Dried bark - used for the treatment of tapeworm , astringent, anthelmintic, cooling and strengthening of gums.
  6. Fresh juice is used as an ingredient of cooling and refrigerant mixture and of some medicine  for dyspepsia. Rind is valued as an astringent in case of diarrhoea and dysentery.
  7. Sweet fruits - mildly laxative while the less sweet is good in inflammation of stomach and in heart pain.
  8. Powdered flower buds - used in bronchitis.
  9. The rind is valued as an astringent and used in combination with aromatics and used for treating diarrhea and dysentery as well as for dyspepsia, colitis, piles and uterine disorders.
  10. In southern Orissa a paste prepared  from the unripe fruit or leaf buds is mixed with water in which rice has been washed, and a pinch of opium is administered to check diarrhea.
  11. In northeastern Karnataka, the juice of the flowers, mixed with that of Cynodon dactylon leaves is applied in the nose to control nose bleeds.
  12. Food: The seeds have a fresh, sweet-sour, very pleasant taste, grenadine, a reduced juice from fresh pomegranate seeds, is common in Northern India for desserts, marinating and tenderizing meat due to its proteolytic enzymes. Dried pomegranate seeds, ‘anardana’, has culinary importance as spice for vegetable and legume dishes in Northern India. They impart a subtle, sweet-sour and tart flavour popular in Punjab and Gujrat. The edible fruit portion contains 10% sugars, 36.6 mg Vitamin C/100 ml of juice, 2.6% protein, 0.1% Phosphorous, 0.4% Potassium, 0.03%, 0.1% Calcium and 0.13% Magnesium.  Extract of different parts exhibit antibiotic activity. Extracts of the whole fruit have shown high activity against Micrococcus  pyrogenes var. aureus, E. coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. They are very effective against intestinal pathogenic bacilli.
  13. Fodder: The leaves are browsed by domesticated stock.
  14. Fuel: Tree branches used as firewood.
  15. Timber: The wood is hard and durable, mostly used in making farm implements.
  16. Medicine: The bark of the pomegranate tree may be used as a very strong purgative, but it has several serious side-effects. The fresh root bark is used in an anthelmintic preparation, the alkaloid punicine is responsible for this activity. Unripe fruit and flowers are significant emetics. Ripe fruits are laxative and blood enriching also useful in managing sore throat, spre eyes, brain diseases and chest troubles.
  17. Erosion control: This deep rooting tree is important in soil erosion control, and is planted along rivers to stabilize banks.
  18. Shade or shelter: Pomegranate is an important shade tree and its wind firm stature makes it a good wind break.
  19. Reclamation: P. granatum is a drought tolerant tree suitable for arid and semi-arid zone afforestation. A resilient tree whose withering is mentioned as the judgement of God upon evil on the earth.
  20. Soil improver: Pomegranate leaf litter decomposes slowly and is suitable for mulching.
  21. Ornamental: Leaf resprouts of the pomegranate are a beautiful light red, making it a suitable ornamental choice for gardens and amenity parks, especially in cultivar ‘nana’. The beautiful persistent calyx in pomegranate was inspiration for King Solomon’s and later other European crowns.
  22. Boundary or barrier or support: The tree is sometimes used for fencing.
  23. Intercropping: Pomegranate grows along well with grapes in Mediterranean countries.
  24. Pollution control: Used in water purification.
  25. Other uses: Greatly mentioned in the Bible, the skirt of the priest’s blue robe and ephod was adorned with the reprints of pomegranates.

Economic Potential To The Country

Geographic regions of cultivation/wild population:

Wild or semiwild  plants still exists in the north of Syria, Gilead and on Mount Carmel. Also found  in various regions from Caucasus to Afghanistan.

According to De Candolle , Vavilov and others, Pomegranate originated in South-West Asia probably in Iran and some adjoining countries.

It has been classified into two subspecies, Chlorocarpa and Porphrocarpa each having two varities. These subspecies have been  established on the basis of the colouring of the ovary, a stable feature which is retained when they are reproduced by seeds. Subspecies Chlorocarpa  is mainly found in the Transcaucasus , whereas the second, Porphyrocarpa  is  mainly Central Asian in distribution.

Several types are cultivated in Indian. Among the numerous types grown, the Bedana and Kandahari are considered the best.

 Miscellaneous Information

Two subspecies are recognized on basis of ovary colour; subsp. chlorocarpa and porphyrocarpa. Numerous cultivars, some dating to the 13th Century, are known. The specific epithet granatum derives from Latin granum "grain" and means "many-grained". Only two species, P. granatum and P. protopunica, are known for this monogeneric family with close affiliations to the Lythraceae. P. protopunica is endemic to Socotra and is listed as an endangered plant in the IUCN Red List.
