Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Coriander is an annual semi-erect to erect herb. Stem is soft, non-hairy and hollow, frequently pigmented at the lower portion. Growth habit is indeterminate with alternate branching. Leaves are tender, fragrant and dissected. Inflorescence is compound umbel with small white or pink flower. The fruit is a schizocarp that consists of two mericarps. Fruit is globular yellowish-brown, almost round, 3-4 mm in diameter with straight and curving in distinct ridges, aromatic, having a peculiar aroma and flavour. The Indian varieties are more or less oblong in shape. The Russian varieties are smaller in size and have higher oil content.

Climate and soil
Coriander requires a dry and cold climate. It is grown as a winter crop (Rabi crop) all over North India. Coriander thrives well in fully exposured to sunlight but with less heat and medium-to-heavy loamy soil, good drainage and well-distributed moisture (2). When grown for leaf purposes, coriander is grown throughout the year under irrigation. In Tamil Nadu, it is also grown as a kharif season crop (June-July) depending upon the agroclimatic conditions and soil moisture. Cloudy weather during flowering and seed setting stages favour diseases and pests. Loam to clay loam soils having good water holding capacity, acidic to neutral pH are favourable. Heavy frost especially during flowering and fruit setting stages, result in poor seed setting. Dry, cool weather during seed formation stage favours high setting. After stem elongation, crop is resistant to drought. Coriander for fruit production is grown in tropical highlands, subtropics and temperate regions and for green herb it is grown in lowland tropics (1).


Availability :

Centre of Origin is Mediterranean region. Known only in cultivation. Spread throughout the world. Forms with ovoid fruits are from India and those with small, globular fruits are from China. In all South East Asian countries it is grown as a culinary herb and vegetable. Cropping for its fruit is restricted to higher altitudes (1).
Conservation/ Threat Status : Cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Erect, annual, glabrous, usually profusely branching herb. Up to 1.30m tall with a well developed tap root. Stem solid, subterete, up to 2 cm in diameter, older internodes sometimes becoming hollow, sulcate, mostly with a white bloom, light green with darker green ribs, sometimes with some violet. Leaves alternate, rather variable in shape, size and number, with a yellow green scariously margined sheath surrounding the supporting stem for up to 3 quarters of its circumference; petiole and rachis subterete, sulcate, light green; blade white waxy, shiny green often with darker green veins; basal 1-3 leaves usually simple, withering early, often in rosette, blade ovate in outline, deeply cleft or parted into usually 3 incised-dentate lobes; next leaves decompound, petiole 0-15 cm long, blade ovate or elliptical in outline, up to 30 cm x 15 cm, usually pinnately divided into 3-11 leaflets; all higher leaves compound, petiole restricted to the sheath, blade divided into 3 leaflets of which central one is largest, each often variously divided into ultimately sublinear, entire, acute lobes (1).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:
Inflorescence an indeterminate compound umbel; peduncle up to 15 cm long; bracts sublinear, 0-2 up to 11 mm long; bracteoles 0-6, linear up to 1 cm long; usually each umbellet has bisexual peripheral flowers and central male flowers; calyx in all flowers with 5 lobes; corolla with 5 white or pale pink petals, heart shaped, very small (1 mm x 1 mm) in male flowers, in bisexual peripheral flowers 3 petals are larger; stamens 5, filaments up to 2.5 mm long, white; pistil rudimentary in male flowers, in bisexual with inferior ovary, a conical stylopodium bearing 2 diverging styles up to 2 mm long, with a papillate stigma each (1).

Fruit
An ovoid to globose schizocarp, up to 5 mm in diameter, yellow-brown with 10 straight longitudinal ribs alternating with 10 wavy longitudinal ridges often crowned with the dense persistent calyx lobes and the stylopodium with styles. Does not split at maturity, contains 2 mericarps which each bear on their concave side 2 longitudinal, rather wide lines (vittae), containing essential oil (1).

Breeding system and varietal improvement:
A facultative cross-pollinator.

Phenology: Flowering is protoandrous and starts in the primary umbel, about 50-90 days after sowing (1).


Pollinators:Geitonogamy is predominant mode. Insects including bees are also involved in pollination (1).

Floral and seed biology:
Seed Biology
One per mericarp, with testa attached to fruit wall.


CUTIVATION ASPECT:
Seed germination:
Epigeal germination. For better germination these fruits are split into halves (each is a mericarp) by trampling under feet on smooth floor. Split fruits are treated with Bavistin or Agrosan GN or Thiram @ 2.0 gm/kg seed before sowing. Germination occurs above 4°C, but optimal temperature is 17-20°C for genotypes with small fruits and 22-27°C for genotypes with larger fruits (1).

Seedling development:
Taproot thin with many lateral roots; hypocotyl up to 2.5 cm long; cotyledons opposite, oblanceolate, up to 3 cm x 4 mm, pale green.

Released varieties:
CO.1, CO.2, CO.3, Guj. CO.1, Guj. Cor.2, Rajendra Swathi, RCr-41, Sadhana, Swathi, CS-287, Sindhu, Hisar Anand, Azad Dhania-1, RCr-20, RCr-435, RCr-436, RCr-684, Pant Haritima, Hisar Sugandh, UD-446, DWD-3 and CIMPO S-33.

Chromosome number:
2n=22

Selection of planting material:
Seeds are selected for planting. Heat treatment of fruits after harvest or before sowing, either artificially or exposed to sun, will promote physiological ripening and break seed dormancy. Splitting of fruit into mericarps enhances germination and better distribution. For fruit production, plant density should be 15-40 plants/m2 and higher for green herb (1).

Planting:
In Northern India, coriander is grown as rabi crop and optimum time for its sowing is between 15th October to 15th November. In southern part summer crop is planted in the month of May where as rabi crop is sown in October. Seed rate is 10-15 kg/ha. Sowing method is broadcasting of seeds. Spacing is 30 cm between rows with a depth of 2-3.5 cm.

For unirrigated crops, field preparation should be started as soon as the reserved moisture of rainy season comes down to proper stage. First ploughing should be done with soil turning and then 2-3 ploughing or harrowing may be done to bring the field to fine tilth. Field should be immediately planked to check the loss of any moisture and break the clods. Under irrigated conditions first ploughing may be done after presowing irrigation if reserved soil moisture is in adequate. In heavy soils deep ploughing during summer is useful in reducing weeds, insects and pests as well as improve better absorption and retention of moisture. 10 to 20 tons organic manure/ha may be added to the soil at the time of field preparation. In addition to this full dose of 30 kg Nitrogen and 30 kg P2O5/ha should be applied for unirrigated conditions. For irrigated crops the amount of Nitrogen should be increased to 60 kg/ha. For irrigated conditions, however, complete dose of P2O5 and 1/3 of Nitrogen i.e. 20 kg/ha may be added at the time of field preparation.

Management after planting:
Of the remaining 40 kg, 20 kg may be applied by broadcasting with irrigation after first weeding and thinning is done i.e. 40-45 days after sowing. Last dose of 20 kg/ha may be applied with irrigation at the time of grain formation.

Fertilizer recommendations for major coriander producing states:
State Recommendations under irrigated condition full dose of fertilizer may be added to the soil at the time of field preparation
Rajasthan 10-20 t of well decomposed FYM + 60:30:40 Kg NPK/ha + neem cake, FYM is applied at the time of field preparation, out of which 20 kg N, 30 kg P and 20 kg K/ha is applied at the time of sowing for irrigated and unirrigated crop where as for irrigated crop and the rest in two equal splits one at the time of irrigation (30days) and second at flowering stage (75 DAS) 20:30:20 NPK/ha is recommended for non-irrigated conditions.
Gujarat 20 t FYM + 60:60:30 kg NPK/ha. N is applied in two split doses as basal and at flower initiation stage and P & K are applied as single basal dose in last ploughing.
Tamil Nadu 10-15 t FYM + 20:40:40kg NPK/ha.
Andhra Pradesh 10-15 t FYM + 30:40:20 kg NPK/ha in single dose in last ploughing under irrigated conditions.
Bihar 60:40:20 kg NPK/ha under irrigation, N applied in two equal splits at sowing and 60 DAS.
Uttar Pradesh 60:30:30 NPK/ha as basal dose.
Haryana 60 kg N & 50 kg P/ha as basal dose.
Under irrigated condition full dose of fertilizer may be added to the soil at the time of field preparation

Irrigation and Drainage: Coriander is often grown as rainfed crop. However, under irrigated conditions 4-6 irrigations after germination are given. Stagnation of water may be harmful for crop hence proper drainage must be provided for drain out excess water.
Weed Control and Inter-culture Operations: First weeding must be done 30 to 45 days after sowing. Subsequent one or two more weeding may be done if required. Shallow hoeing during first weeding is particularly useful in unirrigated crop as it checks soil moisture losses and enhances aeration. Excess plants should be thinned out at the time of first weeding so that proper plant-to-plant distance is achieved. Chemical weed control: Pre-sowing application of pendimethalin @ 1.0 1/ha or oxiflurofan @ 0.15 1/ha, uniformly spraying as mixing of the weedicide on the top most soil; or pre-emergence application of fluchloralin @ 1.0 1/ha has been found effective to control the common weeds of the crop.


MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
Aphids Hyadaphis coriandri and white fly Myzus persicae, Aphis sp. Cavariella sp. Brevicoryne sp. infect the crop.
Symptoms: Aphids suck the sap from the tender parts and flowers and plant turn to yellow and result in shrivelled and poor seed formation.
Control: Spray Endosulfan (0.05%) twice just at the time of floral initiation and after 15 days thereafter with Monocrotophos or Quinalphos (0.1%) or Dimethoate (1.5%) or
Methyldemethon (2.0%) or Phosphomidon (0.5%) thrice @ 400-500 lits/ha at 10 days interval. Spray in the evenings only.

Heliothis & Caterpillar Brevicoryne coriandri
Symptoms: Attack is at stage of seed formation, infestation is more severe on the young inflorescence, whole plant become whitish yellow in appearance. Reduction in yield and seed become shrivelled.
Control: Foliar application of 0.03% Phosphomidon or Dimethoate or Methyldemethon (0.05%). Foliar application of Sulfex 3 gm or Dicofol 3 ml/L of water, 2-3 times at 10 days interval or spraying Endosulfan 0.07% twice just at the time of floral initiation and 15 days after or two sprays with Monocrotophos/Quinalphos (0.1%) at 15 days interval.

Red mites Petropbia latens
Control: One light irrigation can save the crop in irrigated areas. In rainfed areas spraying 0.1% H2SO4 can save the crop and smoking around the border of coriander field is also recommended.
Frost: Crop is very vulnerable to frost damage at flowering and seed formation stage (1).



Diseases:

Fusarial Wilt: Caused by Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. corianderi, causing yield loss of about 25% in Rajasthan. Crop loss as high as 60% is reported.
Symptoms: Disease affected plants appear drooping and later dry up. This is a typical vascular wilt. Infection results in growth retardation. In affected plants seed set is attached sometimes leading to sterility. Both pre and post emergence rots are noticed.
Epidemiology: Disease is seed and soil borne. Pathogen can remain viable in seed for 5 months and for longer time in soil. Primary source of inoculum is infected plant debris in soil. Soil pH of 5.8-6.9 is highly favourable for disease development. Soil moisture has got an important role in disease incidence. Mortality of 37% has been reported in irrigated soils compared to 14% in unirrigated soils. Ambient temperature of 24-27°C was found optimum for disease development even though disease is noticed in 20-29°C. Soil moisture of about 50-60% is found ideal for disease development. This being a typical wilt, the fungus colonises the vascular system and plugs the xylem leading to wilting and death of plants.
Control: Organic amendments were found to have suppressive effect on disease. Recent studies have shown that seed treatments with Trichoderma viride and soil applications of neem cake along with T. viride are effective. UD-20 has been reported wilt resistant. Variety Sindhu, CS203, Swati, Sadana, Gujarat coriander 1 & 2 are reported to be tolerant. Treat seeds with Bavistin 0.1%. Sow in the first/second week of November for reducing disease incidence. Biocontrol agent T. viride in combination with foliar spray of either Hexacomazole or Thiophenate methyl on 25th, 40th and 55th DAS and soil application of neem cake @ 150 kg/ha reduces the incidence. Seed dressing with spraying twice with Carbendazim at 1 and 2 months of crop growth reduced disease incidence. Adopt crop rotation, give deep summer ploughing, and use disease free seeds.

Powdery mildew: Caused by Erysiphe polygoni. Just like in cumin, powdery mildew causes severe damage to foliage. About 50-52% loss has been reported from Maharashtra.
Symptoms: White patches appear in leaves and stem which later merge covering the entire leaf surface. Infection is generally noticed during the time of flowering on leaves, stem and inflorescence. Umbels dry up and premature sterility and poor seed set are noticed.
Epidemiology: Infection is generally noticed in February-March period. Dry season and high temperature favours disease development.
Control: Early sown crop during October showed less disease compared to late sown crops. Like cumin powdery mildew, sulphur dusting when the crop is tow months old and again after fruit set is found effective. In Gujarat, Sulphur dusting @ 25 kg/ha followed by 3 sprays of Dinocap (0.05%) were found suppressive. Varieties Gujarat coriander 1,2, RZ41 were found tolerant. Variety Swati, an early maturing variety escaped the diseases.

Stemgall: Caused by Protomyces macrosporus. Soil borne though not serious. Noticed in some parts of Bihar in serious form. Crop losses of 23% were reported from Madhya Pradesh. It also causes loss in quality particularly with respect to flavour, protein, carbohydrates and amino acids.
Symptoms: Tumor like swellings appear on stem, petioles, pedicels and the size of tumor varies according to plant part infected.
Epidemiology: Hyperplacy of the cortical cells are responsible for tumor like growth. Leaf debris and chlamydospores appear to be the primary source of infection. Disease is severe in irrigated soil. Lower temperatures, abundant dew with higher day temperature predispose the plants to infection. Infection is minimum at low soil pH 3.6 and increase with pH up to 4. Roots showed maximum infection of 43.9% and 40.1% at pH 7.4 and 8.4 respectively. At lower dose of P disease incidence is noticed.
Control: Seed and soil treatment with Thiram @ 25g/kg seed gave better protection. Besides foliar spray with Thiram, Captafol 0.2% and Caroboxin 0.1% also gave protection. Cultivars UD48, UD4, UD46, Swathi, RCR41, UD20 are highly resistant.

Blight: Caused by Alternaria poonensis.
Symptoms: Appears as dark brown spots on stems and leaves.
Control: Spray 500-700 Lits. solution of 0.2% Indofil 45 or 0.1% Bavistin per ha. Blisters appear on the leaves and stem of infected plants which deforms the seeds and reduces the yield and quality of seeds. Selection of disease free seeds, collection and destruction of affected plants, growing coriander variety RCr-41, seed treatment with Agrosan GN/Thiram (2.5g/kg) or Bavistin (1g/kg) of seed. Spraying of Bayletenten or Systhane (0.4 ml/L of water) repeat after 20 days. Spray 500-700 Lits. of 0.1% solution of Bavistin and repeat the spray twice or thrice at 20 days interval.

Root rot: Caused by Rhizoctonia solani
Control: Drenching Brassicol or Banstin 0.1% twice at 20 days intervals.

Grain mould: Caused by Alternaria sp, Curvularia sp, Helminthosporium sp.
Control: Spray 0.1% carbendazim (0.1%) 20 days after flowering or captan (0.2%) twice at grain set and at 15 days interval (1).

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Coriander seed, the dried ripe fruit and leaf of the plant used both in culinary and medicinal purpose. Seed whole or powdered is used as spice. It is one of the most extensively used spices in Indian cookery and forms an essential ingredient of curry powders (up to 40%), pickling spices, spice mixtures, garam masalas etc. whole plant, when freshly bruised, gives out a peculiarly strong, rather obnoxious odour similar to that associated with bugs. Happily, when the plant grows and matures, these disagreeable traits are wholly lost and the ripe fruits are completely free from them (1). Indian coriander or dhania seeds are sweet and are used for flavouring tea. This is called 'Dhania tea'. Our ancestors gave this kind of decoction for memory improvement. Dhania chutneys are popular especially in the winter months (5). Flavour of seed is aromatic and pleasant with characteristic texture and colour, used to flavour confectioneries, pastries, desserts, biscuits, baked foods, ice cream, chewing gums, meat industry, bakery items, beverages, cookies, cakes, soups, salads, liquors and various other preparations. Volatile oil of seeds is used in perfume and soap industries. Oil is also used as a source of pleasant aroma in preparation of chocolates, candy, soup, wine and canned food materials. Also used to subside bad odour of certain allopathic medicines. Fresh green leaves and tender stems are used in vegetable, chutneys, soups, sauces and salad.

Parts Used:

Seeds, stems and leaves

Harvesting Practices:
Done soon as top umbels start turning yellow. Delayed harvesting results in poor quality. On an average a yield of 4.0 to 7.0 q/ha may be obtained from unirrigated and 6.0 to 10.0 q/ha for irrigated crop. For green herb entire plant is uprooted about 60 days after sowing. Yield of fruits can exceed 4 t/ha. Rainfed crops produce 400-700 kg/ha of fruits. Irrigated crops usually 2 t/ha (1).

Post Harvest Handling
Dry the crop preferably in shade as handling it help retaining attractive grain colour. Seeds can be separated by beating crop bundles with wooden sticks followed by winnowing. Split fruits are usually not accepted in market for medicinal purposes. Seeds should be stored in gunny bags after properly drying in humidity free stores. Once crushed it will lose its essential oil very fast. Excess humidity causes quality deterioration.


Produce and Products:
Coriander fruit, ground seeds, fruit oil.

Medicinal uses
Ayurvedic medicines prescribed for indigestion and urinary diseases. Volatile oil of coriander is also used to subside bad odour of certain allopathic medicines. Coriander seeds are considered to be carminative, diuretic, tonic, stomachic, antbilious, refrigerant and aphrodisiac they are used in preparation of medicines (1). Leaves are acrid, astringent, aromatic, analgesic, anti-inflammatory, emollient, thermogenic, anthelmintic, stomachic, carminative, digestive, appetiser, constipating, diuretic, antipyretic, stimulant, expectorant and anodyne. Useful in vitiated conditions of pitta, burning sensation, cough, bronchitis, pharyngopathy, vomiting, dyspepsia, anorexia, colic, flatulence, diarrhoea, dysentery, chronic, conjunctivitis, cephalalgia, epistaxis, erysipelas, strangury, dropsy, scrofula, helminthiasis, haemorrhoids, intermittent fevers, hyperdipsia, gout, rheumatism and giddiness. Leaf paste is good for allergic inflammations caused by 'Marking nut' (3). In early hindu medicine, coriander was mentioned as a treatment for constipation & insomnia, and an aid to child bearing. In medicine, now used to mark the taste of other laxatives & to lessen gripping tendencies (4). The oil has been researched at Cairo University for its effects in lowering glucose and insulin levels and supporting pancreatic function. Coriander's analgesic and tonic properties make it suitable for headaches and neuralgia. It also stimulates appetite in cases of anorexia, and fatigue, enhances circulation and is used for hemorrhoids, poor circulation, and fluid retention (6).

Chemistry:
Essential oil is 0.1 - 2.0 %. Small fruited types have highest content. Oil consists of several monoterpenoids. Main component is linalool (>60%). Other components are: a-pinene, ?-terpinene, geranyl acetate, camphor and geraniol. Coriander fruits from Indian subcontinent contains lesser essential oil, with little or no camphor, myrcene, limonene but much linalool.

Per 100 g edible portion seeds contain approx.: water - 11 g, crude protein - 11 g, fatty oil - 19 g, carbohydrates - 22.9 g (starch 11 g, pentosans 10 g, sugar 1.9 g), crude fibre - 28 g, mineral constituents 5 g and essential oil 1.0 g.

Fatty oil in endosperm of ripe fruits vary from12-25% and is dependent on environmental conditions. Major fatty acid (60%) is petroselinic acid (C18:1(6C)), which is the isomer of oleic acid (C18:1(9C)) that is also present. Other components are linoleic acid, palmitic acid, stearic acid, vaccenic acid and myristic acid. Use as culinary herb is based on volatile comounds with a bug-like odour contained in the roots, stems and leaves.

Per 1 g fresh leaves about 4 mg of essential oil is present. About 41 volatile components have been detected in the foliar essential oil including alkenals in the C9-C16 range, C7-C17 alkanals, C10-C12 primary alkenols, alkanols, and nonane. Aldehydes make up more than 80% of these volatile compounds. Herb also contains provitamin A (up to 12 mg/100 g), vitamin B2 (up to 60 mg/100 g), and vitamin C (up to 250 mg/100 g). 1000 seed weight is 7-17 g. In US, regulatory status 'Generally regarded as safe' (GRAS) is accorded to coriander fruits (GRAS 2333) and coriander fruit oil (GRAS 2334) (1).

Coriander fruit oil contains: 63.8% linalool, 5.5% camphor, 4.9% para-cymene, 4.6% ?-terpinene, 3.3% a-pinene, 2.4% limonene, 1.8% geraniol, 1.2% myrcene, 1.1% d-3-carene, 1.0% geranyl acetate, 1.0% ß-pinene, 1.0% a-terpineol, 0.8% trans-linalool oxide (5) (furanoid), 0.7% anethole, 0.6% camphene, 0.6% cis-linalool oxide (5) (furanoid), 0.6% terpinen-4-ol, 0.5% carvone, 0.1% borneol, 0.1% citronellol, 0.1% eugenol, 0.1% 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-one, 0.1% neral, 0.1% neryl acetate, 0.1% sabinene, 0.1% a-terpinene, trace carvacrol, trace ß-caryophyllene, trace caryophyllene oxide, trace 1,8-cineole, trace p-cymen-8-ol, trace geranial, trace (Z)-3-hexnyl butyrate, trace a-humulene, trace menthol, trace a-thujene, trace thymol (Source: anitescu et al., 1997).

Coriander leaf oil contains: 15.6% (E)-2-dodecenal, 12.7% (E)-2-tetradecenal, 12.1% (E)-2-decenal, 9.3% decanal, 8.2% (E)-2-decenol, 5.3% (E)-2-undecenal, 5.0% dodecanal, 4.8% 2-pentadecenal, 2.8% phytol, 2.5% (E)-2-tridecenal, 2.3% undecanal, 2.3% undecenal (unknown isomer), 2.1% 1-decanol, 1.7% tetradecanal, 1.5% 1-eicosanol, 1.4% tridecanal, 1.4% 1-docosanol, 1.0% didecenal (unknown isomer), 0.9% 2-hexadecenal, 0.7% tetradecenal (unknown isomer), 0.7% decenal (unknown isomer), 0.6% 2-dodecenol, 0.5% octanal, 0.5% pentadecanal, 0.5% 9-decenal, 0.4% 1-tetracosanol, 0.4% noname, 0.3% nonanal, 0.2% 2-undecenol, 0.2% pentadecenal (unknown isomer), 0.2% 4-decenal, 0.1% germacrene B, 0.1% tridecenal (unknown isomer), 0.1% 1-dodecanol, 0.1% trans-5-methyltetrahydrofurfurylal, 0.1% 1-undecanol, 0.1% methyl octadecanoate, 0.1% hexadecanal, trace 2-tridecen-1-ol, trace undecane, trace limonene, trace phenylacetaldehyde, trace hexadecanal (unknown isomer) (Source: Potter, 1996) (7).

Export potential:

The main importers are USA, the UK, Malaysia, Singapore, Sri Lanka the Netherlands, Germany and Japan. Besides coriander as such, India also exports its powder and essential oil (2). Export: 12,574 t (source: Spices Statistics, Spices Board, 1996-97).

Miscellaneous Information

 · Monograph on physiological properties of coriander fruit oil has been published by Research Institute for Fragrance Materials (RIFM).
· Commercial essential oil is sometimes adulterated with sweet-orange oil, cedar wood oil, turpentine and anethole oil. Aromatic taste of green coriander is provided by substitutes Eryngium foetidum L. - 'Mexican coriander' and Persicaria odorata (Lour.) Sojak - 'Vietnamese coriander' (1).
· The name 'coriander' is based on the Greek work 'kopis' which means 'bug' (2).
· References to coriander are found in Sanskrit writings, while coriander seeds were placed in Egyptian tombs.
