HABIT & HABITAT

Semecarpus anacardium L.f.,  Anacadiaceae, the marking nut tree, occurs in the wild in the sub-Himalayan tract, up to 3,500 ft., in Assam, Khasi hills, Madhya Pradesh;, Gujarat, Konkan, and the deciduous forests of the southern states of India. Found in the outer Himalayas from the Sutlej to Sikkim, and fairly common throughout the hotter parts of India as far as Assam.
It is a moderate-sized deciduous tree, 

A common tree of dry deciduous forests, it is frequently found in drier rather than damp localities.  It grows on a variety of soils over different kinds of rocks thereby showing no specific soil affinity.

Availability :  
The species is distributed in tropical outer Himalayas of India and Nepal.  It ascends up to  1100m.  Fairly common in 'Shorea' forests.  Also found in southern India and northern Australia.

Globally the species i distributed in the Paleotropics, from Tropical Asia to Australia.  Within India it is distributed in the Sub-Himalayan tracts, Central, Peninsular and Eastern regions of the country.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

Bark dark-brown, rough, leaves, simple 10.0-30.0 cm, obavate-oblong; flowers small, dull greenish-yellow, dioecious, in terminal panicles, drupes 2.5 cm long, obliquely ovoid, smooth and shining, black when ripe, situated on a fleshy orange-coloured receptacle.

Seed Biology :

The nut is described as black, glossy and smooth, about 1 inch in length, subcordate, obtuse, and flattish.

Seeds do not retain their viability for more than 6 to 9 months and they should be sown soon after collection.  Seeds per kilogram vary from 460 to 880.  

The marking nut, Semecarpus Anacardium, is a fruit closely allied in its source and properties to the cashew nut (q.v.). Like the cashew nut, the true fruit is black, oily and bitter.   The kernels of the nut are edible, but the juice of the nut is highly vesicant, and has been traditionally used to mark cloth by washer men. The marking nut is a native of the East Indies, where the extremely acrid juice of the shell of the fruit in its unripe state is mixed with quicklime and used as a marking-ink. The juice also possesses medicinal virtues as an external application, and when dry it is the basis of a valuable caulking material and black varnish. The seeds are edible, and the source of a useful oil.

Seedling Germination :
Seeds have poor viability and should be sown soon after collection. Seedling are frost-sensitive but have good power of recovery. They are found to damp off at a tender stage.

Seeds collected  are subjected to scarification, ie., nuts are treated with concentrated sulphuric acid for 10 to 15 minutes and washed thoroughly in running water.  Seeds are sown in polybags in December and germination is noticed within 15 to 27 days.  A layer of hay is used over polybags for maintaining uniform heat during germination.  Germination varies from 20 to 57 percent.


Cultivation:
It does not stand transplanting well.  Seedlings of 5cm height can be transplanted to polythene bags.  One year old plants can be Planted in the field in 45cm cube pits adopting a spacing of 8 x 8m.

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used : Fruit

Uses

Dyes and Tans: 

  • The pericarp of the fruit contains a bitter and powerfully astringent principle, which is universally used in India as a substitute for marking ink.  It gives a black colour to cotton fabrics, which is said to be insoluble in water, but soluble in alcohol.  The juice of the pericarp is mixed with lime water as a mordant before it is used to mark cloth.  It is believed this substance is an ingredient in some of the marking ink preparations sold at the present day in Europe.  
  • In some parts of Bengal the fruits are regularly used as a dye for cotton cloths.  They are employed either alone or with alum.
  • The fruit of this tree, better known as the Marking-nut tree, is used in medicine and as an ingredient in varnish.  When pounded and boiled in rape oil it is applied to stop putrefaction in hides.
  • The nuts of this tree are used by tanners, especially in dressing the hides of the rhino's and buffalo to form targets.

Gum:

  • The tree yields an acrid viscid juice, from which a varnish is made.  It is usually described as a coarse black gum in amorphous carbonaceous masses with a shining coal like fracture and having a dull brownish-black colour.  It is said to be not that useful for commercial purpose.
  • The tree bark exudes a gum resin used in leprosy, venereal infections and nervous debility.   Juice from the nut is used in ascites, rheumatism, asthma, neuralgia, epilepsy and psoriases, as well as for warts and tumours.   The juice of the nut was effective against epidermal carcinoma.   It also has some antidiabetic activity.   Nut bruised and the exudates is used as an abortifacient and a vermifuge.

Other Uses

  1. Fresh orange cup (hypocarp) of the fruit is eaten when  ripe, Astringent.
  2. Pounded and boiled in rape oil, the fruit of this tree makes an excellent remedy for staying putrefaction when begun in a hide.
  3. The kernels contain a small quantity of sweet oil.  The pericarp contains 32 percent of a vesicating oil of specific gravity 0.991, easily soluble in ether and blackening on exposure to the air.  
  4. The acrid juice of the pericarp is a powerful vesicant and is often employed for producing fictitious marks of bruises.
  5. The yellow fleshy cup on which the fruit rests is somewhat acrid in the fresh state, but when roasted in ashes it takes the flavour of a roasted apple and is eaten.  The kernels of the nuts are also eaten.  They are supposed to stimulate the mental powers, especially the memory.
  6. The ripe fruit is described in Hindu works off medicine as having acrid, heating, stimulant, digestive, nervine and eschrotic properties.  It is used in dyspepsis, skin diseases, rheumatism, sprains, leprous affections, piles, and nervous debility etc.,
  7. Oil from the seeds is also used as a preventive against the attacks of white-ants and as a lubricant to the wooden axles of native carts.  This brown-black oil having strongly vesicant properties, is derived from the mesocarp of the fruit. Serious eruptions of an eczematous nature follow the vesication produced by it, and bloody alvine discharges and disordered urination have resulted from its application. 
  8. The leaves employed as plates. 
  9. The wood  is employed for making charcoal.
  10. The juice of the nut wall is known in the trade as Bhiloawan Shell Liquid, is rich in phenols.   The non-vesicant form of the juice is used in the paint industry and in water proofing
  11. Anacardic acid, cardol, catechol, anacardol, semecarpol and an oil are reported from different parts in use.
  12. It is used quite extensively in medicine, being prepared for internal use by boiling it with cow-dung and subsequently washing with cold water. The Hindus use it for dyspepsia, neurasthenia, hemorrhoids, and cutaneous diseases (Dymock).
