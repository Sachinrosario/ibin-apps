Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Moringa oleifera is a small to medium sized deciduous tree. Native to sub-Himalayan tracts of northern India up to an elevation of about 500 m, but it is now grown world wide in the tropics and sub-tropics. In Himalayan region it is chiefly confined to stream beds and depressions where sufficient moisture is available. It does not grow on dry hill slopes where the soil is shallow and dry. It is a tree of subtropical climate. In its natural habitat, the absolute maximum shade temperature varies from about 38° to 48°C, the minimum temperature from about -1 to 30C and the annual rainfall from about 750 to 2,000 mm. Though it can grow on a variety of soils, it prefers alluvial sandy soil with sufficient moisture supply. It avoids stiff clayey soil with impeded drainage (1, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
The tree can reach 12 meters in height with a trunk 30cm wide, usually tree is cut back to one meter or less from the ground. Bark is thick, soft, corky, deeply fissured, branches and stem brittle. Leaves leathery, alternate, tripinnate, 30-60 cm long, rachis thickened and auriculated at the base, pinnae and pinnules opposite. Leaflets small and many, 1.3–2 cm long, 0.6–0.3 cm wide, lateral ones somewhat elliptic, terminal one obovate and slightly larger than the lateral ones. The leaflets are quite pale when young, but become richer in colour with maturity. Fruit capsular, 30-120 cm long, 1.8 cm wide, more or less trigonous, pendulous, 9 ribbed, 1 celled and many seeded (1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 11).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Flowers fragrant, white or creamy-white, 2.5 cm in diameter, spreading panicle, bracts linear, 3mm long. Calyx cup shaped, 5 cleft, the lobes 13-15 x 4 mm, linear lanceolate, reflexed, puberulous outside. Corolla 15-20 x 7-10 mm, spathulate, unequal, viened. Stamens yellow with subulate filaments and are fertile. Ovary glabrous, sessile, ovule solitary (1, 3, 5, 6, 9, 10).

Phenology :
The leaves turn yellow and fall in December-January. White flowers in numerous clusters appear from January to April, while new flush of leaves appears about February-March (6, 7, 12).

Seed Biology :
Seeds trigonous, 2.5 cm long with wings on angles. About 8,000 to 9,000 seeds weigh one kilogram. The seeds does not store well and loses viability considerably during the first year's storage (6, 7).

Seedling Germination :
The seeds exhibit no dormancy and germinate readily on getting the favourable conditions. Fresh seeds have high germinative capacity. The seeds take about 7 to 10 days to germinate. Germination is epigeous. The growth of the seedlings is rapid. They attain a height of about one metre by the end of the first growing season. Growth ceases in December when the seedlings become leafless and starts again in February. Fairly rapid height growth is maintained during the first few years (7).

Cultivation:

Moringa oleifera can be raised by seeds or by planting out either entire plants or stumps. It can also be raised by planting large branch cuttings. Sowing in the nursery is done in June. Sunken beds alternated by irrigation channels are made after working the soil well. Addition of compost encourages seedling growth. Sowing is done in lines 20 cm apart and the spacing maintained between the seeds in lines is about 2 cm. Seeds are sown at a depth of about 1 cm. Deeper sowings delay and reduce germination. About 30-35 g of seed is sufficient to sow about 1 m2 of nursery area. The nursery beds are irrigated after sowing. Flooding of nursery beds is, however, avoided. The nursery beds are kept regularly weeded. On completion of germination, the seedlings are spaced about 10 cm in lines. The seedlings become fit for planting out after one year.

Either entire plants or stumps are planted out in July-August in pits of 30 cm3 dug during summer months to permit weathering of the soil. In the case of entire plants, the seedlings are uprooted from the nursery with care and with balls of earth. The root portion with balls of earth is wrapped in moist gunny bags to prevent desiccation. Planting out should be done with the least possible delay after uprooting the seedlings from the nursery. Planting is preferably done on cloudy or rainy days. The spacing adopted is generally 3 x 3 m for block planting and 5 m for line planting.

For stump planting, the stumps are prepared from one year old healthy seedlings. These are wrapped with moist gunny bags and carried to the planting site. Stumps are planted in 30 cm3 pits or in crow bar holes. Planting should preferably be completed during July as late planting gives poor survival.

Large branch cuttings of about 2 m length and having sufficient hardwood are also used as planting material. Branch cuttings should be straight and well formed and should be taken from healthy and well grown trees. These are planted in pits of about 50 cm3 to ensure that about 0.5 m bottom portion of the branch cutting is put firmly in the soil. Regular watering is necessary to ensure that the cuttings strike roots. Protection of the cuttings from cattle and goats is also essential.

Trimming: If left alone, a Moringa tree has a tendency to grow up straight and tall, putting out leaves and pods only at its crown. To encourage production of many branches and pods within easy reach of the ground, cut off the central growing tip when the tree is 1.5 to 2m high. If the trees are being grown for pod production, it has been recommended to remove the flowers the first year as this will increase the pod yield the second year.

Fertilizing: Moringa trees will generally grow well without fertilizers. In some parts of India, however, ring trenches are dug about 10 cm from trees during the rainy season and filled with green leaves, manure and ash, then covered. This is said to promote higher yields of pods. Research done in India showed that applications of 7.5kg farmyard manure and 0.37kg ammonium sulfate per tree can increase pod yields threefold.

Varieties: Considerable variety exists within the M. oleifera species. In India, Moringa hybrids have been developed to reflect local tastes for the fruit. A variety called Kodikalmurungai found in Tamil Nadu produces short 15-23cm pods. The Jaffna variety in southern India produces fruit 60-90cm long. Chavakacheri muruna, another Jaffna type, can produce fruits up to 120cm long and Chemmurunga types are said to flower throughout the year and produce heavy yields. One Indian variety cultivated as an annual crop will fruit in 150-165 days, bear continuously for one year and then become exhausted (6, 7, 9, 10, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

In India, various caterpillars are reported to cause defoliation and are controlled by spraying. The budworm Noordia moringae and the scale insects Diaspidotus sp. and Ceroplastodes cajani are reportedly able to cause serious damage. Cattle, sheep, pigs and goats will eat Moringa seedlings, pods and leaves. Protect Moringa seedlings from livestock by installing a fence or by planting a living fence around the plantation. Termites can be a problem, especially when cuttings are planted. Among approaches recommended to protect seedlings from termite attack are

  • Apply mulches of castor oil plant leaves, mahogany chips, tephrosia leaves or Persian lilac leaves around the base of the plants.

  • Heap ashes around the base of seedlings.

  • Dry and crush stems and leaves of Mexican poppy and spread the dust around the base of plants.

Other pests in India are Aphis craccibora, the borer Diaxenopsis apomecynoides and the fruit fly Gitonia sp (6, 7, 10, 12, 14).

Diseases:

The tree is not affected by any serious disease in India. In very water-logged conditions, Diploidia root rot can occur. Fungi which attack the drumstick tree are: Cercospora moringicola (Leaf spot), Sphaceloma morindae (Spot anthracnose), Puccinia moringae (rust), Oidium sp., Polyporus gilvus (6, 7, 10, 12, 14).

Economic Potential and Uses

Oil extracted from the mature pods (oil of Ben) is yellowish, non-drying, with good keeping qualities. Used in arts and for lubricating watches and other delicate machinery. Oil is clear, sweet and odorless, never becoming rancid; consequently it is edible and useful in the manufacture of perfumes and hairdressings and to some extent is a substitute for sperm-whale oil. Oil is also used by cosmetic industry and is used for culinary purposes. The oil has also been reported to have been used extensively in the 'enfleurage' process whereby delicate fragrances are extracted from flower petals. More recently the oil has also been shown to be particularly effective in the manufacture of soap producing a stable lather with high washing efficiency. In India, seed oil considered to be a tonic, purgative and is applied externally to relieve pain and swelling in case of gout or rheumatism, it is also used to treat prostrate and bladder troubles, hysteria, scurvy and to treat skin diseases. The press cake left after oil extraction from the seeds can be used as a soil conditioner or as fertilizer. The seed cake could be used as animal feed if it is pre-treated to remove its alkaloid and saponin content (6, 10, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20).

Uses

Food: The leaves, a good source of protein, vitamins A, B and C and minerals such as calcium and iron, are used as a spinach equivalent. They are an excellent source of the sulphur-containing amino acids methionine and cystine. Young plants are eaten as a tender vegetable and the taproots as an alternative for horseradish. Young pods are edible. The green peas and surrounding white material can be removed from larger pods and cooked in various ways. The flowers can be eaten or used to make a tea and they are good source of bee forage.

Wood/Timber: The wood is very soft and light and is useful only for light construction work. The soft and light wood is an acceptable firewood for cooking but makes poor charcoal. Wood pulp is highly suitable for making newsprint and writing paper. Bark used for tanning hides and wood yields a blue dye.

Fiber: Bark, when beaten, produces a fiber used to make small ropes and mats. Studies have shown that it is suitable as a raw material for the production of high alpha cellulose pulp for use in cellophane and textiles.

Gum or resin: When the tree is injured, the stem exudes a gum that is used in calico printing, as a condiment, and for stomach and bladder ailments. The mucilaginous gum has a bland taste and belongs to the hog series of gums.

Seeds: Powdered seeds can be used to clarify honey without boiling. It can also be used to clarify sugar cane juice. Powdered seed kernels act as natural flocculants, able to clarify even the most turbid water.

Moringa leaves provide an excellent material for production of biogas. Juice extracted from the leaves can be used to make a foliar nutrient capable of increasing crop yields by up to 30%. Leaves can also be incorporated as Green manure. It is suited to areas where strong winds and long, dry spells occur simultaneously, causing serious soil erosion. Planted as a hedge in courtyards, M. oleifera provides wind protection, shade and support for climbing garden plants. Widely used for live fences and hedges in Kenya, Nigeria, Tanzania, India, and elsewhere. The species is widely planted as an ornamental (5, 6, 9, 10, 13, 15, 16, 19, 20 ).

Harvesting Practices
When harvesting pods for human consumption, harvest when the pods are still young and pliable for eating whole. Older pods develop a tough exterior, but their pulp and immature seeds remain edible until shortly before the ripening process begins. When producing seed for oil extraction, allow the pods to dry and turn brown on the tree. In some cases, it may be necessary to prop up a branch which holds many pods to prevent it breaking off. Harvest them before the pods split open and fall to the ground. Within three years a tree will yield 400-600 pods annually and a mature tree can produce up to 1,600 pods. Pods are collected in May-June, dried in the sun, thrashed and winnowed to separate clean seed (7, 12, 14).

Produce and Products

Pods are used as vegetable. Seeds, leaves and flowers are eaten. An excellent oil is extracted from the seeds which is of commercial value. Press cake is used as fertilizer. Apart from that all plant parts have medicinal importance.

Processing Techniques

Moringa seed has a fairly soft kernel, so the oil can be extracted by hand using a screw press (also known as a "spindle" or "bridge" press). The seed is first crushed, 10% by volume of water is added, followed by gentle heating over a low fire for 10-15 minutes, take care not to burn the seed. Such test yielded 2.6 litres of oil from 11 kg of kernels (16).

Other Uses
Leaves: In India, juice from leaves is believed to have a stabilizing effect on blood pressure and is used to treat anxiety. In Senegal, a infusion of leaf juice is believed to control glucose levels in cases of diabetes. Leaves are eaten with honey and followed by a drink of coconut milk 2 or 3 times a day, provide a remedy for diarrhea, dysentery and colitis. Leaf juice, sometimes added with carrot juice is used as a diuretic and skin antiseptic. Eating leaves is recommended in cases of gonorrhea on account of its diuretic action. In India and Nicaragua, leaves and young buds are rubbed on the temple for headache. In India and the Philippines, a poultice made from fresh leaves is applied to reduce glandular swelling. In India, leaves used to treat fevers, bronchitis, eye and ear infections, scurvy and catarrh. Leaves are considered to be anthelmintic (able to kill intestinal worms). Leaves are used as an irritant and as a purgative. In the Philippines, eating leaves is believed to increase a woman's milk production and is sometimes prescribed for anemia.

Flowers: Flowers are traditionally used as a tonic, diuretic, anthelminitic and abortifacient. Used to cure inflammations, muscle diseases, tumors and enlargement of the spleen. In India, juice pressed from the flowers is said to alleviate sore throat and catarrh. In Puerto Rico, an infusion of the flowers is used as an eyewash and a decoction from the flowers has been used to treat hysteria.

Pods: Pods are believed to be anthelminitic and are used in affections of the liver and spleen, and in treating articular pains (pain in the joints).

Roots: In India, roots are used as a carminative (promotes gas expulsion from the alimentary canal, against intestinal pain or spasms) and as a laxative. Roots are used as an abortifacient, purgative, diuretic and a cardiac and circulatory tonic. Roots are also useful against intermittent fevers, to treat epilepsy, nervous debility, hysteria and sometimes chewed to relieve cold symptoms. Juice from roots is applied externally as a rubefacient (skin tonic), counter irritant or vesicant (agent to induce blistering). In Senegal and India, roots are pounded and mixed with salt to make a poultice for treating rheumatism and articular pains. In Senegal, this poultice is also used to relieve lower back or kidney pain. A decoction of roots is used to cleanse sores and ulcers. A snuff made from roots is inhaled to relieve earache and toothache. In India and Indo-China roots are used to treat cases of scurvy. A juice made from a combination of fresh roots, bark and leaves is inserted into the nostrils to arouse a patient from coma.

Root bark and stem bark: In India, root and tree bark are used to treat sores, skin infections and are taken as appetizers and digestive. In India, a decoction of the root bark is used as a fermentation to relieve intestinal spasm and is considered useful in calculous affections (mineral buildup/kidney stones). In Senegal, a decoction of root bark, roots, leaves and flowers are used to treat epilepsy, hysteria, and intestinal spasms. Stem bark is used to cure eye diseases. In India, stem and root bark are believed to be aphrodisiacs and anthelmintic. In India, root bark is said to prevent enlargement of the spleen and formation of tuberculous glands of the neck, to destroy tumors and to heal ulcers. Juice from root bark is put into the ear to relieve earaches and also placed in a toothache cavity as a pain killer. Bark is regarded as useful in treating scurvy and it is used in the treatment of delirious patients. In India, bark is sometimes mixed with pepper corns and used as abortifacient, rubefacient and as a vesicant.

Gum: Gum, mixed with sesame oil, is used to relieve headaches. This is also poured into ears for the relief of earache. It is given for intestinal complaints. In India, gum is used to treat syphilis, rheumatism and for dental problems. In India and in Senegal, gum is considered useful in treating fevers, dysentery and asthma. In India, Gum is considered to be diuretic, astringent, rubefacient and abortifacient.

Seeds: Seeds are used against fevers and paste of crushed seeds is spread on warts. Seeds are also used as a remedy for various tumors (5, 6, 10, 15, 16, 18, 19, 22, 23 ).

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

This species is one of the most useful plants. All parts of the tree except the wood are edible, providing a highly nutritious food for both humans and animals. The flowers are a good nectar source for honey and an excellent oil is derived from the seeds, which is used for cooking and lubrication of delicate mechanisms. Many parts of the plant have been used in medicinal preparations. M. oleifera is also of interest because of its production of compounds with antibiotic activity such as the glucosinolate 4 alpha-L-rhamnosyloxy benzyl isothiocyanate. Other research has focused on the use of M. oleifera seeds and fruits in water purification (6, 20).
