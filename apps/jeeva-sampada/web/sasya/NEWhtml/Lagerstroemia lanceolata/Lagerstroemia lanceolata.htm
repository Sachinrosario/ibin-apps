Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:

Moderate to large deciduous tree Distributed in Deciduous forests of the western coast ascending up to 1300 m altitude. Prefers gneiss soils to laterite and does best where rainfall is heavy. Occurs in Deccan peninsular India.

Conservation/ Threat Status :

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

A large tree with ash-colored bark, peeling off in large strips. Leaves elliptic-lanceolate, acute, glabrous above, usually hoary-tomentose beneath; main nerves 8-12 pairs, prominent. Flowers in large usually compound panicles, pedicels short, pubescent. Calyx usually hoary-tomentose; tube hemispheric, lobes triangular, acute, patent or often reflexed. Petals oblong-obovate, with a long slender claw. Capsules ellipsoid, not ribbed. Seeds cultriform.

Floral and seed biology:

Seedling Germination: Natural reproduction takes place by seeds which fall early in the hot season and germinate in the beginning of the rains; the seeds are light and are carried by winds to some distance from the tree and germination appear to be uncertain. Seedlings do not stand exposure to sun and benefit by slight shade, bare loose soil aids natural reproduction which appears freely on abandoned cultivation. Limited grazing suppresses undergrowth and favour natural reproduction. The growth is moderately fast.

 

MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:

The tree is susceptible to attack by white spongy rot (Daedalea flavida Lev.), white stump rot (Fomes durissimus Lloyd) and tar spot (Rhytisma lagerstroemiae Rabenh.).

Cercospora lythracearum Heald & Wolf var. macrophora Chiddarwar – leaf spot on both the surfaces

Fomes durissimus – heart rot.

Rhytisma lagerstroemiae Rabenh – tar spot on the upper surface of leaves in Belgaum (Karnataka) and Coimbtore (Tamil Nadu)

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts Used: Bark, whole plant,fruits and leaves.

Uses:

Wood good fuel.Wood chiefly used for building construction, bridges, ships and boats. Also used for railway carriages, wagons, motor lorry and bus bodies, and handbarrows. Suitable for commercial plywood, matchboxes and splints, trestle stands and shooting sticks of spring-back type. Used for furniture, boxes, grinding mills, agricultural implements, carts, spokes and felloes, general carpentry purposes, golf stick shafts and picker arms, and appears promising for badminton and tennis rackets. Useful for turnery, cooperage and bent-wood work. Recommended for electric transmission poles and railway sleepers. Reddish timber used for bridges, coffee boxes, furniture.


Harvesting Practices: Logs should be protected from sun and hot winds. Good results by felling trees ten months after girdling and storing sawn stock for 18 months.

Produce and Products:

Tanins.

Chemistry:

Calorific value: sapwood - 4956 cal, 8921 Btu; Heartwood - 5078 cal, 9141 Btu.

Processing Techniques: Difficult to season. Liable to end splits and warps. Kiln seasoned in 12-15. Easy to season as planks. Thick planks liable to surface cracking. Logs converted green and immersed in water for 4-6 months, followed by stacking and drying, yields better results.

Other uses:

Leaves used as green manure in arecanut gardens.

Tanin is present in leaves, bark and fruit. Bark - Bark yields tannin.

Miscellaneous Information


 Wood: Sapwood distinct from heartwood, greyish or with pinkish tinge. Heartwood light reddish-brown turning to walnut-brown on exposure. Wood moderately hard, moderately heavy, average weight 640 kg/m3 (40 lb/ft3) at 12 per cent moisture content; medium coarse-textured with straight to somewhat interlocked-grain. Strong, elastic and heavy. Specific gravity 0.7, 42 lb./ c ft.

A ring-porous to semi-ring-porous wood. Growth rings distinct, delimited by large early-wood pores. Pores in the early wood large, clearly visible to eye forming a prominent zone at the beginning of the growth ring; late wood pores moderately large to small, mostly solitary or in short radial multiples of 2-3; usually filled with abundant tyloses; vessel lines distinct on longitudinal surfaces. Soft tissues predominantly aliform, often connecting adjacent pores by narrow wavy extensions particularly in the extreme late wood. Rays very fine, not visible to eye but seen under the lens as fine, numerous closely-spaced lines.

Wood similar to Jaru (Lagerstroemia speciosa Pers.) but can usually be distinguished by the confluent parenchyma in the late wood, which is more interrupted and less conspicuous than in jarul.
