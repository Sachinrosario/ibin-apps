Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

It is tuber plant with a modified stem growing from the tuber that grows to a length of approximately 30feet (9meters).The stem is a tendril. The plant is a herb, most suitable when planted in shady areas. The yams are usually planted near tall trees (usually the breadfruit tree) and have poles extending from it to the higher branches of the tree for the vines of the yam to climb.

Dioscorea alata is Asiatic (South-East Asia) in origin. It is grown nearly in all parts of the tropics but extensively cultivated in the West Indies, the Pacific island and tropical Asia. In India it is cultivated practically in all the states but the major Yam producing states are Kerala, West Bengal, Bihar, Orissa, Assam, Gujarath and Maharashtra.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

Stems square, winged, twining to the right, green or purplish, spineless; leaves variable in shape and size, opposite, ovate, deeply cordate, acuminate, glabrous, 10-30 x 5-20 cm; petiole 6-12 cm long. Tubers variable in size, shape and colour, usually produced singly; often very large, and tubers weighing 60 kg have been reoported, but usually 5-10 kg; tubers mostly cylindrical, from long and serpentine to almost globular, but branched, lobed, flattened and fan-shaped tubers occur in some cvs; outer cortex and flesh varies from white to deep reddish purple in colour. Axillary tubers or bulbils formed in leaf axils, but not as freely as in some other spp. (1, 2,7,12)

Flowers globose in axillary inflorescences; male flowers in axillary panicles up to 25 cm long, green; female flowers in simple, solitary, glabrous, short spikes; majority of cvs seldom produce fertile seeds and some are completely sterile. (1, 2,7,12)

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Breeding System :

Yams are vegetatively propagated dioecious species. In Dioscorea alata, clonal selection had been the only mode of improvement as flowering is erratic and many of the clones are sterile. But two female clones showed coincidal flowering along with the male clones which led the way for generation of sexual seedlings.

Evaluation of tuber yield of the genetic stocks resulted in the isolation of two superior high yielding types with desirable tuber shape shape and good cooking quality, they are Sree Keerthi and Sree Roopa. (2,12)

Phenology :

Seed tubers are normally planted during the later part of the dry season (March-April) and start sprouting with the onset of premonsoon showers and harvesting is done between 8-9 months from planting. (1, 2)

Pollinators: (2)

Anthesis occurred in male flowers by 12.30 hours and in female flowers between 13.00 and 14.30 hours. Male flowers remained open for 4-5 h while female flowers stayed open for 9-11 days. Natural pollination was primarily absent but parthenocarpic fruit development was observed in rare cases. For hand pollination a simple and efficient method was developed using a pencil. Pollen remained viable for 4-5 hours whereas stigma receptivity lasted for 9-10 days. The optimum time of pollination was between 12.00 h and 15.00 hours. Several factors such as relative humidity, atmospheric temperatures below 300C and non-bagging of female flowers promoted fruit set.

Seed Biology :

Majority of cvs seldom produce fertile seeds and some are completely sterile; fruits trans- versely elliptic, 3-winged, about 2.5 cm long and 3.5 cm broad; seeds orbicular, winged all round. (1, 2,7,12)

Seedling Germination :

The normal growing period is 8-10 months and the tubers have a dormancy period of 3-4 months before sprouting. (2)


Cultivation: (1, 2,11,49)

Climate: Yams are essentially tropical plants with temperature range of 25 to 30 degree celsius. And growth is restricted below 20 degree celsius. Day length of 12 hours is favourable for vine growth and shorter day length is favourable for tuber formation.

Soil Condition: Yams are relatively tolerant to dry conditions, but thrives best when supplied with ample moisture but at the same time good drainage should also be available.

Planting Material: The most common method of propagation is by tuber and it is commercially most important method. The most successful planting material is the whole seed Yam tuber.

Seed material: 250-330 g per stand for Dioscorea alata and thus 2500-3000 Kg of seed material is required to cover one hectare of land. The larger the set size higher is the yield.

Seed Dormancy: The cutting of large tubers into sets is done 1-2 days before the envisaged date of planting, this helps the cut surface to heal over before placing on the ground. While preparing the set, care must be taken to keep the skin-cut surface ratio as high as possible. Dusting the cut surface with ash is a common traditional practice. Treating with pesticides to avoid damage of the set is also done under certain conditions.

Spacing: 75cm X 75 cm with a plant density of 18000 per hectare.

Staking: Yams must be staked for optimizing the yield.

Season: Seed tubers are normally planted during the later part of the dry season (March-April) and start sprouting with the onset of premonsoon showers.

Interculture operations and Weed Control: Earthing up is done along with fertilizer application. Earthing up is an important operation to cover up the growing tuber which is exposed by rain as well as to reassemble the fertile soil moved away by rains from the Yam rhizosphere.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests: (2)

Spotted Beetle

  1. Galerucida bicolor Hope attacks and defoliates the leaves of yam.

  2. Crioceris impressa Fb. - Grubs feed on the tender leaves and succulent tissues of shoot tips. Adult feeds on mature leaves by perforating leaf blade without affecting leaf veins. Adult is stout 7-9 mm, fulvous beetle. Female lays 250- 300 eggs in groups of 3 to 5 on leaves or on stem. The grubs emerge out in 3 to 4 days and pass through three instars in 5 to 8 days. It pupates inside the soil and adults come out after 10-13 days.

  3. Lema lacordairei Baly. attacks D. alata in Kerala. It feeds on leaves and tender vines. Adult is small (8 mm long) beetle with blue elytra and yellow body. Eggs are laid in groups of 10-12 on leaves or vines. On an average, the incubation, larval and pupal periods last for 2.5, 6.5 and 8 days respectively. The grub is yellowish with thick fleshy abdomen and it carries its excrement on its back. Pupation takes place in a white frothy cocoon. Longevity of adult is 90-95 days. It appears in May and is found up to November. The damage caused by defoliation is seen often serious. It is often regulated by a tachinid parasitoid Pseudalsomiya sp.

Caterpillars:

  1. The common cut worm Spodotera litura is a leaf feeder causing severe defoliation.

  2. Pericallia ricini Fb. is a leaf eating hairy caterpillar causing severe damage when the crop is left untrailed.

  3. Hornworms- Hippotion celerio Linn., H. oldenlandiae Fb. and Theretra gnoma Fb. are leaf eating ones.

These caterpillars are sporadic on yams. It was observed that hairy caterpillars and cut worms in the yam growing areas of Kerala and Andhra Pradesh. They are of minor importance.


Sawfly

In south India, larvae of Senocolidia dioscreae R. was reported as a leaf feeder. A number of larvae defoliate by feeding together often in rows. It is sporadic in nature. The pest was later reported as S. purpurata (F. Sm.).


Spidermites

In 1973 it was observed Dioscorea as a new host for Tetranychus telarius Linn. which was later on identified as T. cinnabarinus.

Scale Insects

The white scale insect (Aspidiella hartil) is an important pest of yams in India. Among the different species of Dioscorea, D. alata, D. esculenta and D. rotundata were found attacked by the pest, while D. hispida and D. bulbifera were relatively free from attack. This insect also infests ginger, turmeric, taro and elephant foot yam. The field infestation ranged from 1 to 100 per cent among different varieties and species of Dioscorea. In severe cases of infestation, attacked plants show drying due to continuous sucking and desapping.

The males are winged and free living. The females are wingless, covered by a dirty white scale and parasitic. It lays eggs inside the scale itself. The emerging out nymphs are known as crawlers. They move about in search of a suitable feeding site. Once a crawler starts feeding, it settles down and looses its legs and becomes sedentary. Soon a white secretion covers the body and becomes hard to form a scaly cover. Male has one instar less than the female. The life cycle is completed in 11-20 days. The scale insects are small (1-2 mm) in size. Nymphs and females infest the basal vines and tubers. During January to April, they multiply very fast in large numbers covering the entire tubers. The infested tubers start shriveling affecting the quality, viability and marketability. The pest problem is perpetuated through seed tubers. The nymphs are parasitized by an aphelinid Physcus comperi H. and an encyrtid Adelencyrtus moderatus H..

White Grubs

The white grub, Leucopholis coneophora Burm., attacks root and tuber. The extent of infestation was such that the cultivation of yams has to be abandoned for years. It is prevalent in Kerala state. During severe incidence of white grubs, the intensity of damage was assessed as 10 to 35 pits per tuber and each gnawed pit measured 15-20 mm deep. The tuber yield loss was in the range of 4 to 9 per cent in greater and lesser Yams. The damaged tubers are adversely affected and loose the quality and culinary taste. The field infestation was recorded in the range of 4 to 46 per.

Termites

The species identified attacking yams are Odontotermes obesus (Rambur) and cherichi. These occur during prolonged rain less dry hot periods. It attacks basal vine and tubers and is an occasional pest of local importance. It is noted in Orissa, and Andhra Pradesh. During 1980-81, the termite incidence was severe in Trivandrum with 0 to 100 per cent attack on yams.

Nematodes

The association of yam nematode Scutellonema bradys with dry rot of Dioscorea alata was reported in India as early 1967. This spiral nematode is a migratory parasite invading root and tuber through root tips and cracks of skin. Eggs are mainly laid in the plants where they hatch and develop into adults and all stages of the life cycle are ineffective. It causes a disease of tuber commonly known as 'dry rot'. It feeds intracellularly causing cell destruction and necrosis. The nematode infection is restricted to 1-2 cm of tuber in the peridermal and sub-peridermal layers. In the early stages of infection, yellow lesions can be observed just below the tuber skin and these become brown resulting into a continuous dark dry rot layer just beneath the surface in heavily infected tubers. The infected tuber exhibit cracks in the skin and at times shows skin flakes off exposing dark brown dry rot. Due to nematode infection, the quality, consumption and market values are lost.

Meloidogyne spp. causes malformation and reduction in size of tubers. It is found infecting the root and tuber in large numbers.

Rat

The tubers of yams are reported to be attacked by field rat Bandicota bengalensis, it is only of minor importance.


Storage Pests

Scale Insect

When Aspidiella hartii infested tubers are stored along with uninfested tubers it multiplies and spreads rapidly. If the infested tubers are used as planting material, it results in poor germination and growth and establishment. The pest multiplies in the, field perpetuating the problem.


Arecanut Beetle

Araecerus fasciculatus De Geer often found damaging the tubers as well as dried yam chips. It was observed that the damage due to this pest renders the tubers unfit for consumption as well as for seed purpose.

Nematodes

The yam nematode Scutellonema bradys causes storage loss. Whenever the infected tubers are stored, the nematode continue feeding, causing severe dry rot and wet rot. Moisture loss is significantly greater in infected tubers during storage than the non-infected ones.


ResistantlTolerant Varieties

Among the different species of Dioscorea, D. bulbifera, D. hispida and, to certain extent, D.rotundata are relatively tolerant to scale insects. About 190 accessions of D. alata available in the germplasm collection were evaluated for the scale insect resistance/susceptibility during five cropping seasons (1978 to 1983). Of which, 97 were found infested with scale insect. Among these, 38 accessions had recurring infestation for more than three seasons. The remaining 93 accessions were identified as resistant or tolerant to scale insect. Depending upon the grade of scale infestation on tubers, the infested 97 accessions were grouped into mild (70), moderate (9), severe (9) and very severe (9) in susceptibility to the scale insect.

The white grub damage was free in as many as 174 accessions of D. alata, while 16 accessions were identified as susceptible with maximum damage (46 per cent) in Da 172. During 1980-81, the termite attack was very serious in the farm of CTCRI, Trivandrum. About 156 accessions did not show any infestation, 34 accession showed termite attack with cent per cent infestation on Da 15, Da 35, Da 58, Da 102, Da 135, Da 176, Da 177 and Da 187.

Control

Incidence of leaf feeding beetles, caterpillars, mealy bugs and/or saw fly can

be controlled with Malathion or Carbaryl (0.05 per cent) spray. Application of Sevidol at the rate of 20-25 kg/ ha was recommended against white grubs and termites.

The scale insect can be controlled in the field using pest-free seed tubers and dipping seed tubers before planting in 0.1 per cent Monocrotophos if scale infestation was noted during storage.


Diseases: (2)

Anthracnose of Yams

Anthracnose of D. alata caused by Colletotrichum gloeosporioides was first found to be devastating in nature in Rajasthan and responsible for 70 to 80 per cent loss in yield under favourable climatic conditions. Later, the same pathogen has been reported on D. esculenta from Kerala. This pathogen was found to infect other non-edible and medicinal yams such as D. composita Hemsl.The disease is now wide spread and occur every year on all Dioscorea spp. but in severe form only in D. alata.

  • Symptom

The disease first appears as brown pin-head like spots on the leaves and stems. Spots on the stem spread, coalesce providing a glazed black colour to the stem which gives a charred appearance externally. The leaf spots also enlarge and coalesce and the leaves dry and wither. When young leaves are infected the whole leaf is blighted and killed. During continuous rains, light brown to dark brown macroscopic acervuli in concentric rings can be observed on infected tissues of both leaves and stems. At the base of the lamina and petioles, brown to black spots appear which become necrotic quickly killing the tissues and causing shedding of leaves, which is the main characteristic of the disease. In advanced stages, leaves and stems completely dry up resulting in total failure of tuber formation. Symptoms slightly vary between the species. All the species are susceptible to this disease but D. alata is more susceptible than the other two species.

  • Pathogen

The pathogen causing anthracnose in Dioscorea has been identified as Colletotrichum gloeosporioides Penz. f.sp. alatae.

  • Control .

  1. Field sanitation: Since crop debris are the main source of inoculun of debris and field sanitation helps to reduce the intensity and spred of the disease.

  2. Use of resistant/tolerant varieties: The field performance of the yam varieties at CTCRI have indicated the availability of several lines which are comparatively less susceptible to disease (Anon., 1987, 1989).

  3. Fungicidal control: Zineb and Febram sprayed at 10 days interval controlled the disease effectively. And the efficacy organic fungicide has been found to be more than the inorganic fungicides.

Cercospora Leaf spot

Leaf spots caused by Cercospora spp. affect the Dioscorea spp. In all the places where crop is grown but severe only in warm humid regions with good rainfall. The disease was first reported from Mysore. All the three edible spp. of Dioscorea are susceptible but the disease is severe and damaging only in D. alata.

  • Pathogen

At least four species of Cercospora have been recorded as causal agents of the disease. They are Cercospora carbonaceae Miles, C. dioscoreae Ellis and Martin, Pseudocercospora contraria (H. & P. Syd.) and C. golaghatii Saikia and Sarbhoy.

  • Symptoms

Though the general symptoms are same for all the spp., i.e., brownish spots which darken with age and coalesce as the disease progresses, slight variation in symptoms caused by the different species have been noticed.

The spots produced by C. carbonaceae (most commonly recorded sp.) are amphigenous, more conspicuous on the upper surface as a burn or scald, mostly angular, limited by veins or vein lets, slightly raised, 1-1.5 cm in diameter, brown below then dark in colour. Severely affected leaves dry and fall prematurely.

The spots produced by C. dioscoreae are dirty brown 0.25-1 cm broad, formed on the lower surface producing yellowish brown discolouration on the upper surface.

Pseudocercospora contraria produces leaf spots which are amphigenous, sub- orbicular, light brown with hypophyllous fruit bodies, while C. golaghati produce brown to reddish brown discolouration on the upper surface of the leaves.

There are at least two more Cercospora spp., viz., C. pachyderma H. and P Sydow and C. cylindrata Chupp and Linder have been recorded on Dioscorea elsewhere but not so far known from India.

  • Control

The disease can be effectively controlled by spraying fungicides, such as bavistin (0.05%), nicozin copper (0.25%), difolatan (0.05%), dithane M45 (0.25%) at 15 days interval with the commencement of the disease. 2-3 sprays would be sufficient (Thankappan, 1990). Since the damage caused by the disease is not usually significant, fungicides need be used only during severe outbreak of the disease.

Screening of Dioscorea germplasm indicated the availability of many lines which are tolerant or resistant to Cercospora leaf spot disease. Wherever the disease recurs in severe form, either tolerant lines or tolerant species of Dioscorea such as D. rotundata or D. esculenta can be used with advantage.

Curvularia Leaf Spot

A severe leaf spot was found to occur on D. alata at Assam during 1976. However, the disease is not wide spread and not reported from other parts of the country.

  • Symptoms

The disease appears as minute globose, tan coloured spots on the leaves, which gradually increase in size up to 20 mm in diameter, remaining almost circular or slightly elongated with a distinct yellow halo. When the spots develop on the main veins the discolouration spreads along the veins beyond the spots. The spots ultimately dries and the dead tissue falls off leaving shot holes. In extreme cases, the whole leaf may dry up. Infection may also develop. on the stems.

  • Pathogen

Curvularia eragostidis (P. Henri) Meyer has been identified as the causal organisms of this disease.

The conidiophores erect, unbranched, develop from free mycelium or stromatic masses, 5-8 septate, 168-252 x 5-8.3 micro in size: Conidia brown, usually 3 septate, occasionally 4 septate, end cells are lighter and smaller than the mid cells usually straight 16.6-26.3 x 8.3-14.9 micro in size.

In cultures, the conidiophores solitary or arise in groups of 2 or 3, 5-8 septate, 120-320 x 5.2-9.0 micro in size. Conidia triseptate, elipsoidal, the terminal cells smaller and subhyaline and measure 16.6-34.9 x 6.6-14.1 micro.

  • Control

Control is not usually required and no information on control is presently available.


Minor Diseases

Besides, the diseases described above, at least three more diseases, which are of minor economic importance or limited. distribution have also been recorded on Dioscorea spp. from India. They are leaf spots caused by Alternaria alternata(Fr.) Keissler, Dreschslera sorokiniana (Sacc.) Subram. and Jain and leaf blight caused by Rhizoctonia sp.

However, detailed studies on the damage caused by these disease, severity, mode of spread and control measures have not been investigated and presently no separate control measures are required for these diseases.

Storage Diseases of Yams

Yam tubers suffer from severe rots in storage caused by a number of micro- organisms resulting in significant losses. However, in India, only two pathogen have so far been reported. They are the dry rots caused by Penicillium spp. and Fusarium spp.

    Dry Rot (Penicillium Rot)

This disease was first reported from the sample collected from Uttar Pradesh (Agra) in 1973. About 10-15 per cent of the tubers are found to be attacked by this disease. Mostly wounded or injured tubers are susceptible to this disease.

  • Symptoms: The diseased tubers show complete drying of tissues and produce a hard brown mass presenting the characteristic symptom of dry rot. The initial symptom is a slight shrinkage and darkening of affected parts. With the progress of rot, most of the interior part was affected and the skin wrinkles. The underlying tissues assume dark brown to black discolouration which become dry and cavities develop which are lined with the fungal mycelium. The worst affected tubers are usually hard and much lighter in weight in comparison with healthy ones.

A temperature of above 25°C and relative. humidity of 80% or above and initial injuries are most favourable for the development of the disease. Tubers exposed to 15°C do not get infected by the pathogen.

  • Pathogen: Pathogen causing the dry rot of yam tubers has been identified as Penicillium sclerotigenum Yamamoto.


Several other fungi such as Alternaria alternata (Fr.) Keissler, A. solani (Ell. and Mart) Sorauer, Fusarium oxysporum, Schlecht. F. chlamydosporum Wollenw. Helminthosporium sp. Penicillium sp. Verticillium sp., Cephalosporium indicum Patch. Curvularia pallescens Boedijn, C. lunata (Wakker) Boedijn. Aspergillus flavous Link. Rhizopus stolonifer(Fr.) Linder, have been found to affect the stored tubers of Dioscorea prazeri Prain and Rurkil grown for the medicinal purpose, causing soft or dry rots. It is possible that these fungi can also cause similar rot to the tubers of edible species of Dioscorea.

  • Control: Since most of the rot causing organisms are wound parasites tubers used for planting should be carefully selected avoiding injured tubers for storage. When cut tubers are used, the cut end should be protected with fungicides~

Post-harvest dip treatment of tubers with Bavistin, Benomyl, Karathane and Griseofulvin can help in reducing the rot incidence and to prolong the shelf life of the tubers.

Dipping of tubers in 300 ppm Benlate solution for 5 minutes and dusting of cut ends with 0.36 Benomyl in talcum powder gave significant control of rots caused by Botryodiplodia theobromae, Rhizopus nodosus, Cladosporiumsp. Penicilliumsp., Rhizopus sp. on the tubers of Dioscorea floribunda another medicinal yam.

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used and Uses (1, 2,3,10)

Tubers:Similar to the other important tuber crops, Dioscorea alata could also be utilized for extraction of starch. Starch is used in manufacture of biscuits. The tubers are also used in the preparetion of 'tuber mash', 'bitsoy' sweet stuffed porothas, Wafers and crispies.


Harvesting Practices  

The crop is harvested when the season is over, i.e., harvesting may be done at any time after large scale leaf yellowing sets in. Since little or no more tuber material is added during the last month before vine death, the time of harvest is not critical and hence it can be harvested at any time for one month before senescence till 1-2 months after senescence. It is reported to be between 7 and 8 months after planting for Dioscorea alata. (1, 2)


Storage:

A major problem in storing the seed tubers is control of sprouting. The terminal buds of the tuber grow into lanky shoots of varying lengths by mobilizing the food material from the tuber leading to considerable loss in dry matter. Soaking the tubers in 1000 ppm maleic hydrazide solution for 10 h has been found effective in completely inhibiting the storage sprouting for a period up to three weeks. Further studies on the effect of post-harvest application of maleic hydrazide (MH) on growth and development of tuber have suggested that the seedling establishment, tuberization and tuber yield were not affected due to MH treatment.


Produce and Products (1, 2,3,10,15,17,18,19)

The nutritive value and utilization of D.alata for recipes, processed foods and feeds. It was found that novel recipes like 'tuber mash', 'bitsoy' and sweet stuffed porothas could be prepared from fresh tubers. Wafers, crispies and biscuits made out of D.alata tubers were found to have good acceptability and open up a new avenue in the industrial utilization of D.alata.

Based on the desirable properties of Dioscorea rotundata, starch like low viscosity breakdown, good gel strength, bland taste, etc., the efficacy of yam starch in biscuit making was evaluated. It was found that starch could be used at 30-50 per cent levels in biscuit doughs without affecting the quality. The biscuits were crisp and had no objectionable taste or flavour.

Traditionally, in many Indian families, D.alata tubers are peeled and cooked or used as a vegetable.

Processing Techniques ( 2,10,20,48,49)

Cleaning and Washing of the tuber before extraction:

Loose dirt, sand and gravel are removed on a rotating screen before the potatoes are deposited in the store - the better the dirt removal, the lesser the problems with stones and sand in the fluming channels later. The soil also contains considerable quantities of nutrients, which will dissolve in the washing water and contribute to the environmental effect caused by the effluent.

The potatoes are flumed by water in channels - passing a stone trap - to the washing station. The stone trap utilises the difference in specific weights between stones and potatoes - an upstream water flow carries the potatoes over the stone trap, while the heavier stones are trapped and collected on a stone conveyor.

Steps in starch extraction:

RASPING.

Rasping is the first step in the starch extraction. The goal is to open the tuber cells and release the starch granules. The slurry obtained can be considered as a mixture of pulp (cell walls), fruit juice and starch. With modern high-speed raspers, rasping is a one-pass operation only.

USE OF SULPHUR.

The cell juice is rich in sugar and protein. When opening the cells the juice is instantly exposed to air and reacts with the oxygen, forming coloured components, which may adhere to the starch.

Sulphur dioxide gas or sodium-bisulphite-solution therefore has to be added. A considerable reduction potential of the sulphur compounds prevents discoloration. Sufficient sulphur has to be added to maintain the juice and pulp light yellow.

EXTRACTION. 

Powerful washing is needed to flush the starch granules out from the cells - the cells are torn apart in the rasper and form a filtering mat that tries to retain the starch. Water has previously been used for the extraction, but today extraction takes place in closed systems allowing the use of the potato juice itself. It has the advantage that the juice can later be recovered in concentrated and undiluted form, reducing transport costs for its use as a fertiliser.

The flushed-out starch discharges from the extraction sieves along with the fruit juice, and the cell walls (pulp) are pumped to the pulp dewatering sieves. The pulp leaves the dewatering sieves as drip- dry - i.e. approximately 13% dry matter. 

The extraction takes place on rotating conical sieves, where centrifugal power increases the capacity per unit of area. The high efficiency makes it feasible to utilise high quality sieve plates made of stainless steel, which will withstand abrasion and CIP-chemicals. The sieve plates have long perforations only 125 microns across. 

Operating Principle of a Starch Extractor.
The extraction is a counter current process in which the pulp-dewatering screen is actually the last step. If the pulp is required in almost dry form, the number of spray nozzles with washing water is reduced. Instead continuous back spraying is maintained to ensure that the dry pulp will slide down the screen.

CONCENTRATING THE CRUDE STARCH SLURRY.

On hydrocyclone unit as much juice is excreted as possible. The starch leaves the concentrator as pumpable slurry.
The concentrating stage typically consists of a unit with hydrocyclone blocks for defoaming, concentrating and starch recovering arranged in series.

REFINING

It now remains to purify the crude starch milk (suspension) and remove residual fruit juice and impurities. The way it is done is more or less based on the same principles used when removing soap water from the laundry - by wringing and soak in clean water again and again. The harder you wring the fewer rinsing steps are required.

In the same way, the starch slurry is diluted and concentrated again and again. To save rinsing water the wash is done counter currently - i.e. the incoming fresh water is used on the very last step and the overflow is recycled for dilution on the previous step and so on.

HYDROCYCLONES. 

Refining is based on the difference in specific density of water, fibers and starch:

In the strong gravitational fields of a hydrocyclone and a centrifuge, starch settle quickly, while fibers (pulp residuals) just float in the water. The juice is directly diluted in the water and goes with the water phase.
By creating a water flow moving towards the starch, lots of fibers just floating in the water may be forced into the overflow. Soil, sand and many fungi etc. are of equal density or heavier than starch and it is not possible to separate these particles from starch by centrifugal force - that is why it is so important to remove as many impurities as possible from the potato surface in the washing station. 
Although some impurities go with the starch in the underflow, there is - by means of a sieve - a last chance to remove the larger particles - that is particles larger than 125 microns. The particles are not spherical. On the contrary, they are of irregular shape and may be forced through refining sieve, if the spray pressure is too high. 
Impurities not removed this way are not removable by any known technique.
In the recovery steps all starch has to be retained in the underflow so only very little is wasted in the effluent (fruit water). 

COOLING

The lower the water consumption, the more pumps are involved in the process and the more heat is generated. To retard bacterial growth refrigerator temperatures are ideal.
In the effluent of concentrated fruit juice, cooling during extraction is a must because in hot juice microbes that break down protein and a bad smell may take control.

CIP - CLEANING IN PLACE.

Cleaning in Place is done with caustic and hypochlorite as cleaning agents. Caustic is a powerful agent for removal of the protein build-up on the interior walls and the hypochlorite is an efficient germ killer.
During CIP it is of the utmost importance to keep the pipes filled up. Tanks are most efficiently fitted with rotating disc nozzles - and covered tanks are required.

DRYING AND SIFTING.

The moist starch from the rotating vacuum filters is dried in a flash dryer with moderate hot air. The air is indirectly heated.
Before delivery the starch is sifted on a fine sieve in order to remove any scale formed in screw conveyors etc.

By applying different reaction conditions - temperature, pH, additives - and strict process control speciality products with unique properties are made.

These speciality products are named modified starches, because they still retain their original granule form and thereby resemble the native (unmodified) starch in appearance. But the modification has introduced improved qualities in the starch when cooked. The paste may have obtained improved clarity, viscosity, film-forming ability etc.

Being a pure renewable natural polymer starch has a multitude of applications.

Cottage-scale Starch Extraction

Roots, peeled and washed, are manually rasped to a pulp on a stationary grater and the pulp is vigorously washed with water by hand over a piece of fabric fastened on four poles. Starch milk, collected underneath in a bucket, is allowed to settle and 'the starch cake is obtained after decanting. The starch cake is then crumbled and sun- dried. The fibrous waste or 'thippi' retained on the fabric is also sun-dried and is used as animal feed.

Other Uses  

Contraception with yams : it is based on an intricate hormonal feedback system called the menstrual cycle. The discovery of a natural steroidal precursor in tropical yams has greatly reduced the cost of synthetic steroids used in birth control pills and corticosteroids used to treat Addison's disease, asthma, arthritis, bursitis, tendinitis, dermatitis, hemorrhoids, and many other ailments. It is possible to synthesize these hormones from bile acids of cattle or extract them from excised adrenal cortexes, but such methods are time- consuming and very expensive. The most cumbersome part of the chemical synthesis of these hormones is the complex steroidal backbone. At one time it took 40 oxen to provide enough cortisone to treat one patient for one day, and in 1938 one gram of cortisone cost $100. Today the naturally- occurring diosgenin in Mexican yams can readily be converted into these valuable steroids by simply adding the appropriate chemical group to the already-present steroidal backbone. In fact, more than 60,000 tons of fresh yam tubers are imported into the United States each year for the production of birth control pills. You can also purchase extracts of yams in tablet form that supposedly prevent ovulation and provide a "natural" method of birth control; however, it would be advisable to thoroughly research these claims before trying the latter method on a mate.
