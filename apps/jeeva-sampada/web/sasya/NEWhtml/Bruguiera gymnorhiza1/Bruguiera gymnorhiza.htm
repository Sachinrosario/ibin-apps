Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:

A glabrous evergreen shrub or tree. Frequent on elevated interior parts of mangrove forests, often in association with species of Rhizophora and Ceriops, in the intertidal regions. In India this species occurs in tidal swamps along both the coasts of the Peninsula and in the Andaman & Nicobar Islands. The species is distributed in the tropics of Africa to Pacific isles through Egypt, Indus, India, Sri Lanka, Tropical Asia and Australia. Common along the sea-coast and in salt-marshes.

Mangroves are found only in tropics and subtropics. They are very sensitive to frost.

Trees are suited to coastal and estuarine zones.

Mangroves are best in moist tropical areas where annual rainfall is over 1,000 mm.

Mangroves can survive in highly saline, poorly drained soils that are irregularly inundated by the tide. They grow best, however, in areas that are regularly flushed by the tide or in areas with freshwater seepage. Regular flushing with seawater or freshwater appears to be necessary to avois excessively high salinities, which arise through evaporation and would otherwise restrict growth. Consequently, attempts to impound mangrove areas within levees often reduce productivity and sometimes lead to death of trees. Soil in mangrove swamps is usually deep, black mud that is almost continuously water logged, but mangroves also will colonize sand and carbonate soils.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

The tree is a valuable tanniferous mangrove. A glabrous evergreen, buttressed shrub or tree up to 30 m tall, bark grey to almost black, roughly fissured and stem-bases, with many knee-like bent roots; young branches smooth, marked towards the apex with the scars of fallen leaves and stipules. Leaves elliptic or elliptic-oblong, acute or slightly acuminate (not mucronate), bright green above, paler and with a prominent midrib beneath, glabrous, cuneate at the base; stipules caducous.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Flowers solitary, axillary; peduncles deflexed. Calyx orange-yellow, campanulate, ribbed, divided about ½ way down in 10-14 (often 11) narrow acute erect segments keeled on the inner face. Petals shorter than the calyx-lobes and equal to them in number, 2-lobed at the apex, with 2-4 stout bristles at the point of each lobe and 1 in the notch, hairy at the base, glabrous above. Stamens 2 opposite to each petal and shorter than it; filaments of each pair alternately long and short. Fruit less than 1 in. long, obconic, crowned at first by the erect calyx-segments which afterwards drop off, scarlet; protruding radicle fusiform, more or less angled, often reaching 1 ft. in length before falling.


Breeding system and varietal improvement:

Phenology: Flowers Dec-Feb. Flowering and fruiting throughout the year.


Pollinators: Pollinated by bird.

Floral and seed biology:

It can be grown by collecting the ripe or germinating seeds at high tide and dropping them in the mud in suitable locations at low tide. Such planting gives better and more even stands than those resulting from natural regeneration. Plants grow from the tips of branches. Air layering has become important in mangrove propagation.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts Used:

Fuel: Wood used as firewood by coastal people of tropical and sub-tropical countries. Calorific value: 4,700 cal. and of moisture free sapwood - 5169 cal, of heartwood - 5079 cals). Wood used for charcoal making. Charcoal burns steadily, giving off intense heat without sparking. Charcoal is made from wood.

Wood: The wood is dull red or reddish brown, heavy to very heavy (Weight 865-977 kg/m3), straight grained and even- and fine- textured. It is durable, strong, tough and extremely hard, and is difficult to saw and work. It is easy to treat and the absorption of preservatives is excellent.

The wood is used in house construction, for posts, beams, door-frames and planks. It is used for rough furniture and tool handles. Hardwood stems used in building fishing boats and houses, electric poles.

Heartwood is prized for furniture.

Wood used as timber.
Chips are used for making pulp and rayon.

Other uses:

Radicles may be eaten.

Fruit - The fruit is sometimes used as an astringent in betel quid and is also made into a sweetmeat. The pith and seeds are also edible.

Stems and branches are used for thatching mudwalls as they are immune to white ants. The stem is also used for making combs. Netting thread is boiled with the stems and leaves to make it strong. Leaves are used as fodder.

Bark - The bark is used as astringent in diarrhoea, sometimes inmalaria, and also to flavour a preparation of fish. It yields a black dye, which, with treatment, turns orange-red, brown and voilet.Bark - Bark is source tannin. Black dye is obtained from this.

Coastal protection – Mangroves can be instrumental in developing coastal and estuarine areas. They provide natural protection against a turbulent sea, reducing typhoon and storm damage, and binding and building sand and soil.

Food and wildlife Production – Mangrove swamps are a "cradle of life". They are spawning and nursery grounds for many species of fish, as well as shrimp, crab, clams, oysters and crocodiles. They are feeding and nesting grounds for many sea birds and home to other wildlife. Mangrove areas are potential resources for aquaculture.

Extractives – Tannins used to produce hard leather (such as for shoe soles) and resins used for bonding plywood are extracted from various species.
