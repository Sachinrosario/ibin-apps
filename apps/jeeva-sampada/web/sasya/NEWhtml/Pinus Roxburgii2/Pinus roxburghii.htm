Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
A tall tree, with a spreading crown, found in the Himalayas from Kashmir to Bhutan and in the Siwalik hills at altitudes of 450-2400 m; It comes up tolerably well in the plains  also and is sometimes planted in gardens for ornamental purposes.

The plant prefers light (sandy) and medium (loamy) soils, requires well-drained soil and can grow in nutritionally poor soil. The plant prefers acid, neutral and basic (alkaline) soils and can grow in very alkaline soil.  It cannot grow in the shade.  It requires dry or moist soil and can tolerate drought.

Chirpine grows well in a rainfall of 100-300cms. It can tolerate snowfall and lower temperatures which may fall to the freezing point or even below it.



Availability :  
Chir pine is widely tapped for resin on a commercial basis, particularly in the Jammy hills, Himachal pradesh, Garhwal and Kumaun.

Status : 
Planted in garden for ornamental purposes.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Chir tree usually grows up to 30 m in height and 2.5 m in girth, with a cylindrical bole of about 12 m. Branches more or less whorled;  Bark dark grey , often reddish, deeply fissured, rough, exfoliating in longitudinally elongated plates;  The sapwood is white to creamy white and heartwood light red, turning brown on exposure. Annual rings and dark streaks denoting resin canals are very conspicuous. The fresh wood has a resinous odour. Leaves in clusters of three, 20-30 cm. long,  triquetrous, finely toothed, light green, persisting on an average for a year and a half; male flowers about 1.5 cm long, arranged in the form of cones; solitary or 2-5 together, ovoid, 10-20  cm x 13.0 cm when ripe, brown, woody; seeds winged; without wing 7.5-13.0 mm x 5.0-6.5 mm; wings long membranous polyembryonate; pollen grains are shed at 2-celled stage.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Breeding System :
The flowers are monoecious (individual flowers are either male or female, but both sexes can be found on the same plant) and are pollinated by Wind.  The plant is not self-fertile.

Plants are strongly outbreeding, self-fertilized seed usually grows poorly. They hybridize freely with other members of this genus.

Phenology :
Sprouting of needles from buds takes place in January, winter buds form in October-November and growth continues only upto the end of December or early January.

Male flowers ripen and shed pollen grains between February to April depending upon altitude.  Female flowers begin appear from February onwards.

Chirpine produces seeds at the age of 30 years.

Pollinators:
Pollinated by wind

Seed Biology :
Seeds - 8-16 mm long with about 2.5cm long membranous wings.

Seed coat extends into a membranous wing, which can not be detached without injury.

Seedling Germination :

Seeds get dispersed during April-July. Under natural conditions, seeds germinate as soon as sufficient moisture is available. Germination commences at the beginning of the monsoon and germination is epigeous.

It is best to sow the seed in individual pots in a cold frame as soon as it is ripe if this is possible, otherwise in late winter. A short stratification of 6 weeks at 4°c can improve the germination of stored seed.  Plant seedlings out into their permanent positions as soon as possible and protect them for their first winter or two. Plants have a very sparse root system and the sooner they are planted into their permanent positions the better they will grow. Trees should be planted into their permanent positions when they are quite small, between 30 and 90cm. We actually plant them out when they are about 5 - 10cm tall. So long as they are given a very good weed-excluding mulch they establish very well. Larger trees will check badly and hardly put on any growth for several years. This also badly affects root development and wind resistance.

Cuttings. This method only works when taken from very young trees less than 10 years old. Use single leaf fascicles with the base of the short shoot.  Disbudding the shoots some weeks before taking the cuttings can help. Cuttings are normally slow to grow away.

Cultivation:
Thrives in a light well-drained sandy or gravelly loam. Succeeds on calcareous soils. Dislikes poorly drained moorland soils. Dislikes shade. Established plants tolerate drought.

The chir pine is not very hardy in Britain, succeeding outdoors only in the mildest areas of the country. In the driest parts of its native range, the leaves are shed after 10 - 11 months, making it deciduous.

Trees are extensively tapped for their resin in India and are the main source of resin in the region.

Plants are strongly out breeding, self-fertilized seed usually grows poorly. They hybridize freely with other members of this genus.

Leaf secretions inhibit the germination of seeds, thereby reducing the amount of plants that can grow under the trees.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests: 

A number of insect pests have been recorded on chir:  Chlorophorus strobilicola  Champion destroys the cones. Larvae of species of Anomala, granida, Hilytrogus and Popillia damage  seedlings in the nurseries. Beetles and larvae of the species of Cyrtepistomus , Athalia  and Pygaera defoliate and the beetles belonging to the genara Aetheomorpha, Aphthona  and Colasposoma  gnaw the needles. Beetles and larvae of several insects of the family Scolytidae bore into the bark and young branches . Dead and fallen wood is attacked by a large number of borer. Particularly the species of the genara Anthaxia , Capnodis and Criocephanus

The problem of mortality of Chir pine in the states of Haryana and Himachal Pradesh was found due to the heavy attack by four types of insect stem borers viz., Sphaenoptera aterrima, Cryptorhynchus rufescens, Platypus biformis and Polygraphus longifolia.

Diseases: 

The chir pine is affected by a number of rusts and wood-rot diseases.

Cronartium Himalayense Bagchee with its aecial stage as Peridermim himalayense attacks stems, causing blisters on the bark; the effected tree may die.

Peridermium orientale Cooke attacks needles, but the damage is not very serious.

Hysterium pinastri Schr causes diseases of nursery seedlings; the needles are  attacked and badly affected seedlings die.

Needles rust caused by Coleosporium campanulae Lev is also frequent , but the damage is not serious. Among the wood-rotting fungi Fomes annosus Cooke, F .pini Lloyd syn. Trametes pini Fr. and Poria monticola Murr.attack the living trees; Fomes pinicola Cooke, Polystictus abietinus Fr. and Lenzites spp. have also been recorded on the wood.Plants in this genus are notably susceptible to honey fungus.

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Bark, wood, oleoresin, oil

Uses

A resin is obtained from the sapwood. Trees are tapped for three years and then rested for three years. The yield is up to 5.5 kilos per tree. Oleo-resins are present in the tissues of all species of pines, but these are often not present in sufficient quantity to make their extraction economically worthwhile. The resins are obtained by tapping the trunk, or by destructive distillation of the wood. In general, trees from warmer areas of distribution give the higher yields. Turpentine consists of an average of 20% of the oleo-resin and is separated by distillation . 

  • Oleoresin from the Chir is the   main source of turpentine oil in the country. Used as a solvent, especially for thinning paints and varnishes. Used in pharmaceutical preparations, perfumary industry and in the manufacture of the synthetic pine oil, disinfectants, insecticides and denaturants.
  • Oleoresin is acrid, bitter, thermogenic, expectorant, anodyne, anti-inflammatory, purgative and dermulcent. It is useful in vitiated conditions of vata , asthma, chronic bronchitis, otalgia, odontalgia, inflamations, hepatopathy, splenopathy, urethrorrhea, gonorrhoea, scabies and epilepsy.
  • Bark contains 11-14 per cent tannin.  
  • A yellow dye present in the bark colours leather catechuic brown.
  • A tan or green dye is obtained from the needles
  • A sweet edible manna exudes from the bark and twigs. It is actually a gum.

 


Harvesting Practices

Methods of tapping.

1)      Light-tapping which consists of making a moderate number of blazes in the tree not to be felled in the near future;

2)      Heavy- tapping , also termed “tapping to death”. In heavy tapping as many blazes as possible are made on the tree which will be felled within five years. Normally tapping  commences in March and continues till November  after which  the flow of resin ceases.

In India, Pinus roxburghii trees yield the highest amounts of oleoresin.  Blazes on the tree trunk are cut to facilitate the flow of oleoresin from resin canals. Traditionally, oleoresin is collected by the cup-and-lip method from March to early November. The rill method for tapping, though more scientific, has not found favor in the field. Treatment of blazes with acid or 2-4D solution reportedly increases and prolongs the flow of resin. The yield is highest in June, when the sun is hottest. The oleoresin collected in cups is transferred to tins every time the cups are filled. The tins are transported to depots, then to factories for processing.

Pine resin is an oleo-resin secreted from resin canals of two kinds;

1. Large longitudinal ducts in the wood.

2. Smaller ducts in the rays at right angles to the large ducts.

Other facts:

Resin is obtained by tapping the trees.  A small cut or incision is made so as to expose the wood.  The resin in the longitudinal ducts reaches the surface through the smaller ones in the rays.  Thus only a small cut or incision is enough to make the resin flow.

As a matter of fact, incision of wounding of the pine tree helps to stimulate secretion.  Wood is formed to close the wound and this usually contains more than the normal number of resin ducts in it.  The resin serves to prevent fungal infection and ingress of boring insects through the wound or incision.

Maximum flow of resin occurs from the top of incision where both the vertical and horizontal ducts are cut.  Soon after the resin comes in contact with air, the volatile oil in it evaporates and leaves a clear hard glassy substance, which forms a protective cover over the wound.  It tends to stop the further flow of resin.

Broad principles of resin tapping:

1. The resin exudes from the served resin canals where an incision is made.

2. More resin oozes out from the sapwood than from the heartwood where the resin ducts often tend to be blocked.

3. The size, frequency of occurrence and pattern of distribution of resins canals depend upon; age of the tree, heredity characters, environmental conditions.

4. Resin canals are large and irregularly distributed in chir pine trees. Many such canals or ducts are severed when a thin layer of bark and underlying sapwood is removed.  Resin begins to ooze out of this wound.  This is the main principle of resin tapping used in obtaining resin from chir pine trees.

Chemistry
Turpentine is a colourless, viscous liquid having a strong smell and a hot, unpleasant taste.  The specific gravity of turpentine oil is between 0.85 to 0.88.  It begins to boil at about 155 degree C.  Turpentine oil is virtually insoluble in water, miscible with absolute alcohol and ether and dissolves in sulphur, phosphorus, resins and caoutechouc.  Turpentine oil dries to a solid resin on exposure to air, absorbing oxygen and giving off ozones.  Chemically turpentine oil is a mixture of terpenes. 

Rosin is colourless, yellow, red, brown, blue or black.  Its specfic gravity is 1.08 and melting point is from 100 to 140degre C.

Processing Techniques
Crude resin will be in a very impure state when it reaches the factory.  It contains water, bark, needles, dirt and even insects.  They stored in a suitable shed after being brought to the factory in trucks.  Before processing the tape of the tin is cut and the dirt that is floating on top is removed manually.  Thereafter the resin is emptied into big melters or vats.  These are large vessels made of mild steel.  A little impure turpentine is added to the crude resin in the melter after the lid is covered and water sealed.  The steam jacked is used to heat the resin in the melter.  A helical mixer is provided in the melter.  The resin is mixed with it and allowed to settle down over-night.  The heavy impurities float at the top.  The latter are removed by hand.  The diluted resin is allowed to flow into a sludge tank.  This leaves the slude containing the heavy impurities in the melter.  A part of the diluted resin is drawn from the charge tank into a compressor from where it is pumped into a still provided with a steam jacket for distillation.  A large number of copper pipes  are connected with the steam jacket at both ends.  A swan necked pipe connects the condenser through a catch still with the dome at the top of steam jacket.  The mass of resin in the charge is heated by the steam still and the process of distillation begins.  The higher oils are recovered first followed by the heavier ones.  Once all the turpentine has been recovered only rosin is left in the still.  It is drawn off and packed in wood casks.  Turpentine obtained from the first distillation is distilled once again.  It leaves behind the turpentine oil.

There are several  methods of turpentine oil tapping

      On distillation of “turpentine oil” an aromatic solid residue is obtained, this is called “Rosin”.  It is in the form of transparent of slightly translucent lump and is usually classified into different types based on its colour.

Other Uses

 

  1. A charcoal made from the leaves, mixed with rice water, is used as an ink.
  2. A vanillin flavouring is obtained as a by-product of other resins that are released from the pulpwood.
  3.   Pine needle oil - used as an adjunct in the scenting of soaps, in bath preparation, room sprays, deodorants and similar products.
  4. Pine oil - used in paints, varnishes, lacquers, distempers, soaps and detergents and in perfumery  and pharmaceuticals. It is employed in textiles, as a degreasing agent in leather industry and as a synthergist for insecticides, and as a frothing agent in mining industry. It is also used in the paper and rubber industries, and in the preparation of floor waxes, furniture polishes, shoe polishes, metal polishes and printing inks.
  5.   Pine wool - is considered vermin-proof and is used for stuffing pillows, cushions and mattresses. Packaging material for fruits. Wood is acrid, bitter, sweet, Thermogenic, emollient, aromatic, antiseptic, deodorant, haemostatic, stimulant, anathematic, digestive, liver tonic, diaphoretic, rubefacient and diuretic. Useful in ophthalmopathy, otopathy, pharyngophagy, vitiated conditions of vata  halitosis, foul ulcers, hemorrhages, haemoptysis, helminthiasis, dyspepsia, flatulence, hepatopathy, strangury, bronchitis, inflammations, skin diseases, pruritus and giddiness.
  6. Rosin -  It is used in the manufacture of soaps and for sizing paper, paints, varnish, rubber and polish industries. Rosin gives a stiff colour coated surface to certain kinds of paper.  This needed for printing and writing.  It is also used in manufacture of linoleum sealing wax, oil, cloth, special flooring and roofing compounds, coverings, inks and disinfectants.  It is widely used for soldering, in the manufacture of paints and varnishes and as a dressing for machine belts and bows of violins and cellos. It is utilized in the manufacture of fireworks, match compositions, shell explosives, insecticides and disinfectants and enters into certain lubricating compositions and hair fixing and nail-polishing preparations. 
  7. Rosin is used by violinists on their bows and also in making sealing wax, varnish etc. Pitch can also be obtained from the resin and is used for waterproofing, as a wood preservative etc.
  8.   Rosin oil is used in the manufacture of printer’s ink, varnishes and antiseptics, adulterant for oil.
  9. Seed - raw or cooked. Not very nice, it has a strong flavour of turpentine and is only eaten as an emergency food. 
  10. The needles contain a substance called terpene, this is released when rain washes over the needles and it has a negative effect on the germination of some plants, including wheat.
  11. The turpentine obtained from the resin of all pine trees is antiseptic, diuretic, rubefacient and vermifuge. It is a valuable remedy used internally in the treatment of kidney and bladder complaints and is used both internally and as a rub and steam bath in the treatment of rheumatic affections. It is also very beneficial to the respiratory system and so is useful in treating diseases of the mucous membranes and respiratory complaints such as coughs, colds, influenza and TB. Externally it is a very beneficial treatment for a variety of skin complaints, wounds, sores, burns, boils etc and is used in the form of liniment plasters, poultices, herbal steam baths and inhalers.
  12. The turpentine oil is one of the most important basic raw materials for the synthesis of turpene chemicals which are used in a wide variety of industries such as adhesives, lubricants, additives, synthetic resins, solvents, plasticizers, paints, perfumery, cosmetics, paper and rubber industry. It is used for dissolving fats, resins, caoutchouc and for a wide variety of domestic purposes.  Turpentine oil based products are used in t he manufacture of synthetic rubber, waxes, synthetic camphor, insecticides and germicide.
  13. The wood is very resinous and can be splintered and used as a torch.
  14. Timber - constructional purposes, such as rafters, posts, boarding door and window frames, house furniture, beams, packaging cases, bus bodies, electric transmission poles, railway carriages and wagons. Raw material for paper pulp. White and printing paper, wrapper paper. It is useful in cold climates but is not resistant to white ants.  The wood is diaphoretic and stimulant. It is useful in treating burning of the body, cough, fainting and ulcers.
  15. Turpentine has a wide range of uses including as a solvent for waxes etc, for making varnish, medicinal etc. 
  16. Turpentine oil is aromatic, anodyne, antispasmodic, antiseptic, expectorant, rubefacient, carminative, stomachic. It is useful in chronic bronchitis, gangrene of the lungs, vitiated conditions of vata, neuralgia, inflammations, obstinate constipation, dyspepsia, flatulence and colic
