Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Basil is obtained from a small, erect, glabrous, profusely branched annual herb, indigenous to India. It forms part of the 'sweet' herbs, along with marjoram and tarragon. Basil is a member of the mint family. Among spices, it offers one of the highest yields per acre (2). 

Climate and soil
Adapted to wide range of conditions favourable for vegetative propagation. Grows in altitude range of 450-1000 m and a temperature range of 5-30˚C (20˚C is optimum). Susceptible to water stress, water logging and frost. Thrives best on fertile, light, well-drained soils, in warm, temperate climates and has relatively high nitrogen and water requirements. A pH tolerance of 5.5-6.5 is optimal (1).

Availability :

Sweet basil or French basil is believed to have originated in central tropical Asia - perhaps in the Indo-Afghan region. But now this is of world-wide distribution and is cultivated in many countries. In India it is distributed throughout and cultivated (3). The prehistoric religious significance of basil has helped in its transactional spread.

Conservation/ Threat Status : Wild and cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES: Basil is an erect, profusely branched annual or short-lived perennial herb, up to 75 cm height and has strong aromatic smell. Stem is quadrangular, densely pilose in young parts and light green to dark purple in colour. Leaves are simple, decussately opposite, petiole 1-2 cm long, blade ovate to elliptical, pilose, light green to dark purplish green, acuminate, alternate at base, margin crenate-serrate, shortly ciliated leaves densely glandular punctate. Taproot is brown, thick up to 6 mm and many secondary roots (1). 

Back to Top

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR: Inflorescence terminal, up to about 30 cm long, composed of decussate, 3-flowered cymes, bracts oblanceolate to rhombic, 3-10 x 1-3 mm, pilose; pedicel 3-4 mm long, densely white, pilose; calyx bilobed, tubular, with a flat suborbicular upper lobe 3.5 mm in diameter and a canaliculated lower lobe 3.5 mm long; corolla tubular, 2-lipped, 5-8 mm long, pilose outside, whitish-purplish, white or creamy yellow, upper lip 4-lobed, lower lip entire, ovate; stamens 4, didynamous, exceeding corolla by 2-4 mm, 2 outer ones longest; pistil with a 4-lobed ovary, a gynobasic style up to 9 mm long at top splitting into 2-stigmatic lobes up to 1 mm long (1).

Fruit
Fruit composed of 4 distinct ellipsoid, black and pitted nutlets enclosed within the tube of the persistent calyx (3). 

Breeding system and varietal improvement: Mainly autogamous. The species is highly polymorphic and owing to abundant cross pollination there evolved many races, varieties and subspecies.

Phenology:
Flowering starts 3-4 months after planting and occurs year around in the tropics and in summer in temperate areas (1).

Pollinators:

Floral and seed biology: Nutlets ovoid, 1-2 mm x 1 mm, black to dark brown.


CULTIVATION ASPECT:


Seed germination:
Seeds germinate epigeally and have long viability.

Local cultivars :
For south east Asia, there are 3 other species of Ocimum (Flora Malesiana): 
O. americanum L. – Hoary basil, occurring wild and cultivated, primarily used as vegetable.
O. gratissimum L. – Shrubby basil, occurring wild and cultivated, best known from Africa, used as an essential oil plant. 
O. tenuiflorum L. – Holy basil, only cultivated, used as a minor spice herb. 

Widely grown cultivars and cultivar groups are: ‘Sweet’, ‘Genovese’, ’Large-leaf’ and ‘Mammoth’; examples of purple leaved cultivars are: ‘Dark Opal’ and ‘Purple Ruffles’. O. basilicum cultivars with a different aroma and taste are: ‘Lemon-scented’, ‘Sweet Dani’, ‘Cinnamon Basil’, ‘Spicy Bush’, ‘Camphor’, ‘Anise’ and ‘Licorice’ (1).

Types of basil:
European or sweet basil: Common in Western Europe, Eastern Europe, Egypt and USA. The oil contains approximately equal proportion of linalool and methyl chavicol.
Reunion type: From the Malagassy Republic, Comoro Island, Reunion Island, etc. It is rich in methyl chavicol and little linalool. More coarse herbaceous with a slightly camphoraceous top note. 
Methyl cinnamate type: Found in many tropical countries, including India. Rich in methyl cinnamate. 
Eugenol rich type: Common in USSR and North African countries. Rich in Eugenol (1).

Another species O. gratissimum is cultivated in USSR and in many other countries as a source of eugenol. A hybrid between O. gratissimum and O. basilicum (eugenol type) has been designated as clocimum and is being grown for commercial production of eugenol. 

Chromosome number:
2n=48, 50-74. Believed to be a natural tetraploid.

Selection of planting material:
Propagation is through seeds or cuttings. Seeds remain viable for 10 years if refrigerated. 

Planting:
Seeds are sown directly in the field @ approx. 5 kg/ha, later seedlings are thinned to get a spacing of 15-30 cm. Germination will be completed in about 2 weeks. When seedlings are 5-8 cm tall, they are transplanted into the field, planted in rows 30-45 cm apart and 20 cm between plants. Plant prefers sunny areas protected form wind. Seedlings are trimmed to encourage branching and growth. In the case of stem cuttings, stem section with 4-5 leaf pairs cut right below the fifth node from the shoot apex are suitable. Roots develop within 2-3 weeks. Flowering starts after 2-3 months of sowing though the time taken for flowering varies very much among cultivars. The stump sprouts and it is harvested further (1). 

Management after planting:
Weed control is important for good growth, which is done manually or using weedicide. Mulching is done using straw or black polythene sheet. Branches are trimmed regularly for better leaf production (1). 


MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
Ocimum leaf feeder (Syngamia abruptalis – Lepidoptera), the larvae feed in folded leaves.
Birds are reported to cause damage after transplantation (1).

Diseases:
Ocimum is attacked by many diseases. The common ones are:
Leaf spot – Caused by Corynespora cassicola. 
Symptoms: This appears as small water woaked spots which turn brown later.
Scab – Caused by Elsinow arxii. 
Symptoms: drooping of the leaves, distortion of the tender twigs and defoliation of the leaves with puckering.
Blight – Caused by Alternaria sp. Affects mainly the young plants. 
Symptoms: Leading to drying up.
Control: Spraying Dithane z-78 (0.2%) or Dithane M-45 (0.2%) two to three times at weekly intervals.
Leaf Blight – Caused by Colletotrichum capsici
Symptoms: Small chlorotic spots on the leaves which enlarge rapidly.
Basil wilt – Caused by Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. basilici Disease is more severe during the rainy season. 
Symptoms: Brown streaks on the stem, later the wilting of leaves, which spreads to the whole plant. 
Basal rot: Caused by Rhizoctonia solani, to a lesser extent by Microdochium tabacinum and below 20˚C also by Sclerotinia spp. 
Symptoms: Damping off in rapidly expanding circular patches. Spreads rapidly through the soil.
Grey mould: Caused by Botrytis cinerea.
Black spot: Caused by Colletotrichum spp.
Symptoms: Dry necrotic spots on leaves (1).

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Sweet basil is a popular savory herb. Both fresh and dried leaves are used to impart fragrant, warm and sweet flavour with pungent and clove-like notes to dishes and drinks. Leaves complement many salads and soups, vegetable dishes like tomato paste, pasta sauces etc. lamb is often flavoured with basil leaves. In Italian cooking leaves are used in pizzas, pasta, chicken and cheese dishes and in the famous pesto. In France leaves are popular in omelettes and soups. Leaves should be added at the end of cooking to best retain their flavour. Leaves are the source of essential oil and oleoresin mainly applied in industry to flavour baked goods, sauces, pickles, vinegar, meat products and to modify flavour of chartreuse liqueurs. Oil is also used in cosmetics, dental and oral products and occasionally in perfumes and to repel bugs and flies (1). 

Parts Used: Whole plant


Harvesting Practices:When grown for fresh or dried leaves, they are harvested just before flowering. For essential oil production it is harvested in full bloom. Leaves and shoots are harvested when 15-20 cm long, 80-110 days after harvesting. In temperate areas 1-2 cuts may be taken and in warmer areas, 3-5 cuts per year are possible. Plants should be cut 10-15 cm above the ground, leaving 2-4 pairs of leaves to ensure good regrowth and allow subsequent harvests to be taken at 15-20 days. The total yield of fresh leaf varies much. Leaf yield of 20,000–25,000 kg/ ha is reported from USA. In India, where flowers and leaves are distilled separately, a good crop yields about 13 kg of flower oil and 27 kg of leaf oil (1).

Post Harvest Handling
Before processing, the leaves and branches are washed and cleaned by removing all weeds and extraneous material. To maintain their aroma and colour, leaves should be dried at temperatures not exceeding 35˚C. Dried leaves are chopped to specific sizes and graded. Then they are packed in polythene line cartons or bags and marketed. For oil production plants are dried in the field for 1-3 day and then subjected to steam distillation (1).


Produce and Products: Fresh and dried basil leaves and essential oil.

Chemistry: On an average the whole herb contains 0.1% to 0.25% oil, while the inflorescence contain 0.4-0.5% oil. When the whole herb is harvested and distilled it can give about 30 kg oil per ha. Composition of the oil varies of the oil varies with the variety. Chemical compositions range as follows: Cis – Ocimene +1, 8-cineole: trace-13.6% linalool: 0.2-75.4% methyl chavicol: 0.03-88.6% methyl cinnamate: trace – 15.5%; eugenol: trace-11.2%.
Per 100 g fresh material approximately contains: water – 87 g, protein – 3 g, fat – 1 g, carbohydrates – 5 g, fibre – 2 g and ash – 2 g. seed is rich in protein and fat but less in carbohydrates. 1000 seed weight is 0.8-1.3 g. In US, regulatory status ‘generally regarded as safe’ (GRAS) has been accorded to sweet basil (GRAS 2118), sweet basil oil (GRAS 2119) and sweet basil oleoresin (GRAS 2120) (1).

Basil oil (from Philippines) contains: 53.3% methyl chavicol, 22.0% linalool, 4.5% β-caryophyllene, 3.7% 1,8-cineole, 1.4% eugenol, 1.4% α-pinene, 0.8% geranyl acetate, 0.7% (E)-β-ocimene, 0.7% geraniol, 0.5% δ-guaiene, 0.5% α-terpineol, 0.5% myrcene, 0.4% neral, 0.3% camphor, 0.3% β-pinene, 0.3% δ-3-carene, 0.3% limonene, 0.3% α-pinene, 0.1% terpinolene, 0.1% chavicol, 0.1% para-cymene, 0.1% methyl eugenol, 0.1% γ-terpinene, 0.1% copaene (unknown isomer), 0.1% 3-octanol, 0.1% camphene, trace (Z)-β-ocimene, trace (Z)-3-hexenol, trace octyl acetate, trace α-phellandrene, trace α-terpinene, trace α-thujene, trace benzaldehyde, trace borneol, trace (Z)-3-hexenyl acetate, (Source: Hasegawa et al., 1997) (5).

Processing Techniques:

Other uses: Plant is acrid, bitter, aromatic, thermogenic, anti-inflammatory, anodyne, alexiphrmic, antispasmodic, galactagogue, appetising, carminative, digestive, stomachic, anthelmintic, cardiotonic, antiemetic, insecticidal, antibacterial, stimulant, pectoral, antidiarrhoeal, emmenagogue, diuretic, expectorant, depurative, sudorific and antipyretic. Useful in vitiated conditions of vāta and kapha, halitosis, inflammations, otalgia, cephalalgia, arthralgia, rheumatoid arthritis, spasmodic affections, agalactia, anorexia, dyspepsia, flatulence, colic, helminthiasis, cardiac debility, leucoderma, pruritus, ringworm, other skin diseases, cough, asthma, bronchitis, strangury, amenorrhoea, dysmenorrhoea, diarrhoea, vomiting, pharyngopathy, neuralgia, ophthalmia, giddiness, intermittent and malarial fevers and catarrh. Seeds are acrid, bitter, emollient, refrigerant, vulnerary, haemostatic, aphrodisiac, stimulant, diuretic and tonic. Useful in vitiated conditions of vāta and pitta, dipsia, burning sensation, wounds, sores, haemorrhages, haemorrhoids, seminal weakness, strangury, diarrhoea, chronic dysentery and general debility (3). 

In Vietnam, used to treat fever and malaria. Oil possesses antiseptic activity against number of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria and inhibits the activity of several fungi. An alcoholic extract of O. basilicum has some anti-HIV-1 activity in vitro (1).

Basil can be relaxing to muscles, including smooth muscles. Also be used to soothe insect bites. Beneficial for mental fatigue, stimulate and sharpen the sense of smell (4).

Export potential:
Several grades of Indian basil are available for the international consumer. Basil leaves are also offered for export in small quantities (2). 

Grown commercially in Mediterranean, US, Madagascar and nearby islands, India and Thailand (1).
