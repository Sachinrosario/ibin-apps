Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Sterculia foetida is a moderate to tall trees, rising in some instances to 30 meters, bark whitish, flaking off, branches whorled, horizontal. There are over 100 species of sterculias distributed through all warm or tropical climates. These forest trees are quite common all over Indochina and sometimes cultivated. In India it is found on the west coast from konkan southwards (1, 3, 5, 8, 12, 19).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
The leaves are digitate, crowded at the ends of the branches, leaflets 5 to 9, subsessile, 10 to 18 by 3.8 to 5 cm., oblong-lanceolate, acute or acuminate, tapering to the base, pubescent when young, glabrous on both surfaces when mature, petioles 15 to 23 cm. Long, terete, grooved, stipules caducous. (1, 3, 5, 8, 12, 19)

Flowers 2.5 to 3.8 cm. In diameter, in erect racemose panicles 15 to 20 cm. Long, formed immediately under the young leaves, pedicels 2 to 2.5 cm. Long, joined above the middle. Calyx deeply divided, lobes linear-oblong, subacute, spreading, much longer than the tube, slightly pubescent ouside, wolly inside and the flowers are red or purple in colour. Male flowers: Staminal column 9 mm. Long, curved, hairy at the base, bearing 10 to 15 anthers. Female flowers: Carpels 5, downy, gynophore stout, 6 mm. Long. Staminodes subsessile, in a ring beneath the carpels. Styles deflexed, hairy. Follicles 1-5 (commonly 3), 10 to 12.5 cm. Long, boat-shaped, shortly beaked, thick and woody, bright red when ripe, nearly glabrous without, quite glabrous within. The fruit with a red, segmented seedpod containing many edible black seeds. (1, 3, 5, 8, 12, 13, 16, 19). Seeds numerous ¾ inch long, ovoid-oblong, the seed is black with a small yellow caruncle on one side of the base. (3, 12 19)

Breeding System :

Sterculia foetida L. is a cross pollinated crop and is self-incompatible and breed through xenogamy only. (11)

Phenology :

The plant flowers during March to May.

Pollinators:

The flowers are pollinated by Honey bees. (11)

Seedling Germination :

Cultivation:

It needs warm climates and fertile grounds. It is multiplied by seeds. These plants can also be raised by tissue culture. Hypocotyl and shoot tip explants derived from the seedlings of Sterculia foetida were placed on MS medium supplemented with various cytokinin and auxin combinations. Cytokinin-like N6-benzylaminopurine (BAP), kinetin (KN), thidiazuron (TDZ) and auxin-like indole acetic acid (IAA), naphthalene acetic acid (NAA) and indole butyric acid (IBA) were supplemented either individually or in combinations. The regenerated shoots were separated and placed on the rooting medium augmented with different auxin-like IAA, IBA and NAA (0.1-2 mgL-1). IAA (2 mgL-1) induced 3-4 roots from the cut end of the micro shoots. Rooted plantlets were acclimatized to field conditions by placing them in pots containing sterilized sand, soil and manure mixture (1:1:1) and subsequently transferred to field with 40% survival (8, 26, 27).

Pests:

A new report of Sylepta balteata Fab. (Lepidoptera: Pyralidae) as a pest on Sterculia foetida Linn. was recorded (24).


Diseases:

Shot-hole leaf disease of Sterculia foetida, fine mist sprays before rainy periods of fungicides containing copper and/or sulfur are recommended for control. (21)

Economic Potential and Uses

The seeds yield 30-36 % of a bland, light yellow, fatty oil with feeble drying powder. The pure kernel oil is liquid, whereas the oil obtained from the pulpy layer of the testa is a soft, buttery solid. The seed kernel oil when heated to 2400 C, polymerizes and produces a solid rubber like jelly (due to the high percentage of sterculic acid) which may be used to make rubber substitutes. Attempts have also been made to utilize the drying properties of seed-fat in combination with the shell-oil complex of bhilawa (Semecarpus anacardium) in making low-stoving enamels. The seed-oil is suitable for culinary purposes, but is mostly used as an illuminant, other likely uses are in the surface-coating industry and in soap making. It has also been reported to be externally used and internally given in itch and other diseases of skin. (1, 3, 20)

Parts used

Seeds, stem, bark, leaves, fruits. (1)

Uses

Seeds: Yeilds seed oil. The seeds of are edible and have a pleasant taste similar to cocoa. You can eat them like nuts, either raw or roasted.

Stem: The wood is yellowish white to reddish brown colour. It is perishable in the open, but quite durable for interior work. The wood is used locally for inferior construction, dugouts, rough packing-cases, tea chests and cheap plywood. The tree also exudes a gum, resembling tragacanth, which can be used for book-binding and similar purposes.

The bark and leaves are reported to be aperient and diuretic. The bark yeilds a weak cordage-fibre.

Fruits: Decoction of the fruits is mucilaginous and astringent.

(1, 3, 12, 14, 17, 18, 19, 20)

Processing Techniques

On a commercial scale oil is obtained from the seed by expression or by extraction by solvents the former being a common practice in the villages. There are three traditional methods of expressing oil from seed. (1) Boiling method (2) Chappa method and (3) Bullock Ghani method. In boiling method the depulped seed is boiled in water for some time and then dried. Thereafter it is roasted on a hot pan until the shell become crisp and friable. The whole seed is crushed into powder and it is boiled in water for some time after which the oil floating at the top water is separated by decantation. In the Chappa method the powdered kernel is steamed over a vessel of boiling water and pressed between the two limbs of chappa. In bullock ghani method the powdered kernel is sprinkled with little hot water and pressed in a Bullock-Ghani. (22, 23)

Back to Top

Other Uses

The oil from the seed is a mild laxative and used as a carminative, the fruit is employed in gonorrhoea, the bark and leaves also have medinal value. The wood boiled with seed oil is said to be employed in rheumatism. Various parts of the tree is reported to contain hydrocyanic acid. An alcoholic extract of S. foetida leaves showed reduced exploratory activity in mice. The extract also exhibited significant antiinflammatory activity in animals (3, 14, 20, 25).

Export Potential

At present there is no export of the oil. World demand for medicinal purposes is estimated at about 100 tonnes of oil. Thailand, which exported the oil in the past, at present is not able to do so because of depletion of the resource. (9)
