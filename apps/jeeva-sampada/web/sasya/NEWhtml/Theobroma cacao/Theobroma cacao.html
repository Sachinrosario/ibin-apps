Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
A Small Evergreen tree considered native to the tropical forests of the Amazon Valley in South America. It is now extensively grown in the continents of Africa, Asia, South America and in parts of North America. In India it was introduced 50 years ago from Srilanka. In India cocao is cultivated or grown in Kanyakumari, Tirunelveli, Palni Hills of Tamil Nadu. Nynad, Trivandrum, Quilon and Kottayam in Kerala. Chikmagalur, South Kanara and North Kanara Districts in Karnataka. Orissa and certain parts of eastern India. Cacao is cultivated in the tropical lowlands where the maximum temperature does not fall below 15deg celsius, the absolute minimum not below 10 deg celsius. The main production centres are confined to 10 deg north and south of the equator. It is grown upto altitudes of 1000 metres where the low temperatures are the limiting factor. The rainfall should be uniform throughout the year a minimum of 90 to 100 mm per month is required and an annual precipitation of 1500 to 2000 mm unless supplemented by irrigation.

Cacao thrives on a wide range of soil types, with pH ranging from 4.5 to 7.0. It requires a deep soil. Impervious soils or those with hard substrata are unsuitable. Clayey soils or mixtures of clay and sand are preferable. Ill-drained soils are very inimical to cocao trees. In India where a monsoonal type of climate exists it is difficult to find ideal locations comparable with those in West Africa (1, 2, 3, 4).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A small evergreen tree, up to 9 m. in height, with a dense rounded crown. Tree shows dimorphic branching. Seedlings form a single main stem or chupon, 1-1.5 m high at about 14 months. The terminal bud then breaks up into 3-5 meristems to give the so-called jorquette (the bud being used up in the process), and grow out into lateral plagiotropic fan branches, which may be almost horizontal. Further increase in height is made by an axillary bud just below the jorquette and this produces an orthotropic sucker or chupon which grows up vertically between the fan branches and then repeats the growth pattern by forming another jorquette and a second whorl of fan branches. Trunks or stems below a jorquette produce only chupons, these are morphologically the same as the trunk, are determinate in growth and have a phyllotaxy of 3/8.

Leaves large, simple, dark green when mature, petiole 1-4 cm long, longer on chupons, pubescent, well-marked pulvinus at each end, stipules lanceolate, 5-20 x 1-2 mm, pubescent, early deciduous, except when chupons about to branch when they form a brush at tip, lamina elliptic or obovate-oblong, entire, usually glabrous, 12-60 x 4-20 cm, being largest in middle of tree where they receive least light, base rounded and obtuse, apex acuminate, main vein prominent, lateral veins pinnate, 9-12 pairs, axil spot correlated with red pod, absence of spot with green pod.

Leaves on fan branches produced in flushes, young leaves being produced in rapid succession by burst of activity of terminal buds, which then return to dormant condition and new growth hardens. Young leaves soft and limp and hang down vertically, pale green or various shades of red depending on clone. Seedlings are Tap-rooted, grows straight downwords to a depth of 2m in well aerated soil. Fruits drupe, but is commonly called a pod, indehiscent, variable in size, 10-32 cm long, in shape from nearly spherical to cylindrical, pointed or blunt, smooth or warty, with or without 5 or 10 furrows, young pods white, green or red and ripening green, yellow, red or purple (1, 5, 6, 7, 8).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Inflorescence of Cacao is Cauliflorous, with flowers and fruits produced on older leafless parts of trunk and lateral branches, which were originally leaf-axils, they are long pediciliate, yellowish or pink, small. Flowers regular, pentamerous, hermaphrodite. Pedicels 1-2 cm long, greenish, whitish or reddish, with sparse hairs and constricted abscission layer at base. Sepals 5, pink or whitish, 7-10 x 1.5-2.5 mm, triangular, rather fleshy, valvate, shortly united at base. Petals 5, smaller than sepals, base obovate 3-4 mm long, expanding into concave, cup-shaped pouch, white with 2 prominent purple guide lines, end of petal spatulate, 2-3 mm long, yellow, bending outwards and backwards and attached to pouch by narrow connective. Androecium with 5 outer staminodes, opposite sepals, erect, pointed, with dark purple centres and whitish ciliate margins and form ring round style, 5 inner fertile stamens with 4 pollen sacks which dehisce longitudinally, filaments bend outwards so that anthers are concealed in pouch of corresponding petal. Ovary superior, carpels 5, ovules numerous, anatropous, deeply penetrating stylar canal makes placentation axile at base of ovary and parietal above, style single, 2-3 mm long, hollow, shorter than surrounding fence of staminodes, divided at tip into 5 stigmas which are often more or less adherent (1, 5, 6, 7, 8).

Breeding System :
Both Self and Cross-pollination occurs. Most pollination takes place in the first 2-3 hours after dawn except during or after heavy rain. Fertilization takes place 7-8 hours after pollination. Unpollinated flowers drop 24 hours after opening. The pollination efficiency is low, but this is compensated for by the large numbers of flowers produced, it is estimated that only one in every 500 flowers matures to a ripe fruit. Fruit setting is further complicated by self- and cross-incompatibility (5).

Phenology :
The tree begins to bear some flowers and set some fruits at the age of 3-4 years, but full bearing is attained only after 10 years.

Pollinators:
The pollination mechanism in cacao is not well understood. The structure of the flower is unsuitable for pollination by natural agencies. Owing to the lack of nectar, sticky nature of the pollen which prevents wind pollination. Insects are the chief pollinating agency and a certain amount of self pollination is effected by crawling insects such as thrips, ants and aphids. When these are excluded, pollination still occurs and some clones are self-incompatible and yet fruit well. Thus some insects which can fly or be carried by wind must be responsible. It is now believed that the main pollinating agents are ceratopogonid midges in Trinidad These midges appear to be attracted by and to feed on the purple tissues of the staminodes (5, 6).

Seed Biology :
Seeds are usually called beans, 20-60 per pod depending on cultivors. Usually arranged in 5 rows, variable in size, 2-4 x 1.2-2 cm, in shape ovoid or elliptic, and with white to deep purple cotyledons. Embryo develops late in seed using up endosperm. At maturity seed consists of two large convoluted cotyledons, a small embryo, a thin membrane which is the remains of the endosperm and a leathery testa. Fresh seeds surrounded by mucilaginous, whitish, sugary, acid pulp which develops from the outer integument of the ovule (5, 6).

Seedling Germination :
Seeds will germinate immediately on reaching maturity and are viable for only a short time. Germination may take place in the pod. Removal of the testa speeds up germination by a few days, but such seeds lose viability rapidly if allowed to dry out. The moisture content of the cotyledons should be kept at about 50 per cent. Under suitable conditions with free access of air and protection against water loss seeds may be stored for 10-13 weeks without losing viability. Seeds may be transported or stored in charcoal powder of 30 per cent moisture content in perforated containers, polythene bags have proved very satisfactory. Germination is epigeal. The cotyledons and hypocotyl develop chlorophyll and the first true leaves appear 15-20 days after germination. They are usually planted 1-2 inches deep with the hilum scar downwards or horizontally and germinate in 2-3 weeks (5).

Cultivation:

Propagation by seed is the cheapest method. Fresh beans are to be used for sowing as they lose their viability after the pod is broken. They are planted with the pointed end upwards, sown either in bamboo baskets or polythene bags or raised beds. if sown on raised beds the young seedlings are usually transplanted in containers about 2 weeks after sowing. The seedlings will be in the nursery for 6 to 8 months before they are transplanted to the main field. Other methods of propogation include grafting, budding and layering. Cacao is grown in regulated shade, development of one main trunk only is allowed. The trees are grown close so that the foliage forms a canopy to keep the air cool and damp around the trees. Regular weeding before harvesting and removal of unwanted basal 'chupons' is taken up.

Cacao can be cultivated as an intercrop in coconut and arecanut gardens. Mixed plantations can be raised on an area 4mx 4m the cacao seedlings occupying the centre of the square or with the normal spacing of 2.7m x 2.7m for area. Pits of 90cm2 with compost are used for planting the seedlings. Both require artificial shade during the first one or two hot-weather periods. Banana can be profitably grown as a shade crop during the first 2 years. Subsequently, the shade cast by the arecanut trees will be sufficient for the cacao plants. The cacao plants can be manured with 100g of N, 40g of P2O5 and 140g of K2O per tree annually in 2 split doses. The plantation is irrigated at weekly intervals in the hot months. All the fan branches are cut to a height of one metre from the ground level. This operation is very important for obtaining good yields. In coconut plantations spaced at about 7.5 metres cacao may be planted either in a single row alternating with coconut or in double rows in triangular positions between the rows of coconut. This crop combination of cacao and coconut has synergistic effect on the yield of both the crops.


Varieties: The first type originating in venezuela was called 'Cariollo' and was the highest quality in the European markets and the later variety 'Forastero' or 'Trinitario' fell into the inferior quality. The Ecuador cocoa is quite different from Forastro and called 'nacional' of indigenous status.

There are 3 groups of cocaos which are also equal to the market grades. :

1.Criollo cacaos. They include Venezualan cacaos and also those which are native to Maxico or central America and Columbia. The pods are yellow when ripe , deeply furrowed, warty and pointed with thin pod wall and plump seeds. Fresh cotyledons are either white or pale violet.

2.Amazonian Forasteros. They are cocoas of Brazil and West Africa. Pods are yellow, less deeply furrowed, round ended with woody pod wall difficult to cut. The seeds are flattened and fresh cotyledons are violet and black. The seed characters are the most important in this case also.

3.Trinitarios. this is a botanically complex group which is harder but of inferior quality. The members of this group differ in parentage. For convenience they can be called hybrid mixtures of recent origin (1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10).

Economic Potential and Uses

Cacao seeds contain fat 40 to 50 per cent Oil, which is a yellowish white solid, with an odour resembling that of cocao. Cacao butter is used for wide range of edible purposes. It is used as an ingredient in cosmetic ointments and in pharmacy for coating pills and preparing suppositories. It has excellent emollient properties and is used to soften and protect chapped hands and lips. Cocoa butter is used in confections and in manufacture of tobacco and soap. Cocoa butter has been described as the world's most expensive fat, used rather extensively in the emollient bullets used for hemorrhoids. Butter of cacao is a bland article, rather agreeable to the taste, and highly nutritious, it has been used as a substitute for or an alternate with, cod-liver oil, and as an article of diet during the last days of pregnancy. It has also been employed in the formation of pessaries, as a lubricant for massaging, for rectal, vaginal, and other difficulties. It likewise enters into preparations for rough or chafed skin, chapped lips, sore nipples, various cosmetics, pomatums, and fancy soaps. The butter is sometimes adulterated with coconut and palm oils and srearines prepared from them. Hydrogenated cottonseed oil, tallow and lard are also used as adulterants (6, 11, 12, 13, 19).

Uses

Cacao seeds are the source of commercial cocoa, chocolate, and cocoa butter. Fermented seeds are roasted, cracked and ground to give a powdery mass from which fat is expressed. This is the cocoa from which a popular beverage is prepared. In the preparation of chocolate, this mass is mixed with sugar, flavoring, and extra cocoa fat. Milk chocolate incorporates milk as well. The shell and germ are other by-products of the cacao-processing industry. The shells are of low nutritive value. The shells may be used as feed for adult cattle, provided they do not constitute more than 2.5 per cent of the total feed. The shells from the sun-dried beans contain varying, but appreciable, amounts of vitamin D their inclusion in the feeds for cows is reported to improve the content of vitamin D in milk. Infusions of the shells are reported to be added to coffee beans during roasting to improve the taste of coffee. Ground shells are sometimes sold as cocoa-tea. In actual practice, however, the shells are used as conditioners for commercial fertilizers or as fuel. Ground shells may also be used as filler for thermosetting resins in the plastics industry.

The outer wall (husk) of the pod is reported to yield nine per cent furfural. The wood, though not recognized as a commercial timber, is suitable for purposes requiring toughness and strength, rather than attractive appearance or resistance to decay (1, 12, 13, 20).

Harvesting Practices
The tree bears its crop over several months of the year, though there are generally two main crops. It is not yet clear in exactly which months the main crops will occur in India. At Kallar, the peak harvests are during April-May and December-January. Only ripe pods, which are yellow, orange or red, according to the type of cacao grown, are harvested when most of the pods assume a distinctive colour. They remain in a suitable state for harvesting for 2-3 weeks. As the pods are borne on cushions on the stem, they should be harvested carefully, the cushions cease to bear if damaged. Harvesting is, therefore, done with a special sharp knife, a hooked one on a pole being used to reach the pods high up on the tree. Care should be taken to cut the stalk of the pod a little away from the cushion, so that the stump left on the cushion drops off later, leaving a well-healed scar impervious to diseases. The age at which the maximum crop can be harvested depends on the spacing given and the rate of growth of the tree, and is generally between 15 and 25 years. The yield begins to decline after that, though the trees may continue to bear a good crop up to the age of 75 years or even more.

A good tree bears 70-80 pods in a year, though under favourable conditions some trees may yield 300 or more pods. About 400 kg of cured beans per hectare is considered a fair yield, though a yield of 600-700 kg may be obtained from good and well cultivated trees (1, 9, 10).

Cocoa liquor, paste, butter, presscake and powder are the major cocoa products traded in the market.

The cocoa butter has good keeping qualities under satisfactory storage conditions due to the presence of fat-soluble antioxidants. Two phytosterols, sitosterol and stigmasterol, have been identified in the unsaponifiable matter. Small quantities of the obromine and caffeine are present in the butter (1, 14, 15, 20).

Processing Techniques

  • The cocoa nib is ground and the cocoa butter is released in a fluid referred to as chocolate or cocoa mass.

  • Grinding is usually accomplished using steel disc mills, with the discs mounted either horizontally or vertically and with a central steel disc rotating at high speed between two stationary steel faces, all of which are suitably grooved. The nib is fed to the centre of the discs.

  • Some liquor production is also done by cylindrical steel roll refiners and by impact pulverizers of the hammer mill type.

  • The chocolate liquor temperature is controlled during grinding and maintained at 158-176 0F. (70-80 0C.).

  • For efficient removal of cocoa butter, pressing should be at the highest temperature consistent with the quality of the product required. For efficient pressing of low fat cocoas the liquor should ideally contain 1-2% moisture.

  • When the press cake is to be used for cocoa powder production, the hydraulic press, either vertical or horizontal is invariably used. When cocoa butter production is the main object of the pressing, the extrusion press or expeller, a form of cage press can be used.

  • The material for extrusion i.e. the nib or whole bean, is conditioned by steaming to facilitate cell rupture and consequent release of fat.

  • It is then fed continuously to the expeller, which subjects it to intense compression when the butter escapes through gaps in the cage wall.

  • The butter contains a percentage of solid matter and must be clarified before use, usually by centrifuging and plate filtration. Modern large expellers give residual fat contents as low as 8%.

  • The remaining butter may be extracted from the expeller cocoa residue by solvent extraction. Hexane and trichloroethylene have been used, as solvents in conventional-type plants.

  • The fat was formerly used in the crude state i.e., as pressed from the beans, but, ordinary refining methods of neutralisation, bleaching and deodorizing are now used and fully refined cocoa butter is now commonplace (13, 16).

Other Uses
Theobromine, the alkaloid contained in the beans, resembles caffeine in its action, but its effect on the central nervous system is less powerful. Its action on muscle, the kidneys and the heart is more pronounced. It is used principally for its diuretic effect due to stimulation of the renal epithelium, it is especially useful when there is an accumulation of fluid in the body resulting from cardiac failure, when it is often given with digitalis to relieve dilatation. It is also employed in high blood pressure as it dilates the blood-vessels. It is best administered in powders or cachets. Reported to be antiseptic, diuretic, ecbolic, emmenagogue, and parasiticide, cacao is a folk remedy for alopecia, burns, cough, dry lips, eyes, fever, listlessness, malaria, nephrosis, parturition, pregnancy, rheumatism, snakebite, and wounds. Cocoa butter is applied to wrinkles in the hope of correcting them (1, 6, 17, 19).

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

Cocoa as a crop has become important for the country. The domestic demand for cocoa based products has been growing fast during the past few years. In the recent past, the use of cocoa was restricted more or less to the production of chocolates and other confectioneries, bakeries. icecreams, soft drinks and other beverages. But cocoa is increasingly finding widespread applications in pharmaceuticals, cosmetics. etc. The consumption of cocoa in the country was placed at about 1, 500 tonnes per annum during the seventies. This rose to more than 3,000 tonnes during Ninties. Cocoa can provide reasonable return to the small and marginal farmers as the size of the farms is small in Kerala and coastal Karnataka. The crop can be considered as a profitable enterprise to supplement the income of the farmers from the main crops. Apart from that India is endowed with climatically suitable areas for the cultivation of cocoa and the extensive plantation crops like coconut and arecanut in the west coast of the country can provide the required shade for the plant. Cocoa can be raised in these gardens without much difficulty and extra cost (1, 16).

Export Potential

Cocoa has a good export potential as its world consumption is estimated to be about 3 million tonnes. India can take advantage of the trade opportunities in international markets particularly when production in major producing countries suffers on account of natural disasters. The earnings from the exports of cocoa products were very meagre, amounting to a few million rupees. However, the country has a good potential to export cocoa beans and other products in view of the availability of suitable areas and the industrial processing capacity built up over the years. The quality of Indian cocoa beans is also rated as good in the international market (16).

Miscellaneous Information

Chocolate is made from the seeds of a plant called Theobroma cacao. The seeds are dried and roasted and then processed to form cocoa, the basic ingredient in chocolate and chocolate products. The use of cocoa for eating and drinking probably dates back several thousand years. The first evidence of cocoa use comes from cooking vessels containing cocoa residue. Scientists have determined these pots to be from at least 460 to 480 A.D.
By 1828, the cocoa press was developed. The press enabled workers to extract cocoa butter from the cacao bean. Ground roasted beans and sugar were added to the cocoa butter to produce dark eating (solid) chocolate. The first commercially prepared dark chocolate was produced in about 1847. Milk chocolate, made with the addition of dried milk solids, was developed by the Swiss in about 1876 (12).
