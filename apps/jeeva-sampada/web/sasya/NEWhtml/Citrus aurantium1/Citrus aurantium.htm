DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES                                                            
 Tree medium-sized with rounded crown, up to 10 m tall; twigs angular when young, soon terete, with slender axillary spines; leaves 7.5-10 cm long, broadly elliptical, subacute at apex, cuneate or barely rounded below, petioles about 2.6 cm long, broadly winged above, tapering to a wingless base, and the leaves are upright with dark green and distinctively scented leaves. Fruit sub globose, about 7.5 cm in diameter, slightly flattened at both ends, 10- to 12-locular; rind thick, rough, bright orange-red when ripe; pulp acid; core hollow when ripe and then able to float in water; seeds numerous. Oil glands situated beneath minute sunken areas in peel.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Flowers solitary or few in axils, about 5-12% staminate only, very fragrant with oil of neroli.
 Breeding System :It is highly polyembroyonic.

Phenology :
(Not for sour orange)
Flowering and Fruiting - The blossoming periods of both sweet orange and santra differ in different climatic zones of India. In northern India where there is distinct winter, both the oranges flower only once a year in March and the fruits mature in November-December. In South India, the flowering seasons are longer and less distinct and two crops are obtained. In the more even climates of central and western India and Andhra Pradesh, flowering occurs thrice a year-in February, June and October . The February flowering is called ambebahar, the June flowering mrigbahar and the October flowering, hasta or hattibahar. Thus three crops are obtained in a year. Lime and lemons put forth new flushes and blossoms throughout the year. They do not require a resting period; they are harvested throughout the year. However, the season of maximum harvest differs in different parts of the country. A bearing mandarin tree produces 15-40,000 flowers but finally only 1-2,000 fruits are produced, the rest do not develop or drop off especially under unfavourable environmental conditions like late frosts, drought, or excessive rain or loss of soil moisture during summer months. A spray of growth hormones such as 2,4-D and 2,4,5-T has markedly improved fruit-set besides size and quality of fruit in mandarin. 2,4,5-T is also effective in 'Pineapple' and 'Valencia Late' sweet orange. Gibberellin sprays (GAs) are beneficial in grapefruit, sweet lime and 'Jaffa' and 'Pineapple' sweet oranges but such fruits develop a rough and thick skin and are less juicy.

Pollinators:

Bees and butterflies.

Seed Biology :
Fruit is large, round, usually 9-11 celled. Seeds are several in each cell, horizontal, loose in the pulp,.¾th inch long, oblong-ovoid, somewhat compressed, usually with a blunt point at the apex above the chalaza, and somewhat truncate at the hilum; testa soft, pale yellow, veiny; embryo of two large, unequal, irregular thick, plano-convex cotyledons and a small, superior radicle; endosperm none; not very unfrequently two ebryos are produced in a single seed.


Seed Germination:
Germination is most rapid at 320C,the seeds germinate in 3-4 weeks and are ready for planting in a secondary nursery when the seedlings have 4-6 leaves. But at lower temperature it takes time.Trees are propagated by seed, from cutting, or budding. Seeds are usually planted soon after being taken from fruit, as they cannot with stand much drying out.
Vegetative propagation - This is done by budding on appro priate rootstock. In budding, a growing bud of one plant (the scion) is grafted on to the rooted part of another (the rootstock) closely elatedlant.The most commonly used rootstocks are: Sour orang, Rough lemon, Kama khatta, Rangpur lime , Sweet orange , Citranges, Mandarin orange, Nasnaran, Trifoliate orange.
Budding - It is the most common method of vegetative propagation, particularly for sweet orange, mandarin and grapefruit.
Other methods besides propagating through seeds and buds, include raising plants from cuttings, air-layers and grafts. Sweet lime, acid lime, lemon, pummelo and citron are usually propagated from cuttings and air-layers in India. CULTIVATION:

Propagation

Citrus trees are propagated both by vegetative means and from seeds. Vegetative propagation is preferred because it ensures uniform quality, regular bearing, etc. However, seed propagation is still practiced for mandarins and acid limes. Even for grafting and budding, rootstocks are raised from seeds only. Also for developing new cultivars through hybridization, seed propagation is vital. Growing of oranges began with seedling trees, but as they do not produce trees similar to parents in quality, most of the advanced countries have switched over to budded plants since the last century. This has enabled the growers to secure earlier, more abundant and uniform fruiting besides adaptation to different agro-climatic conditions. Seedling trees are slow in coming into bearing tend to be thorny and grow tall and slender. Budded trees show less variation than seedling trees. Both mandarin and sweet oranges are easily budded by the orchard owner himself in leading citrus-growing countries. In India, seed is employed for propagating mandarin in the entire North-East region and South India. Sweet oranges are however, usually propagated by budding. Limes are mostly seed- propagated but recently there is a trend of using buds in several parts of the country.
After care
Irrigation: The young trees can be irrigated by basin system up to 8 years. Usually, under North Indian conditions, irrigation is given once a week during March and June and at fortnightly intervals from November to February. In Vidarbha, a moisture stress of 30-40 days is essential in clayey loams. About 28 irrigations are required during the rest of the year for mandarin. When the plants are grown in degraded lands, good yield can be obtained by conserving moisture with mulching and through proper water-harvesting of post-monsoon rains in rooting zones of theplants to meet the moisture requirement.

Manures and Fertilizers - Young and pre-bearing trees require judicious application of nitrogen to provide a proper balance between growth and production. Addition of farmyard manure in the pits prior to planting is essential. The recommended amount is 20-25 kg farmyard manure together with 0.5 kg ammonium sulphate. After the first year a mixture of 0.09 kg, each of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium is applied per tree. This dose is increased to 0.45 kg each of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium by the seventh year and the farmyard manure dose is increased to 50 kg per tree. In South India, from the second year of planting, 25-50 kg cattle manure or leaf compost and 0.5-1 kg oil-cake is given per tree; as age advances, up to 1 kg nitrogen is given. The manure and fertilizer are spread uniformly on the entire surface of the tree basin but leaving 15-30 cm around the trunk. . In Vidarbha, 'Nagpur' mandarin and sweet oranges are given 0.72-1.0 kg nitrogen per tree per year. The
dose is split into two, the first given at the time of irrigation after release of water stress and the second after fruit-set when fruits are pea-sized. Potash (400-500 g/tree/year) and phosphorus (300 g/tree/year) are given only once at the time of release of water stress.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Leaves, Fruits, Seeds, Flower petals.

Uses

  • Fresh fruit, used as a garnish for beverages and meat.
  • Fresh juice used in beverages, marinates, and seasonings, and squeezed over avocadoes. Used, like other limes and lemons, by the health conscious, often with garlic and capsicum, in lieu of oil-and-vinegar dressing.
  • The oil, like that of the key lime is widely used in cosmetics and flavorings.


Harvesting Practices
In trees grown for fruits, fruits are harvested like any other citrus fruit. From bloom to harvest of fruit varies from 8-10 months, depending on locality and variety. Leaves, twigs and immature fruits are cut and used for oil distillation.
Fruit yields of 50MT/ha are reported. For petitgrain oil, 200-400 kg leaves will yield 1 kg oil. An average still will produce about 4 qts/day.

Produce and Products
The products of citrus are squash, syrup, crush, beverages, sweetened juice, concentrates, segments, jam, marmalade, pectin and peel-oil, the last two being the by-products.

Processing Techniques
There are several varieties of citrus fruits consumed in different ways. Mossambi, santra, grapefruit and pummelo are eaten fresh for their pleasant taste and flavour. They are rich in vitamin C and mineral salts. They contain citric acid, malic acid, sugars, fatty acids, essential oils, glycosides, pectins, P-carotene and col- ouring matter. The sweetness of the juice is attributed to the sugars, glucose, fructose and sucrose present in it, which varies from less than 1% in certain limes to c 15% in some oranges. During ripening of the fruits, the concentration of acids diminishes and that of sugars increases. Citrus fruits also contain coumarins, psoralins, flavonoids and limonoids.
Different citrus fruits, specially oranges, are mainly processed for their juice from which many products like single strength canned juice, beverages, concentrates, etc. are obtained. Many by-products like volatile oil, pectin and cattle-feed are also obtained from some of these fruits. The second and third grade, ripe, sound oranges are used for processing. The fresh fruits of first grade for table con- sumption fetch better price. The yield of juice depends upon the variety, rootstock, soil type, cultural practices and climate.

In India, various products are manufactured mainly from the mandarin varieties such as 'Nagpur' santra, 'Coorg', 'Assam' and 'Khasi' oranges and 'Kinnow'. The sweet orange varieties 'Malta', 'Malta Blood Red', 'Mossambi' and 'Sathgudi' are used to a limited extent in the preparation of squashes in some areas mainly for blending when mandarins are in short supply. Some varieties of sweet orange are used in the preparation of concentrates.

Extraction of juice

Juice from oranges is extracted either by the application of pressure to the whole fruit or by pressing the halves of crosswise cut fruits on a suitable rosette. Various types of equipments have been developed for orange-juice extraction.

The different types of machines used are rotary juice extractors, Brown models, Taglith, Citromat and Polycitrus juice extractors. In all these, the fruits are cut into halves. In Brown models, the juice is extracted by reaming. In the Taglith, the halves carried in the depression of drums are pressed by perforated heads, and in Citro mat which contains a drum carrying small spikes, the fruit halves are drawn by the drum towards the screen and the juice is squeezed out as the distance between the drum and the screen becomes narrower. In an FMC extractor, the fruit is initially placed between finger-like cups when a perforated steel tube cuts a hole in the bottom of the fruit and the juice flows through the cut-end into the perforated tubes as the fruit is pressed by the upper cup. As the fruit is squeezed, the peel-oil is also pressed from the rind by the pinching action of the fingers and the oil is washed down by water sprays into the trough. This machine also does preliminary screening of seeds and large pulp particles.

CFTRI Citrus juice extractor - The cutting of mandarin fruits into halves and extraction are accomplished in the same machine thereby saving labour. The machine can handle up to 10,000 fruits per hour. The juice is collected in two separate streams, one completely free from peel-oil, and the other, mixed with traces of peel-oil.

Pressure exerted during extraction of juice has a significant effect on the quality of juice. Excessive pressure increases the yield of juice but it becomes bitter due to the presence of more limonin. Fruits extracted with less pressure yield juice having higher colour values and better flavour. For profitable commercial venture, the juice yield should be 55% or more of the whole weight of the fruit.

Screening of the juice - Irrespective of the method of extraction, juice should be screened to remove the pieces of peel, membranes, coarse pulp and seeds, retaining only the natural fruit-cells. The presence of fruit-cells gives an attractive appearance to the juice and exerts a marked effect on the mouth-feel.

In screening, the coarse particles are removed by passing the juice through a perforated screen having 3mm holes. The juice so obtained is called "raw juice" and may be used in the preparation of squash, and in some cases, the pasteurized juice. For the production of concentrates, further screening through smaller perforations is necessary. Excess of air should not be incorporated in the juice. One of the best methods of screening consists of passing the juice through a long, slightly inclined, slowly revolving, stainless steel screen in the form of a drum. The screened juice flows into the blending tanks where it is tested for soluble solids, colour, etc. and is sweetened or acidified as permitted. The screened juice is used for preparing different products.

Other Uses

  1. The Tahiti lime is larger and better suited to large scale agriculture than the key lime. The Tahiti lime thick skin is better for machine handling and long truck trips.
  2. The leaves are prescribed in the treatment of swollen legs, in bronchitis and as a tonic; they are used as tea in headache. An aqueous extract of the flowers is a stimulant and refreshing drink usually employed in fever, inflammation, nervous and hysterical cases. It is very useful in scurvy.It is used as a lotion for skin and hands and is taken internally for dysentery, diarrhoea and rheumatism.
  3. The fruits are laxative and also used for stomach pain. The juice is used with salt as a remedy for ringworm. The fresh rind of the fruit is rubbed on face for acne and on parts affected by eczema. The oil from the rind is externally used as a stimulant liniment in gout and rheumatism.
