Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Terminalia catappa is a large, handsome, deciduous or sometimes semi-evergreen tree considered native of Malaysia and western Pacific seashores, cultivated and become naturalized throughout the tropics and near-tropics. The tree is common in the Andamans in the beach forests on the raised sandy beaches and extends into the forests behind where it is confined to sandy or shingly soils. It also extends to areas not far from the sea where it is invariably confined to sandy soil or to diluvial deposits, formed of detritus. The associates of T. catappa in these forests are Barringtonia asiatica, Calophyllum inophyllum, Erythrina variegata, var. orientalis Gyrocarpus americonus, Hibiscus tiliaceus, Morinda citrifolia, Pongamia pinnata, and Thespesia populnea. The tree is commonly planted in India, in compounds and gardens for ornament and also for the fruit. The mean annual rainfall in its natural habitat is 313 cm. With 150 rainy days in a year. The tree is indifferent to light, being able to grow both under moderate shade and in full overhead light (4, 5, 6).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Terminalia catappa is a 18-24 m. tall, having a bole of 12 m., large, handsome tree which forms a symmetrical, upright silhouette in youth with horizontal branches reaching 35 feet in width. The branches are arranged in obvious tiers, giving the tree a pagoda-like shape. As the tree grows older, the crown spreads and flattens on the top to form a wide-spreading vase shape. The large, 12-inch-long and six-inch-wide, glossy green, leathery leaves change to beautiful shades of red, yellow, and purple before dropping in winter. The tree has smooth grey bark and often buttressed at the base. Leaves alternate, obovate, 15-36 cm long, 8-24 cm wide, subcordate at the base and usually with 2 glands, petiole short, leaves turning red before falling and quickly replaced. Drupes are 2.5 inches long and mature from green to yellow or red during the summer. The outside husk is corky fiber with an inner thin green flesh. The inside holds the edible, almond-like kernel (4, 5, 7, 8, 9).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Flowers are small greenish-white in colour, in short elongated clusters, appearing on upper leaf axils which are star shaped in slender racemes. Described as having a foetid smell. Inflorescence spicate, the female and male flowers are on the same tree; these flowers are inconspicuous and not very showy. Male flowers towards the apex with female flowers below, apetalous. Petals absent, Bracts minute, falling, male flowers most numerous. Calyx five parted (4, 7, 8, 9, 10).

Breeding System :

Phenology :
The tree bears flowers principally during March and April. And again during June and July; and a third flush may even appear. The fruits mostly ripen in May and again in October-November (11).

Seed Biology :
Fruit a somewhat compressed-ellipsoid drupe, 4-7 cm long, 2.5-3.8 cm wide, prominently keeled along the margins; epicarp thin, green turning yellow with a reddish blush; mesocarp fleshy, 3-6 mm thick, adherent to the fibrous husk of the hard-shelled stone containing the spindle-shaped seed; seed 3-4 cm long, 3-5 mm thick, testa very thin, brown, enveloping the coiled cotyledons or kernel. About 176 fruits weigh a kilogram (4, 9, 10).

Seedling Germination :
Seeds have good viability and germinate readily (25% germination) when raised in nurseries. Germination percentage was slightly higher for heavy seeds than the lighter seeds (16).

Cultivation:

Natural reproduction: Under favourable conditions on the coasts of the Andamans, T. catappa springs up fairly well. On loose soils, the fruits germinate and the seedlings establish readily. On the mainland, natural reproduction is unknown as the tree is grown only in gardens and the fruits, being edible, are collected, giving little chance for natural reproduction. The fruits are well adapted for widespread dispersal by water. Probably in the distant past the tree migrated to the Andaman Islands from Malaysia. Dispersal of fruits through fructivorous bats is also common.

Artificial regeneration -The seeds are available in adequate quantities during June-July and to a small extent at other times in the year. The germinative capacity is 25 per cent and the plant per cent is 25. The species can easily be raised from the seed, either by direct sowings or by entire planting. The freshly collected fruits are sown in the nursery in about July, and the seedings are transplanted in the South-West monsoonal rains of the following year (4).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

The leaves of the saplings are very susceptible to the attack by beetles and grasshoppers. It is often difficult to protect the saplings until they have grown sufficiently large to survive the attack. Apoderus tranquebaricus feeds inside the rolled leaf. Adoretus nitidus defoliates the plant. The larvae of several insects, such as Acrocercops erioplaca, A. ordinatella, A. regulifera and A. supplex, mine the leaf. Among other defoliators mention may be made of the larvae of Trabala vishnou and Selepa celtis.


Diseases:

Fungi is known to attack the tree, causing leafspot (Polyrhizon terminaliae, Gloeosporium terminaliae, Phyllosticla catappae), Dieback (Diploidia catappae), Sooty mould (Meliola amphitricha), Brownish heartrot (Fomes fastuosus), Brownish saprot (Polyporus calcuttenis), and Brownish pocketrot (Polyporous gilvus) (4).

Economic Potential and Uses

Sun-dried kernels yield 38-54% of an edible, bland, yellow, semi-drying oil known as Indian almond oil, which becomes turbid and rancid on standing. Oil closely resembles sweet almond, cotton seed, kapok and groundnut oils and could substitute for them for dietetic and industrial uses. The fruit embryo (about 10% of fruit weight) may be eaten and the oil (about 50% of the embryo mass) may be used in cooking. Oil is also used for making soap but its industrial use is limited by the difficulty in extracting the kernel. Oil is also used medicinally. Essential oil is extracted from the leaves and they possess antioxidative activity and hydroxyl radical scavenging activity. Oilcake is used as pig feed (4, 9, 10, 11, 15, 18).

Parts used

Fruits, leaves, bark and seeds.

Uses

Kernels of the fruits of this tree are much like the almond in flavour and are edible. They are said to be wholesome and nutritious. Air-dried kernels contain 52.0% fat, 25.4% protein, 14.6% fibre, 6% glucose and a small percentage of ash. The bark contains a small amount of colouring matter (dye) soluble in water, which produces light brownish yellow, light drab, golden-fawn and slate colours in silk, light drab, olive and grey in cotton, and pale-fawn in wool. The leaves were found to contain moderate amount of colouring matter (dye) which produces various shades of brownish yellow on silk and wool. The timber is used in house-building, wheel-wrights work and in general carpentry. It can be classed as a good constructional timber, very suitable for rafters, scantlings, posts and beams. On account of its some what wavy grain, it may yield a good ornamental ply board, if cut on a rotary machine. The shell comprising the fibrous covering, husk and hard endocarp, (contains 25 per cent pentosans) hence is a good source for making furfural. The tasar or katkura silkworm feed on the leaves of this tree. Tropical almond is also used by breeders of tropical aquarium fishes (These include, bettas, catfishes, black water tetras) to keep them healthy (4, 9, 10, 11, 12, 15).

Harvesting Practices
Seeds are collected from the well grown trees in June-July. Freshly fallen fruits are collected from the ground, the pulp is removed immediately and the seeds dried in sun. There is no organised way of seed collection or production. Two crops a year possible in some areas or even more or less continuous fruiting. A tree may yield 5 kg of kernels per year (4, 9).

Produce and Products

Oil is obtained from the seeds which is being used in the manufacture of soaps. Fruits have industrial importance and has wide number of uses. Leaves are good source of fodder and wood is extensively used in plywood industries. Apart from that plant have lot of medicinal value.

Processing Techniques

On a commercial scale oil is obtained from the seed by expression or by extraction by solvents the former being a common practice in the villages. There are three traditional methods of expressing oil from seed. (1) Boiling method (2) Chappa method and (3) Bullock Ghani method. In boiling method the depulped seed is boiled in water for some time and then dried. Thereafter it is roasted on a hot pan until the shell become crisp and friable. The whole seed is crushed into powder and it is boiled in water for some time after which the oil floating at the top water is separated by decantation. In the Chappa method the powdered kernel is steamed over a vessel of boiling water and pressed between the two limbs of chappa. In bullock ghani method the powdered kernel is sprinkled with little hot water and pressed in a Bullock-Ghani (4, 6).

Other Uses
The leaves act as a sudorific, and they are applied to rheumatic joints. The juice of the young leaves is used in South India to prepare an ointment for scabies, leprosy and other cutaneous diseases. Leaves are also used to get rid of intestinal parasites, treat eye problems, wounds and to stop bleeding during teeth extraction. Fruits used in the treatment of leprosy, headaches, cough, asthama and travel nausea. The bark is endowed with mild diuretic and fairly potent cardiotonic properties,. It contains tannin, which is used as an astringent in dysentery. Methanolic extract of the trunk bark showed physiological activity on blood pressure and action on the intestines of rabbit.

The kernel of Indian almond has shown aphrodisiac activity; it can probally be used in treatment of some forms of sexual inadequacies (premature ejaculation). Ethanol extract of the leaves shown potential in the treatment of sickle cell disorders (4, 9, 11, 12, 14).

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

The plant is of ethnobotanical interest and research suggests that it may be useful in many areas of medicine e.g. as an anti-sickling agent for those that suffer from sickle cell; as an aid to lowering high blood pressure and stress; as a treatment for some forms of liver disorders; as an aid in reducing the effect of several heart conditions etc. In Asia it has long been known that the leaves of T. Catappa contain a toxic, secondary metabolite, which has antibacterial properties. The dried leaves have been used as an aid in the breeding and keeping of some sensitive fish species.
