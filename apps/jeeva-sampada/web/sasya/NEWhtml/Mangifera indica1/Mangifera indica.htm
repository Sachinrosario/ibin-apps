HABIT & HABITAT:                                                                              
It is a king of the edible fruits, The plant is erect, branched, wide-spreading, evergreen, long-lived tree. Native to India and southeast Asia, mango is now pantropical. Although spread under cultivation for at least 4,000 years, mango has become naturalized in many areas. It is thought to have been spread to East Africa, Madagascar, and West Africa; then the Portuguese brought it to Brazil, from there it spread to the West Indies and Mexico.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES                                                                        
Erect, branched, wide-spreading, evergreen, long-lived tree reaching upto 10-30m height, bark grayish-brown and fissured. Leaves alternate, evergreen or nearly so, exstipulate, glabrous, shining, dark green, leathery, 8-40 cm long, 2-10 cm wide narrowly elliptic, lanceolate or oblong, apex acute or acuminate, base tapering into petiole, margin usually undulate, short petiolate, crowded at ends of branches, appearing in flushes. Taproot to 6 m deep with dense surface mat of feeder roots. Fruits of choice may weigh 450 g or more, and have good flavour, but many seedling fruits have coarse fibrous flesh and a flavour or turpentine.

Primary branches near base; individual flowers small, about 0.6 cm in diameter, yellowish or reddish; sepals 4-7; petals 4-7, mostly 5; stamens 3-7; ovary solitary, sessile, one-celled with a single ovule. Fruit a fleshy drupe, variable in size and shape, 2.5-30 cm long, ovoid, oblong or reniform, green, yellow, reddish or purplish, most commonly orange with a reddish flush.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY                                                                    
Flowers numerous in compound terminal panicles, either staminate or bisexual, fruit setting more frequently near tip of panicle than on 

Breeding System :
Cross- pollinated, flowers are staminate or bisexual.
Phenology :   

The flowering  occurs during the months of December-August.  
Flowering and fruiting seasons vary with locality.

Pollinators:                                                                               
Cross-pollination is not critical, but insects are needed to transfer pollen. Six to fifteen colonies of bees are recommended per hectare. (Flower panicles range from 200- 3,000 per tree, with 500-10,000 flowers per panicle. Proportion of perfect to male flowers ranges from 1:4 to 2:1, but fruit set is usually low.) Fruit bats are attracted when in flower due to the copious exudation of nectar. Bats probably assist in fertilization of flowers. Numerous other insects are attracted to flowers and probably help in pollination. Flowers open at dusk and are highly scented.

Seed Biology :
Seed solitary, ovoid, flattened or disc-shaped, reticulately rugose often polyembryonic 

Seed Germination:
Polyembryonic varieties may be propagated from seed, but mono-embryonic varieties must be propagated by grafting or budding. Mango seed must be fresh and germinates better if not dried out before planting. Seeds germinate at about 20 days. Seedlings attain about 30 cm in 12 weeks, but seedling trees take a long time to come into bearing.

Climate and Soil:
Ranging from Warm Temperate Moist through Tropical Desert to Wet Forest Life Zones, mango is reported to tolerate annual precipitation of 4.8 to 43.0 dm (mean of 142 cases = 16.4), annual temperature of 15 to 29°C (mean of 110 cases = 25°C, and pH of 4.3 to 8.0 (mean of 39 cases = 6.2). In the tropics, mangoes are found from sea level to 1,200 m, thriving best at about 600 m, in climates with marked seasons and dry weather. Although young trees are killed by frost and need protection, mature trees will tolerate light ground frosts. Trees thrive under hottest conditions. Annual rainfall may vary widely without adverse effects. Dry weather during flowering and early fruiting is desirable. Optimum temperatures for growth between 24-27°C; rainfall 25-250 cm, provided there is an adequate dry season. Mangoes grow under a wide variety of soil conditions, provided soil is well drained, but with a stable water table, not too alkaline, pH 5.5-7.5, of moderate fertility. Over fertile soils may result in excessive growth and diminished fruiting.

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used:
Fruit, Seeds, Kernels, Peel, Leaves, Wood and bark.

Uses:

  • Fruit:
    Mango is cultivated for its fine flavored fruit, eaten fresh out of hand, cut in half, served like cantaloupe and eaten with a spoon, or made into jams, jellies, juice, or preserves. Unripened fruits are sliced and used in chutneys, curries, pickles, salads, and soups. Canned or otherwise preserved, the fruit is good to eat at any stage of growth. Fibrous types yield a pulpy juice used for making mango halva and mango leather. Spray dried and powdered juice used in infant and invalid foods, and reconstituted and drunk as a beverage. A type of cereal flakes has been made from a blend of the dried juice and wheat flour. A dehydrated mango custard powder has been developed in India. The fruits after boiling used as a cooling agent.

    Its fruits are edible and are also have medicinal value.The mango fruit is considered to be the King of fruits. The Mango tree is large with spreading branches, 6-15 m high , though in dense jungle it often attains gigantic sizes of 30 m or more.The fruit is what is technically called a drupe, that is to say it is a large fleshy fruit with a large stone in the middle.Most unripe fruits carry a strong flavour of turpentine which disappears in the ripe fruit .

  • Seeds:
     Seeds may be roasted and ground into flour. The kernel of the seed is roasted or boiled and eaten when food is scarce. Kernels, dried to 10% moisture, are fed to poultry and cattle. The seed fat is used for soap making, and the seed residue after the fat is extracted is used for cattle feed and to enrich the soil. The peel, which constitutes 20% to 25% of the total weight of the fruit, can be used as a source of pectin (1,30,000 ppm, ZMB). Leaves are used in India as green fodder for cattle; the urine of cattle fed on mango leaves is used as a yellow dye; however, prolonged feeding of leaves to the cattle may cause death. The wood is grey or greenish-brown, coarse-textured, medium-strong, hard, durable in water but not in the ground. It is easy to work and finishes well. Preserved wood is used for rafters, joists, window frames, agriculture implements, boats (especially dugouts), plywood, shoe heels, boxes and crates. It is also used for charcoal. The bark yields a yellow dye, and with 16% to 20% tannin is used for tanning hides. The resinous gum from the trunk is used to repair crockery and as a substitute for gum arable.

Harvesting Practices
Grafted trees bear in about 4 years, with a tendency to biennial hearing or one good crop every 3-4 years. Flowering is irregular, but can sometimes be induced by smudge- pot fires. Fruits mature 4-5 months after flowering. Fruits are harvested ripe or unripe depending on utility. Ripen at 21-24°C. Mangoes are commonly shipped in avocado boxes, which hold about 16 Ibs net. Store at 2-4.5°C, with 85-90% relative humidity. Keep 2-3 weeks Average freezing point is 0-5°C. Wrapping green fruits in polyethylene extends the storage life with subsequent normal ripening. Mangoes are available from May into September, with peak in June sometimes with a small crop in December and January; in other areas main crops are November to January with a small crop in June and July; in wet areas, crop from April to June In some areas, triple cropping is possible.

Produce and Products
Products from Raw Mango- Dehydrated slices(Anchur) and Powder.
Preservation of mango slices for pickle and chutney.
