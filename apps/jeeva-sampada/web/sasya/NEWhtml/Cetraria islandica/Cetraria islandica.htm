Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

A leafy, erect to suberect, divaricately branched lichen found growing on ground with mosses and grasses, often associated with Cetraria everniella and Thamnolia vermicularis in alpine regions of Himalaya and Eastern Nepal. The species is quite common in Europe. 

Availability :

Restricted in alpine regions only.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES: The species is polymorphic and distinguished by its brown chestnut colour thallus, with subcanaliculate laciniae having fibrils on the margins. Laciniae are 2-5cm tall and 2-3 mm wide. Pseudocyphellae are present in submarginal area. Apothecia are absent in Indian material.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
The species is listed in Pharmacopoeia Universalis of 1846, purely on the basis of their folkloric medicinal uses. In Sweden, the iceland moss was used for treating diabetes, lung disease and cataract. Even today Cetraria islandica can be purchased in European pharmacies for the preparation of herbal medicines.
Cetraria islandica thalli are made into pastilles and teas. Decoctions of the later are valued for their astringent flavour. The firm of f. Hunziker at Dietikon near Zürich uses 150kg/year of this lichen imported from the Balkan states to manufacture such products. In the past, lichens were used very widely as food in times of hardship. In Norway during the years of bad harvest from 1807 to 1814, dried Iceland moss was used to supplement flour. It was considered a better substitute than bark flour (phloem tissue from elm, pine or birch), which was also used to a great extent. The lichen was soaked in lye (aqueous extract of fresh wood ash) for 24 hrs, which presumably neutralized the lichen acids. It was then dried and blended with grain before being ground into flour. Unfermented flat breads or porridge were usually made from the flour.
In Perfumery: Cetraria prunastri and Pseudoevernia furfuracea are collected in large quantities in Yugoslavia, Southern France and Morocco. The total annual harvest is estimated at 8000-9300 tons /yr. After collection, the lichens are dried, sorted, baled and sent to processing plants in France and Germany. The final extract of essential oils and depside derivatives, a dark oily solution, is shipped to Paris, New York and other centres to be used in compounding in finished retail perfumery. Some of the extracts are added to soap for scenting.
The species probably rates first as human lichen food. In Norway and Sweden, Cetraria islandica powder is mixed with various cereals and mashed potatoes from which an uncommonly healtful bread is prepared. The powder is mixed with flour, making the bread less friable or less subject to Weevil attack.
