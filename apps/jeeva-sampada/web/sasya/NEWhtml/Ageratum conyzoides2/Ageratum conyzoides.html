HABIT & HABITAT:
Annual or perennial herb, erect, 40-80 cm high, Throughout India; ascending to 1500 m in Himalayas. Common weeds on roadsides and in cultivated fields.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:
Annual or perennial herb, erect, 40-80 cm high. Stem terete, pilose. Leaves opposite, ovate to subrhomboid, 4.5-9.5 x 2.5-6.5 cm, acute at apex, usually crenate at margin, 3-nerved from base, sparsely pilose above, glandular punctate beneath. Heads many flowered. Involucral bracts many, 2-seriate, c 3.5 mm long, prominently 2-nerved, scarious margined, glabrous on back. Corolla white or blue; tube glandular. Achenes black, c 1 mm long, short bristly on ribs. Pappus of 5 scales, flattened at base, aristate.

Pharmacology:

Water soluble portion of the alcoholic extracts of the air dried parts of the drug A. conyzoides Linn.  potentiated phenobaritone induced hypnosis in dose of 1 gm/kg. It produced hypotensive response in dog, which was not blocked by pretreatment of animals with propranolol anthisan or atropine. It showed negative inotropic and chronotropic effects on perfused heart of frog at dose of 10.0-40.0 mg. It also showed direct acting vasodilation response on blood vessels of rat and relaxant action on isolated smooth muscle similar to that of papaverine. (Agarwal, 1978).

The methanolic extract of the whole plant has antimicrobial activity (Almagboul et al., 1985).

Oral adminiratration (p.o.) of the water extract (WE, 0.1 to 5 g/kg) to rats and mice induce quietness and reduced the spontaneous motility. The sleeping time induced by sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, i.p.) in mice was not altered by previous treatment with WE (2 g/kg, p.o.). induced by carrageenan or dextran, nor did it reduce the chronic paw edema induced by complete Freund’s adjuvant or formaldehyde in rats. Tail flick response in immersion test and writhing, induced by 0.8% acetic acid in mice were not altered by WE either. In isolated guinea-pig ilea WE (0.4 to 4 mg/ml) did not alter the EC50 values of histamine or acetylcholine, but reduced the maximal response to the agonists by 20 to 50%. WE (0.01 to 10 mg/ml) produced tonic concentrations of the ileal smooth muscle proportional to the doses, reaching a maximum of 75% relatively to the maximum obtained with histamine. Those contration were blocked by diphenhydramine (10nM) and reduced by 32%. Hence it indicated that oral treatment of rodents with A. conyzoides L. neither reduced the inflammatory edema nor did it decrease the reaction to pain stimuli. In vitro the extract presented an unexpected histamine-like activity chasracteristic of a partial agonist. (Yamamoto et al., 1991).  

The crude extract of the whole plant has been reported to be superior to Vaseline gauze as a wound dressing material. (Horie et al., 1993).

The analgesic activity of the leaf extract was detected by hot plate method and by the writhing syndrome induced by acetic acid at the doses of 50 & 100mg/kg. At these doses A. conyzoides causes a precocious ataxia, a sedation and a slight ptosis. Six hours after injection, those effects disappear: a reduction of spontaneous motor activity. (Abena et al, 1993).

Oral administration of nobiletin (10.0-50.0 mg/kg) to male rats inhibited appearance of ethanol-induced gastric haemorrhagic lesions in dose-dependent manner (ED50-8.0 mg/kg) significantly inhibited gastric motor activity. It exhibited concentration-dependent relaxation of contractions induced by acetylcholine, transmural electrical stimulation and histamine in isolated pig ileum. It is suggested that antiulcer effect of nobiletin are primarily due to maintenance of mucosal barrier integrity and inhibition of gastric motor activity and secondarily due to prevention of effects of endogenous acetylcholine and histamine. (Takase et al., 1994)  

Essential oil of Ageratum conyzides has been tested for anti-inflammatroy, analgasic activities in mice and rats. Doses of 3 and 4 ml/kg per os were found to have a significant anti-inflammatory (cotton pellet granuloma) activity. At 3 ml/kg the antipyrietic (brewer’s yeast injection) effect was comparable with that of the reference substance (acetyl salicylate lysine 100 mg/kg per os), whereas the analgesic (tail-flick and writhing test activity) was shown at 2, 3 and 4 ml/kg. The daily oral administration for seven days failed to show gastric toxicity. (Abena et al., 1996).

The water-soluble fraction (W.S.F) has been shown to be a patent and non-specific flocker of smooth muscle contraction, possessing also a myorelaxing activity. (Megalhaes et al, 1997).

The amino acid content of A. conyzoides was found to vary from 0.5-4.0% of the total dry weight. Fourteen amino acids were identified, among which amino-n-butyric acid, aspartic acid and proline were present in almost all pollen samples. The other major amino acids present in free form included arginine, cystine, glutamic acid, glycine, isoleucine, leucine, methionine, ornithine, tryptophan and tyrosine. (Mandal et al., 1998).

An extract obtained from the leaves of Ageratum conyzoides L. changed the electrocardiogram, atrial impulse velocity, and coronary vessels resistance on isolated guinea-pig heart. (Garcia and Carvalho, 1999).

The extract decreased spontaneous motor activity and caused a fall in rectal temperature. In vitro receptor radioligand assay was carried out on the extract to demonstrate its selectivity to a single receptor implicated in the mediation of pain. Results showed that it produced positive results (>50% inhibition) in the bradykinin (B K II) assay but the activity was lost after PVP treatment suggesting that phenolic compounds could be responsible for the initial bioactivity the extracts however, did not produce positive effects in the neurokinin NKI and calcitonin gene related peptide (CGRP) assays (Sampson et al.,  2000).

The effect of the water soluble fraction on smooth muscles (using isolated rat uterus and intestine smooth muscles) conclude that the fraction posses substances which provoke direct relaxing effect on smooth muscles and inhibit contraction induced by several agonists possibly by blocking the entry of calcium and /or inhibiting cyclic AMP phosphodiesterase.  (Silva et al, 2000).

Any other uses:

The allelopathic potential aqueous extract from different organs of A. conyzoides and from its different development stages especially from different habitats was different. (Hu et al., 1997). C.A. 1997, 127, 260180w.

Aqueous root and shoot extract of ageratum conyzoides produced significant allelopathic effect on germination percent, germination index, radicle length and plumule length of rice seed. A. conyzoides was found to be the most noxious weed which significantly retarded all the physiological processes of the rice seeds. The aqueous shoot extract of weed was more inhibitory to the physiological processes of rice seeds in comparison to root extract of the same weed species at the same weed species at the same concentration. (Kalita et al., 1998). B.A. 1999, 106(14), 184928.

The topical application of the oil to the nymphs of Schistocerca gregaria showed high nymphal mortality (91%) (Pari et al .,1998).

The saturated aqueous solution of the isolated and purified precocene I and III have been reported to have significant inhibitory effect on the seedling growth of radish, tomato and ryegrass. (Kong et al., 1998). C.A. 1999, 130, 22899e.

Antibacterial activity of aqueous residues Ageratum conyzoides showed a significant control of the growth of Alkaligens viscolactis, Klebsiella aerogenas, Bacillus cerues and Streptococcus pyogens. (Perumal et al., 1999).

Allelopathic effects of the volatile oils from A. conyzoides on radish, mung bean and ryegrass. (Tao et al., 1999). C.A. 2000, 133, 117488w.

The oil extracted from A. conyzoides extracted acute toxicity on adults of cowpea weebil, callosobruchus maculatus F. (Gbolade et al., 1999). C.A. 2000, 133, 13697x.

The fungitoxicity of crude extracts and essential oils of Ageratum conyzoides on the Didymella bryoniae was verified in vitro by means of germination of spores and mycelial growth. Crude extract of A. conyzoides were more effective in inhibiting the mycelial growth of D. bryonial whereas in the germination of spores. The essential oils of A. conyzoides provided 100% inhibition of the mycelial growth and germination of spores of D. bryoniae. (Fiori et al., 2000). C.A. 2000, 133, 345848u. B.A. 2000, 107(23), 345-982.

Different extract produced significant allelopathic effects of Ageratum conyzoides on germination and seedling vigour of wheat. (Oudhia, 2000). B.A. 108/5, 2001, 66904.

Aqueous plant extract of Ageratum conzyoides showed nematostatic and nematocidial properties against M. incognita juveniles. (Dias et al., 2000). B.A. 108/2001, 22, 346211.

The interference of the oil of Ageratum conyzoides the protein synthesis process of the haemolymph protein of final instar larvae of Helicoverpa armigera. (Padmaja and Rao, 2000). B.A. 108/8, 2001.  

Effects of essential oils from leaves of A.conyzoides showed efficacy on the mortality of the maize grain weevil, sitophilus zeamais. (Bouda et al., 2001) B.A. 2001,108/5, 77577.

The residue contaning allelochemicals in aqueous aceton extract of the plant shoots inhibited the germination rate and the growth of root and shoot of Amaranthus caudatus, Digitaria sanguinali and lactaca saliva. (Kota-Noguchi, 2001). B.A. 108/14, 220479.
