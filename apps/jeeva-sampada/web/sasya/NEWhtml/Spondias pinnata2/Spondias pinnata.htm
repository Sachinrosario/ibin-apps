Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:

An elegant aromatic deciduous tree found wild or cultivated almost throughout India, ascending up to an altitude of c. 1,500 m. in the Himalayas; also found in the Andamans. The wild mango, though widely distributed in deciduous and often dry forests, is nowhere abundant or gregarious in India. it grows in regions where the maximum temperature in shade ranges from 38 to 480 and the absolute minimum from 1 to 160 and the rainfall between 75 and 375 cm. or more. The tree is a light demander but sensitive to frost.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

An elegant aromatic deciduous tree with dense crown. Trunk perfectly straight. Bark smooth, olive-grey; branches spreading in all directions, from erecto-patent above to divaricate below. Leaflets 5-8 pairs, subopposite, elliptic, caudate-acuminate, entire, obscurely crenulate, polished.

 

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Flowers whitish to greenish-white, polygamous, in large terminal panicles. Drupes yellow-green or rich olive-green, mottled with yellow and black, smooth, ovoid, 4-7 cm long. Seeds 1-3, one being perfect, oblong-elliptic.

Breeding system and varietal improvement:

Phenology: Flowers February – April. The plant fruit in about five years. Fruits ripen during October – November.


Pollinators:

Floral and seed biology:

The stone easily germinates during the rainy season. The radicle is apt to dry, if not covered, or may be eaten by birds and insects. The plant is artificially propagated either through seed or from cuttings. Sowing the seeds directly in the field gives better results than transplanting, provided the stones are covered with earth. However, basket raised seedlings transplant better. For artificial regeneration, sowing the seed in September is recommended. When the seedlings are c.8.0 cm., they are transplanted. The plants fruit in about five years. Planting the saplings near water-channels or perennial streams is recommended.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts Used:

Wood used as fuel, though of poor quality. Calorific value: 4,067 cal., 7,321 Btu.

Wood: The sapwood is white or greyish white; the heartwood is roseate-grey to grey. The wood is dull, straight-grained, very coarse-textured, very light to light (sp. gr. 0.33 – 0.48; wt. 336 – 689 kg. /cu. m.) and not strong. The timber seasons without cracking, splitting or warping, but is directly dealt with after conversion.

Wood employed for packing-cases and tea chest and for floats, canoes or boats. Suitable for match-splints, but not for match boxes. Also used for non-ornamental plywood. Wood fairly good for unbleached wood pulp.


Harvesting Practices: It coppices and pollards well.

Processing Techniques:

If air-seasoned, the logs should be converted when green and the sawn stock open-piled under-cover. It can be probably be treated without difficulty.

 

Other uses:

Fruit eaten as a vegetable when green and as a fruit when ripe. A condiment and can be made into chutneys, stews, pickles and jams. Cattle, deer, monkeys and squirrels eat the fruit.

Fruit and bark of medicinal use. Fruit is astringent and antiscorbutic. Bark is aromatic, astringent and refrigerant. Root is considered useful in regulating menstruation.

Leaves are aromatic, acidic and astringent. They are use for flavouring. Their juice is applied in earache.

Flowers are sour and eaten as such, made into curry, or used as flavouring.

Bark is used for tanning.

Tree exudes a gum, used as a demulcent and for the purpose of fumigation.
