Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Toddy palm is an Asian species that grow in the habit range of India to Burma and on the island country of Sri Lanka. It grows from moist flat lands at sea level, to mountainous rain forests up to 1000' in altitude.
The toddy palm is a fast growing feather palm. Large, solitary palm, to about 20m, with a grey trunk, and gracefull, arching bipinnate leaves. They flower from the top down, and once the last fruit on the bottom inflorescence matures, the plant dies. (1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 9)

Distribution: Found allmost all over India but particularly in thehotter parts of India and Malaya, Sikkim, Himalaya and Assam and Southwards to Ceylon and Singapore, it is also reported to be grown in the gardens in Delhi and Amritsar. It is common in the evergreen forests of Konkan and N.Kanara. (9-23)

Availability : 

Status :Wild

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Monocarpic palm exhibiting an erect single trunk, up to 20-30 m tall, bearing ringed scars left by fallen leaves. The trunk reaches its maximum height before bloom. Leaves are 3 to 6 m long, bipinnate, bearing wedge-shaped, marginally toothed leaflets somewhat like a fish tail, their size is 10-20 cm by 9-10 cm, their colour dark green. (1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 9)

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used

Spathe and trunk(pith). (9)

Uses

From the soft and starchy pith of the kittul palm is obtained kittul flour or sago. (9,32,33)

The sap (tapped from the spathe) of this palm is very high in simple sugars. In India and other Asian countries, the palm is tapped for its syrup which is often fermented into an alcoholic beverage called toddy. The syrup is also processed into a granular sugar called jaggery.


Harvesting Practices

The Sago is extracted from the plant when the plant is about 7 years old (before fruiting). The plant is felled and after removing the outer bark, the core is cut into discs, sun-dried and ground into meal. On stirring up the meal with water and settling, the starch seperates out. About 5 lb. Of Sago are obtained from a stem 4 ft. long. The large, ovalish, orange-red seeds from the fruiting plant also yeild annually about the same quantity of starch, and apparently, it is more economical to extract from the seed than from the felled plants.


Produce and Products

  • Sago (starch)- Infant food and Pudding. Porridge, Soups and Savouries, Sago snow, Sago papad, Sago kichri, Payasam, Kesari, Uppuma, Vangibath, Curd bath and Boondi. (32)

  • Neera, palm gur (jaggery), palm sugar and palm candy. (3)

Processing Techniques

For the manufacture of palm sago, the starch is obtained from the pith of the stem. The trees are felled when they grow about 8-10 m high, stripped of leaves ,and cut into small lengths ( 1 m). The outer woody rind is slit lengthwise and the pith removed, pounded, and the starch extracted with water is filtered and allowed to settle in trays, Further purification is done by re-suspending the starch in water, filling and tabling in long troughs. Starch is dried in the sun and the partially moist cake is pressed through a coarse screen whereby pellets are formed. The pellets are given spherical shapes by subjecting them to a circular swinging motion in a hollow hammock-shaped cloth: They are sieved and roasted in shallow metal pans for partially gelatinizing and drying. A single palm is reported to yield 125-330 kg of sago. Manufacture of sago.(32,33)

Other Uses
Use this quick growing (but short lived) palm to provide texture and interest to groupings. Also use it in shrub borders, for quick shade over a patio, or as a lawn specimen. Other products obtained from sweet toddy are arrack, vinegar and food yeast. (15,9)

The nut is acrid, cooling, allay thirst and fatigue, causes " Kapha ", biliousness, flatulence (Ayurveda). A freshly-drawn toddy early in the morning acts as a laxative. The nut is used as an application to the head incase of hemi crania. (12,18)

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

Prospects for sago are particularly bright for some nontraditional uses. Several developing countries have expressed an interest in establishing industries for the bioconversion of sago starch to protein. In some countries sago palm has recently been used by local industries to produce ethanol, which can be added to gasoline to strech petroleum supplies, or, with appropriately designed engines, can be used as a transportation fuel. (35)

Miscellaneous Information

Avoid contact with the red fruit produced by this palm. It contains oxalic acid which is toxic when ingested, and contact with skin may result in severe chemical burns. (5)
