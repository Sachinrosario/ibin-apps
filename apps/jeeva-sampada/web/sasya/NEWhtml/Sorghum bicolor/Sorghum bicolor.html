Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Herbaceous Summer annual, coarse, erect with much variability in growth characteristics; culms solid or sometimes with spaces in pith, 0.6–5 m tall, depending on variety and growing conditions, 5 to over 30 mm in diameter, either dry at maturity or with sweet insipid juice. The branches appear only after the main stem has headed out. The side branches are smaller than the main stalk and they appear much later. Prop roots may grow from culm nodes; bud at each node from which a tiller may grow.

Sweet sorghum is a grass of Old World origin. The name "sweet sorghum" is used to identify varieties of sorghum, Sorghum bicolor (L.) Moench, that are sweet and juicy. It is a native of Africa, a drought-resistant, heat-tolerant member of the grass family. Main center of distribution of cultivated sorghums is in Africa, having been cultivated in Ethiopia for more than 5,000 years; possibly cultivated sorghums were also developed independently in India and China. Now sorghums are widely distributed throughout tropics, subtropics, and warm temperate areas of the world. (1, 2,3,9,17-30)

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

The inflorescence is panicle has many shapes and sizes. The peduncle carrying the inflorescence either straight and erect, or wavy and erect or goose necked. Inflorescence consists of many primary branches and further divides to form laterals of second and third order. Internodes of these are very short and have the appearance of being in whorls. The joints of the rachis bear paired spikelets. One is fertile and sessile, the other one is staminate or sterile and pedicellate. In the last joint of rachis, are found two sterile spikelets grouped with single fertile spikelet. The reproductive phase is indicated by a characteristic swelling of the apex of the stalk. The swollen area is enclosed by the terminal leaf sheath called the 'boot', whose blade, the 'flag', is usually small and inconspicuous. Emergence of the flag is followed by the ushering out of the boot. Next the inflorescence proper makes its appearance. When the head is completely clear, the peduncle further elongates, elevating the head to a height characteristic of the variety. About six days are required for the emergence of the flag, nine days for, the ushering out of the boot, five days for the complete clearance of the head and five days for the elongation of the peduncle. The anthesis in the inflorescence is basipetal. Time required for all the flowers in a panicle to open averages 7 days and maximum blooming is on third or fourth day. Flowers begin to open 24 hours after complete emergence of the panicle from the boot, occasionally before it is fully emerged. In the panicle or the second flower of the uppermost branch opens first. Usually, the flowers in any horizontal plane within the panicle open at the same time. The anthesis proceeds downwards to the base at a rate of two to five cm per day. Of the paired spikelets, the sessile opens first then pedicelled. Blooming takes place mainly during morning hours, the maximum flower opening is between 12 midnight to 2 a.m., and it continues upto 8 am.,or even 10 am. ,in the morning. Cloudy weather delays flowering, Usually the stigmas become visible before the filaments of the anthers elongate. The period during which the flowers remain open ranges from thirty to ninety minutes. The pollen germinates immediately after pollination and its viability is for a short period. The stigma becomes receptive sometimes even before the flowers open and remain receptive for 8 to 16 hours depending upon the weather conditions. Fertilisation takes place 6 to 12 hours after pollination. (1, 2,3,8,17-30)

Seed Biology :

Seed are covered by glumes. And seeds may be white, yellow, red, or brown; panicle with up to 6,000 spikelets. Seeds 25,000 to 61,740/kg; grass sorghum 120,000 to 159,000/kg. (3)

Seedling Germination :

Freshly harvested seed may show dormancy. Hard textured seed may be scarified to improve germination. Seeds germinate best between 20–30°C. (3)

Cultivation:

Season: Date of planting depends on use for which crop is intended. In tropics, sorghums may be planted nearly anytime. The Karif crop is sown during June-July and harvested during October-November. While, rabi crop is planted in October-November and is harvested during february.

Land Selection:

Many different soils are used for the production of sweet sorghum, but a soil that has good physical characteristics and good fertility produces the best yield. In general, loam and sandy loam soils are best for the growth of sweet sorghum for syrup production. Soils high in organic matter are thought to have a detrimental effect on syrup quality. The field should have good surface drainage and the soil should be deep and moderately to well drained. Shallow soils or soils very low in organic matter may be droughty. Ample moisture during the growing season is important for good yields of stalks and juice. Clayey soils usually produce poor stands, poor yields, and poor syrup.

Land preparation: Crop sown on well-prepared, firm, moist seedbed if the plant is transplanted. Otherwise, the land for sorghum is not as thoroughly prepared as for some other crops, unless it is weedy. A fine tilth is not attempted, as a firm and compact condition of the soil in the root range is believed to be favourable. In alluvial soils, the field may be ploughed 2 or 3 times or narrow furrows 7-12 cm, deep may be made with a wooden rod having an iron point.

Fertilization: Generally fertilizer with 30–60 kg/ha P and 60–120 kg/ha K is used. Good responses of N fertilizer up to 200 kg/ha have been obtained. And about 6 tonnes/ha of farm yard manure and compost for dry land and 12 tonnes/ha for irrigated fields.

Seed Rate

In dry-land conditions, seed normally sown in rows 75–100 cm apart at rate of 3–9 kg/ha; higher seed rates used for more humid areas. In good rainfall or under irrigation, seed should be close drilled or broadcast at rate of 20–35 kg/ha.

Spacing: Non-tillering cvs are usually spaced 10–14 cm apart in rows, whereas profuse tillering varieties are spaced 30–45 cm apart.

Sowing: Seed planted to depth of 1.5–5 cm depending on soil texture and moisture.

Weeding: Weed control by chemicals or mechanical means important as crop grows slowly in early stages. Cultivate or harrow once after plant emergence and later as required; usually 1–3 cultivations necessary. Shallow cultivation essential to prevent damage to surface roots. Constant roguing necessary of off-type plants before flowering for both open pollinated and hybrid seed production.

Cropping pattern: Many type of cropping pattern exists in the cultivation of sorghum(jowar). Crops like pearl millet, wheat, and barley, or an early rice or pea crop is rotated once in 2-4 years in dry land condition. While, in Nilgiris and Bangalore, early sorghum is followed in the same year by horse gram or niger on red soil and Bengal gram or coriander on black soils.

Irrigation: In parts of South-India, sorghums are generally irrigated on the day of sowing. Second irrigation is given 3 or 4 days later the next is given 3 weeks later and then the subsequent irrigation given at an interval of 10-15 days. (1, 3,6,9,16)

covered smut (kernel smut or grain smut)

In infected earheads, the individual grains are replaced by greyish to brown smut sori; the head may be affected wholly or only partially. The infection is seed-borne and attacks the seedling, finally reaching the earhead.

Rust

Puccinia purpurea Cooke

The rust affects the crop at all stages of growth, though the incidence is more at the time of heading or later. The lower leaves are first infected and later the infection spreads to other leaves. Sori develop profusely on both surfaces but more on the lower surface. Plants exhibit varying extent of premature drying of the foliage.

Resistant selections have been found in shallus and milos

4

Downy mildew

Sclerospora sorghi (Kulk.)

Infection takes place at the seedling stage and the leaves become pale yellow in colour, covered on both surfaces (usually on the lower surface) by soft, white, downy growth of the conidial stage of the fungus. Ultimately partial or complete shedding of the leaves takes place, resulting in stunted growth.

Infection being seed-borne, seed treatment is helpful in eliminating the oospores sticking to the seeds. Eradication of diseased plants before oospore formation and growing of resistant varieties have been recommended. Incidence of the disease is less in irrigated fields. Poorly drained areas should be avoided.

The disease appears as greyish, velvety spots on the leaves; many of them may be formed contiguously giving rise to strips or patches. Depending on the variety of t. he host, the spots may be red, purple, brown or straw-coloured.

The disease may be controlled by the use of tolerant or resistant varieties, sanitation and proper crop rotation.

Economic Potential and Uses

Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor (L.) Moench) is a rich source of starch that has good physiochemical and pasting characteristics and amylolytic susceptibility. It is considered a preferred stock for making maltodextrin that can act as a fat replacement and can be exploited in many food products without affecting their organoleptic (taste) qualities. (40)

Parts used

grain, Stalks, stems, seed (3)

Uses

Grain sorghum is a staple cereal in hot dry tropics, the threshed grain ground into a wholesome flour and for production of starch.

Stalks used as animal feed.

Stems containing as much as 10% sucrose, used in manufacture of syrup and jaggery.

Seed is used as food, in brewing "kiffir beer". (3)

Good quality Cookies and Papaya toffees can be prepared with Sorghum starch maltodextrin. (40)

Harvesting Practices :

For seed, crop is cut by hand or mower, smaller dwarf types combined. If cut by hand, heads dried in heaps on ground or on threshing floor. If entire plant is cut by hand or binder, it should be stooked and left in field to dry and mature for 10–14 days, and then threshed. Seed stored at 12–13% moisture or less. In India harvesting and threshing are still carried out manually or with bullock power. Threshing by running a tractor or with power-operated threshers is practiced on a very limited scale. The harvested grain is sun-dried and storage is conventional.

Chemistry

Average protein content of Kansas grain sorghum was 8.6 percent, average starch content 74.5 percent and average oil content 3.4 percent. In other areas more typical values of 12-14 % protein is found. Sorghum is a starch raw material and the starch extraction process resembles that of corn wet milling. Sorghum starch granules are typically 3 - 27 microns with 16 microns in average.

Per 100 g, the seed is reported to contain 342 calories, 12.0 g H2O, 10.0 g protein, 3.7 g fat, 72.7 g total carbohydrate, 2.2 g fiber, 1.5 g ash, 22 mg Ca, 242 mg P, 3.8 mg Fe, 8 mg Na, 44 mg K, 0 mg b-carotene equivalent, 0.33 mg thiamine, 0.18 mg riboflavin, 3.90 mg niacin, and 0 mg ascorbic acid.

Seeds contain butyric-, formic-, myristic-, palmitic-, and stearic-acids, maltose, emulsine, and are rather rich in vitamin B.

Cultivars with highly pigmented seeds are rich in condensed catechin tannin and other phenols (anthocyanins); these are usually the darker bird-, insect-, and/or fungus-resistant cvs. (Morton, 1981)

Plants yield 27.8% pentosan, valued in furfural manufacture.

Culms may contain 3 ppm ascorbic acid.

Processing Techniques:

The cereal starches, such as maize, wheat, rice and sorghum, are recovered by several processes, of which the wet-milling is by far the most important. Other processes commercially employed in the manufacture of unmodified starches are the alka1i.

In all the above processes, starch is recovered in five stages: (i) softening or steeping of the grains, after thorough washing in water, (ii) glinding and sieving of the steeped grains, (iii) tabling or centrifugation of the ground mass, (iv) dewatering and drying of the final products, and (v) the recovery of important byproducts.


Wet-milling process-This process is universally adopted for the manufacture of all types of cereal starches. It makes possible a complete separation of the major components of the cereal kernels.


The raw material comes to the wet-milling plants in the form of shelled grains. The grains are screened to remove all large and small pieces of grains, chaff, sand and other foreign matter.


The screened grains are steeped in steeping conical staves in which water and sulphur dioxide are sent in. Sulphur dioxide helps in the prevention of the growth of putrefactive organisms, dispersion of proteins and in maximizing the recovery of starch. During steeping, washed water from a previous operation, concentrated to attain a soluble level of 0.10-0.20 per cent, is sent counter-currently through the steeping staves. The steep water coming out of the staves contains 40-80 g/l of total solubles and is sent to concentration plants for preparing animal feeds, mixed feeds, or fermentation nutrients.


After steeping, the grains are crushed in attrition mills and the pulverized grains are sent through a set of hydroclones under pressure. The heavier endosperm and fiber particles pass out of the bottom of the hydroclones, while the lighter germ is drawn off the top of the vortex.


The underflow from the hydroclones is sized in screens and ground in Buhrstone or Entroleter impact mills. The pulverized material is passed through screen pumps where fibers are removed as continuous mats which are washed with water, slurried, squeezed and dried. The de-fibered pulp, known as mill starch, is concentrated in centrifuges where protein is removed as a overflow and starch is obtained from the underflow. The starch stream is passed through a series of hydroclones to reduce the protein content to the minimum level of 0.3 per cent. In the last hydroclone, the starch is washed with softened fresh water. The starch milk from the cyclones is dewatered on moving belt filters or in basket centrifuges, and dried in flash driers.


Alkali process- In this process, the proteins in the cereal grains are dispersed by an aqueous solution of caustic soda or ammonium hydroxide. All cereal starches can be manufactured by this process. The cereal grains are crushed and sieved, the flour is slurried with an aqueous solution of ammonium hydroxide, or the grains themselves are directly soaked in a dilute aqueous solution of caustic soda in large vats. The alkaline slurry is screened in vibrating screens, the underflow is fed into a series of centrifuges where the separation of starch from the proteins occurs. The starch milk from the centrifuges is washed with water, filtered in vacuum filters and tunnel-dried. The protein solution from the centrifuges is dried in spray driers.


MODIFIED STARCHES

Natural starches are the basic source material for the manufacture of all types of modified starches. The modified starches may be classified into innumerable groups, such as thick boiling starches and thin boiling starches; or acetylated, esterified, etherified and hydrolyzed starches; or textile, laundry and paper starches and so on. The modified starches are categorized here into (i) dextrins, (ii) acid-modified starches, (iii) oxidized starches, (iv) pregelatinized starches, and (v) starch derivatives.


Dextrins

Known popularly as starch gum, white dextrins, canary dextrins, or the British gum, the dextrin is an intermediate colloidal substance formed during the hydrolysis of starch. It is marketed as a yellow or a ; white powder, or as granulated particles, or as thick brown coloured liquids, or as a white, viscous paste. Dextrins are prepared from unmodified starches and are named after the starch from which they are derived, e.g. Barley dextrin.

The dextrins are commercially prepared by two methods, viz. dry process and wet process. In the dry process, raw starch is directly heated to 200-250°C In steam, oil, or fuel heated roasters. During dextrinization, the colour of the original starch changes to yellow and brown and finally the entire mass is charred. At 150-80°C, a series of low viscosity canary dextrins are obtained, and at above 195°C a series of British gums are produced. In some manufacturing plants, small quantities of hydrochloric acid are added to the raw starch for bringing down the temperatures of dextrinization. In the wet process, dextrins are produced by acid hydrolysis, enzymic action, or by the action of Bacillus macerans on the raw starch. In actual operation, an aqueous suspension or a paste of starch is heated with a trace of acid or an enzyme and after sufficient degree of conversion is accomplished the excess acid is neutralized, the mixture filtered and the product dried. Only hydrochloric acid, nitric acid and formic acid are commercially used in this process. The roasted or the dried product is milled, sieved and packed.


Acid-modified starches

These starches are prepared by treating an aqueous suspension of starch with a dilute solution of an acid at subgelatinizing temperatures (50-55°C). The acids used for the hydrolysis of starch are hydrochloric, nitric, sulphuric, formic, acetic, propionic, and phosphoric acids. In modern industrial practices, it is usual to employ a mixture of hydrochloric and sulphuric acids. The raw Starch is treated with the acid until the desired viscosity change is obtained. The resulting slurry is then neutralized with sodium carbonate, and the acid modified starch is recovered by filtration, washing and drying.


Oxidized starches

These starches are produced by treating raw starch with oxidizing agents such as sodium hypochlorite, periodate, nitrogen dioxide, chlorine, potassium permanganate, potassium dichromate, and ozone. Commercial oxidized starches have been produced mostly from sodium hypochlorite-starch reaction, or to some extent by periodate or nitrogen dioxide-starch reaction. The effect of the other oxidizing agents on starch has been studied only at the laboratory levels.


Hypochlorite oxidized starches

A slurry of refined starch at a concentration of 20-24° Be is introduced into a reaction tank equipped with impellers for mixing the starch with chemicals. A dilute solution of sodium hypochlorite is let into the tank until the reacting solution has a pH of 8-10. As the reaction proceeds, the value of pH goes down and more quantities of the hypochlorite solution are added to maintain the original level of pH. The temperature of reaction is maintained in the range of 21-38°C. The reaction tanks are provided with a mechanical cooling device since the reaction is exothermic. When the desired amount of oxidation has been achieved, the oxidized starch is neutralized or made slightly acidic (pH 6-6.5). A solution of sodium bi-sulphite or sulphur dioxide gas is added to the treated starch to remove free chlorine present in the reaction system. The oxidized starch is finally washed in continuous vacuum filters and dried in hot air driers.


Periodate oxidized starches

These starches, known commercially as dialdehyde starches, are produced by the oxidation of the reducing and non-reducing endgroups of the starch by periodic acid. Periodic acid is manufactured by electrolysing an aqueous solution of iodine in caustic soda in electrolytic cells containing iron cathodes and lead dioxide coated lead anodes. The periodic acid is reacted with starch in polyethylene, or PVC, or glass-lined reactors. After the desired level of oxidation is achieved, the oxidized starch is washed with water, centrifuged and dried. The spent oxidants from the centrifuges are again recycled into the electrolytic cells, generating the periodic acid.


Nitrogen dioxide oxidized starches

These are prepared by reacting raw starches with liquid nitrogen dioxide in carbon tetrachloride at room temperature, or with the vapours of nitrogen dioxide, or with a mixture of sodium nitrite and nitric acid. Low temperature oxidation with a high concentration of nitric acid promotes carbonyl group formation, while the exactly opposite conditions give rise to non-uronyl carboxyl group formation.


Pregelatinized starches

This group of modified starches includes a variety of starches obtained by precooking natural, modified or derivated starches on hot rolls with or without the addition of chemical reagents. The starches have the common property of marked swelling on contact with cold water, and form colloidal dispersions reflecting the characteristics of the raw materials from which they are

derived.


Starch derivatives .

The term starch derivatives is applied to compounds evolved by the reaction of starch with other chemicals. The derivatives of starch outlined in this section include acetates, phosphates, carbamates and sulphonates of starch, hydroxyethyl starch, other types of esters and ethers, cationic starches, and cross-linked starches.

Other Uses

Sorghum is knownto have varied medicinal uses, it is reported to be antiabortive, cyanogenetic, demulcent, diuretic, emollient, intoxicant, and poison, sorghum is a folk remedy for cancer, epilepsy, flux, and stomachache. The root is used for malaria in southern Rhodesia; the seed has been used for breast disease and diarrhea; the stem for tubercular swellings. In India, the plant is considered anthelminthic and insecticidal, and in South Africa, in combination with Erigeron canadense L., it is used for eczema. In China, where the seeds are used to make alcohol, the seed husk is braised in brown sugar with a little water and applied to the chest of measles patients. The stomachic seeds are considered beneficial in fluxes. Curacao natives drink the leaf decoction for measles, grinding the seeds with those of the calabash tree (Cresentia) for lung ailments. Venezuelans toast and pulverize the seeds for diarrhea. Brazilians decoct the seed for bronchitis, cough and other chest ailments, possibly using the ash for goiter. Arubans poultice hot oil packs of the seeds on the back of those suffering pulmonary congestion.

Miscellaneous Information

Parasites -Sorghum is the host for a number of species of the genus Striga (Scrophulariaceae), of which S. asiatica Kuntze and S. densiflora Benth. are the most important in India. These parasites cause considerable damage to sorghum crops, especially in Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu. Striga spp. produce numerous minute seeds which are disseminated by wind, water, crop refuse, etc. The seeds generally do not germinate unless stimulated by the presence of a host plant. The parasite is totally dependent on the host in the early stages when it is underground and penetrates the roots of the host by papillae from its haustoria. Later, on emergence from the soil, the parasite develops shoots and leaves, but is dependent for water and nutrients on the host during its entire life. Wide spacing, intercultivation and weeding of the parasites before flowering are useful methods of control. Other means of control include spray applications of selective weedicides and growing of resistant. (1)

Sorghum contains hydrocyanic acid and the alkaloid hordenine. Sometimes plants accumulate toxic levels of nitrate. Varieties differ considerably in HCN poisonings. Danger is slight when grain is nearly mature. Young plants and suckers are dangerous, particularly when suffering from drought. HCN is destroyed when fodder is ensiled or cured as hay. In India the intoxication is known as jowar poisoning. Plants may contain 0.07% of the alkaloid hordenine. (3)

How to prevent the cattle from being poisoned ?

  • Avoid the cattle from feeding stunted and green Sorghum plants.

  • Silage the Sorghum plant before being fed to the cattle. (22)
