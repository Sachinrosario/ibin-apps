Spike 2-4 ; Flower: Flower fragrant, peduncles axillary, 1-3 nate; Calyx: campanulate, divided  about 1/3 of the way down, teeth distinct, deltoid ; Corolla:  White, twice as long as calyx, divided about half way down; Androecium: Filaments white anthers yellow; Gynoecium; Fruit: Pods shortly stalked, linear-oblong, thin, flat, pale brown and glabrous when ripe; Seed: 5-6 with lng funicle, slightly dilated at hilum, very much compressed, roundish in outline. Flowering & Fruiting : November- March. Flowers are pollinated by insects. Seeds are dispersed by wind, birds or animals.

Chemistry:

·        The degraded gum of A. senegal is shown to have galactose residues as reducing end-groups. There is no evidence for labile, internal, arabinofuranosyl linkages in the whole gum. It also shows the presence of 6-O-(4-O-methyl-[b]. Glucopyranosyluronic acid)-galactose residues. (Anderson and Stodhart 1966.)

·        Gum Arabic of Acacia Senegal showed the presence of uniform blocks of (1®3)-linked-galactopyranosyl residues in the polysaccharide chain. These blocks are comparable in size to those postulated for many arabinogalactans of simpler structure. (Churms et al., 1983.)

·        Gum Arabic obtained from A. senegal gum consists mainly of a (1®3)-[b]-galactan core with (1®6)-[b]-galacto-pyranosyl branches and with [a]-arabinofuranosyl- (1®3)-[a]-arabinofuranosyl and [a] rhamnopyranosyl - (1®4)-[b] glucopyranosyl uronic acid groups attached to position 3 and 6 respectively of the branch units. (Defaye and Wrong 1986.).

·        The anatomical site of gummosis in A. senegal was demonstrated by comparison of the carbohydrate analysis of the tissues from the inner bark, the cambial zone and the xylem of a gum-producing branch with the corresponding tissues of a branch which did not produce gum. The three anatomical zones were successively extracted for water-soluble polysaccharides and alkaline-extractable hemicelluloses.  The material purified by hexadecyltrimethyl ammonium bromide precipitation was abundant in the aqueous extracts from the inner bark and the cambial zone of the gum producing branch and was absent from the non-gummiferous branch. Neutral sugar and uronic acid analysis showed that the precipitated polysaccharides had the same composition at the gum produced by the same branch. The identity with gum Arabic was further demonstrated by methylation analysis and 13CNMR spectroscopy. The same biochemical analyses showed that the presence of gum in the cambial zone was not restricted to the site of gum exudation but that gum existed on both sides of the scarification on the branch, even beyond the limits of the tapping. (Joseleau and Ullmann 1990).

·        Gum arabic obtained from Acacia Senegal gum showed that it contains arabinogalactan protein (AGP). It contains proteins of molecular mass 30,000-45,000. (Osman et al., 1993).

·        Acacia Senegal gum shows the presence of three molecular mass fractions in the gum namely as arabinogalactan (AG), arabinogalactan protein complex (AGP), and a minor component containing possibly two glycoproteins (GP). (Menzies et al., 1994).

·        Gum Arabic fractionated by anion-exchange chromatography on DEAE-cellulose showed that it contains galactose, arabinose, rhamnose and glucuronic acid. Hyp. and Ser were the principle amino acids. It also contains arobinogalacton proteins (AGP). (Osman et al., 1995).

·        A. Senegal var. Senegal gum contains moisture (10.75%), ash (3.77%), nitrogen (0.328%) specific rotation (-31.3[deg]), pH (4.66), equivalent weight (1436) and total uronic acid content (13.71%). There is a wide variation in intrinsic viscosity and viscosity average molecular weight. (mean values 16.4 and nbsp, ml and nbsp; g-1). (Karamalla et al., 1998).

·         Gum arabic glycoprotein (GAGP) is a large molecular weight, hydroproline-rich arabinogalactan-protein (AGP) component of gum arabic GAGP has a simple, highly based amino acid composition indicating a repetitive polypeptide backbone. Small (~11 residue) repetitive peptide motifs each with three Hyp-arabinoside attachment site the motif Ser-Hyp-Hyp-Thr-Leu-Ser. Hyp-Ser-Hyp-Thr-Hyp-Thr-Hyp-Hyp-Leu-Gly-Pro-His, thus judging by Hyp-glycoside profiles of GAGP. The consensus motif contained six Hyp-arabinoside rather than three and two Hyp-polysaccharides rather than one. This inferred the glycosylation sites based on the Hyp contiguity hypotheris precicted arabinosides on contiguous Hyp residues and aralino galactan polysaccharides on clustered non-contiguous Hyp residues, i.e. the GAGP motif would consist of arabinosylated contiguous Hyp residues i.e. GAGP motif would consist of arabinosylated contiguous Hyp blocks flanking two central Hyp-poly saccharides. So this rigidifies the glycoprotein, enhances the overall symmetry of the glycopeptide motif and explains the remarkable properties of gum arabic. (Goodrum et al., 2000).

·        Heterogeneous polysaccharide gum Arabic consist of 80% of branched arabinogalactan (AG) units and the rest is mainly composed of heterogencous arabinogalactan protein complex (AGP) of high molecular weight. (Picton et al., 2000).

·        A. Senegal gum, consists of 1,3-[b]-galactopyranosyl backbone. There are side-chains of 1,3-[b]-galactopyranosyl oligosaccharides attached to position six of the galactan main chain. (Leon de Pinto, 2001).

·        Leaves and leaf stalks of A. Senegal contains low level of ytterbium precipitated phenolics (Ybppt) (mean = 47g/kg DM) and proanthocyanidins (A550 =0.040 au/g DM). It also has low levels of total phenolics (A 675 = 0.370 au/g DM). (Dube et al., 2001).

Pharmaceutical and Toxicology:

·        Gum Arabic (Acacia Senegal (L.) Willd.) are permitted for pharmaceutical and food use by international regulatory authorities. (Anderson and Weiping 1990).

·        Methanol extracts from the bark and wood of Acacia senegal show growth inhibitory effect on the microorganisms like cariogenic bacteria, Streptococcus mutans, Actinomyces viscosus and a yest Candida albican. Bark extract posses antimicrobial activity while wood extract are inactive. It is therefore advisabletc use for toothbrushing, unpeeled rather than peeled chewing sticks in order to exploit their fully antimicrobal properties. (Khan et al., 2000, 38(3), 235-240).
