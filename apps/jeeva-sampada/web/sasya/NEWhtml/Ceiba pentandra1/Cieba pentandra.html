Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
The genus comprises about 16 species of large decidious trees occur wild in tropical America. The plant is widely distributed in the hotter parts of westren and southren India, Andaman Islands, Burma, Srilanka, Malaya, Java and other south east Asian countries. Kapok is a tropical tree and thrives best at elevations below 1,500 ft. It will grow under a wide range of conditions, but for high production requires abundant rainfall during the vegetative period and a drier period for flowering and fruiting, with an annual rainfall of 50-60 in. per annum and temperatures from 20 to 27°C. Can grow on a variety of soils, from sand to clay soils provided they are well drained. Prefers alluvial soils, slightly acidic to neutral. For best results it should be planted on good deep permeable soils with freedom from waterlogging. Exposed situations should be avoided as the tree is easily damaged by high winds (1).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A deciduous, fast-growing tree, tall tree 25-70 m with a diameter of 100-300 cm, shallow rooted. Branches dimorphic. Trunk tapering, wide near ground; widely buttressed in some forms, with or without short conical sharp spines on trunk and branches. Crown thin; branches whorled, whorls usually of 3 branches, horizontal, giving pagoda-form, or ascending in some forms. Leaves alternate, glabrous, crowded at the ends of twigs, stipules small, lanceolate, falling early, petiole 8-20 cm long with pulvinus at both the ends. Lamina digitate with 5-11 leaflets, shortly stalked, lanceolate, acuminate, entire or slightly toothed, curved back and drooping, glaucous beneath, 8-16 x 2-4 cm. Fruits ellipsoidal leathery pendulous capsules, usually tapering at both ends, 7.5-30 x 3-7.5 cm, turning brown when ripe, dehiscing by 5 valves or indehiscent (1, 2, 3, 4).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Flowers in axillary fascicles, usually many-flowered, hermaphroditic, whitish, large. Pedicels 2.5-4 cm long, articulate at top, calyx 1-1.5 cm long, 5-lobed, glabrous; petals united at base, 2-3 cm long, obovate, usually dirty white in colour with foetid milky smell, glabrous within, densely silky, staminal column united at base, dividing into 5 branches, 3-5 cm long, each with 2-3 one celled convoluted anthers, ovary 5-celled, style constricted at base with a dilation above top of staminal tube, then becoming oblique, obscureiy 5-lobed (1, 2, 3, 4).

Breeding System :
Kapok is a Cross pollinated crop. Sometimes seeds may be set by self-pollination when the stigmas and stamens of adjacent flowers come into contact.

Back to Top

 

Phenology :
The tree flowers in December-January and the fruits ripens in March-April (4, 5).

Pollinators:
The flowers are pollinated by birds, bats and bees, when they visit the flowers mainly for nectar.

Seed Biology :
The seeds are probably orthodox. They contain large amounts of oil that tend to go rancid quickly and the viability diminishes rapidly. When the seeds are stored at 10-12 % moisture content in hermetically closed plastic bags at 15°C, they retain viability for 5-6 months. Seeds many, dark brown, obovoid, 4-6 mm in diameter, embedded in copious white, pale yellow or grey floss. There are 10,000-45,000 seeds/kg depending on provenance (1, 4).

Seedling Germination :
Germination percentage of the freshly harvested seeds is 90-95%. Seed for sowing should be obtained from mature, full-sized pods from high-yielding trees. They may be planted either in nursery beds at 12 x 9 inches or by direct seeding in the field. Germination is fairly rapid (2, 4).

Cultivation:

In India, no effort has been made to cultivate this plant on plantation scale. Kapok is usually propagated by seeds when grown on plantation scale. If sown in a nursery, the seedlings are transplanted in the field at 8-10 months old. It is also easily propagated by means of cuttings, 2-3 inches in diameter and 4-6 ft long of 2-3 year old. The trees comes to bearing in 4th or 5th year and the trees continue bearing for 60 years or more (1, 2).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

Tonica niviferana Walker a shoot-borer, bores through the young shoots, often killing the tree. A granular insecticide, Sevidol (6 g/plant) has also been recommended to control this shoot-borer. Batocera rufomaculata also bores the heart wood, sometimes killing the tree. Diapromorpha melanopus, Desmidophorus hebes, Hypomeces squamosa, Myllocerus lineaticollis, Lagria sp, Adoretus bimarginatus, Apogonia ferruginea, Hyposidra successaria are some of the common defoliators. The beetle Alcides affaber bores the top shoots which die above the wound; it lays eggs in these holes and the larvae tunnel down the pith for several centimetres. The larvae of Petasobathra sirina bore the shoot, the larvae of Aleurolobus simulus and Tenaphalara elongata feed upon the sap of leaves (1).

Diseases:

The silk cotton tree is subject to fungal diseases, such as root-rot caused by Ganoderma lucidum. Cladotrichum folicola and Phyllosticta bombacis are reported to cause leaf-spot, and a new species of Dicellomyces causes curling, wrinkling, necrosis and premature dropping of leaves. Species of Ceratostomella and Graphium, mould and stain fungi cause blue stain on the freshly cut logs and planks. Quick seasoning after conversion and treating with 2 per cent sodium pentachlorophenate can prevent the incidence of these diseases. Several wood-destroying fungi such as Trametes corrngata, T. meyenii, T persoonii, Lentinus sajorcaju and Polyporus friabilis, causing white spongy rot. Polystictus hirsutus causing spongy sap-rot. Schizophyllum commune causing mottled sap-rot and Pleurotus flabellatus causing white sap-rot have also been reported on silkcotton (1).

Economic Potential and Uses

Seeds yield 20-25% semi-drying oil. The unsaturation in kapok seed oil makes it ideal as edible oil and margarine for health conscious consumers and as semi-drying oil it is used in paint and varnish industries. Oil is used same way as that of cottonseed oil. The low freezing point of kapok seed oil (0oC) makes it suitable for use in salad oil. Kapok seed oil is readily saponifiable and used widely for lubrication and soap making. In addition, kapok seed oil is also polymerizable and this can be made into factice which is an additive in rubber compounding. Oil used in the manufacture of margarine and for protective coverings. To lesser extent it is used in the packing of fish and cured meats. The cake left after oil expression is a protein rich feed for ruminent livestock (1, 4, 6, 7).

Uses

Kapok finds use in bedding and upholstery industries, in the construction of thermally insulated and sound proof covers and walls. As a filling material it has the advantage over all the other stuffing materials in being light and resilient. It is used for stuffing cushions, pillows and mattresses. It does not get matted under pressure and regains its original volume after the pressure is removed. As kapok can be dry sterilised, it is used as a filling material for mattresses in hospitals. It constitutes an excellent material for making padded surgical dressings. During the War, kapok was employed for insulating tanks, for lining aviation suits, for filling floats of army assault-bridges, and generally for replacing cork wherever lightness, moisture resistance and floating power were needed. It has been produced in sheet, quilt and padding forms suitable for thermal and acoustic insulation. It is now employed in the insulation of refrigerators, cold storage plants, offices, theatres, motor cars and aeroplanes and also for sound insulation in aeroplane cabins, broadcasting studios, hospitals, auditoriums. It is also employed as a packing for water pipes exposed to frost. The wood is light and is suitable for making canoes, toys and matches. The tree is an important source of honey and also suitable for soil erosion control and watershed protection. In agroforestry it is grown with coffee, cocao (1, 2, 6).

Harvesting Practices
When the fruits have turned dark brown, they can be collected from the ground or cut from the tree using hooked knives. 600-900 fruits per tree has been reported. The fruits are left on sieves or in boxes to dry in the sun 3-4 hours every day for 2-3 days until they open. The seeds are removed by beating with sticks or by machine (1, 4).

Produce and Products

Seeds: Oil is extracted from the seed which is used as

-Salad and cooking oil

-Shortenings and margarine

-Packing of fish and cured meat

-Manufacture of soaps

Oil cake is used as livestock feed. Kapok of commerce is used as filling material in bedding and upholstery industries.

Other Uses
Seeds, leaves and bark from the kapok tree are used for dysentery, fevers, venereal diseases, asthma, menstruation bleedings and kidney diseases. The tree yields a dark red, almost opaque gum. It is astringent and is given in certain stages of bowel complaints. The root is diuretic and the young leaves are used as emollient (1, 8).
