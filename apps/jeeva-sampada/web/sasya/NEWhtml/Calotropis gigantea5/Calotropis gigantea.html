 Other Biological Details
HABIT & HABITAT


A shrub or small tree, common through out  India. Native of Pakistan and Nepal through India to Sri Lanka, the Maldieve Islands, Southern China and Malaysia.  In India, it is common on a variety of soils almost throughout the country up to an altitude
of 1000  m from Punjab and Rajasthan to West Bengal and Assam southwards through peninsular India.  It occurs in open habitats such as sandy riverbanks, cultivated fields, roadsides, grazing lands and other disturbed or degraded sites throughout the drier parts of its range.

Altitude: Up to 1 300 m, Mean annual rainfall: 300-400 mm

Soil type: Prefers disturbed sandy soils.

Calotropis gigantea is drought-resistant, salt-tolerant to a relatively high degree, and through its wind and animal dispersed seeds, it quickly becomes established as a weed along degraded roadsides, lagoon edges and in overgrazed native pastures. Has a preference for and is often dominant in areas of abandoned cultivation especially sandy soils in areas of low rainfall; assumed to be an indicator of over-cultivation

Availability :  
An erect, spreading, perennial shrub, chiefly frequenting waste lands.  It ascends to 3000ft on the Himalaya and extends from the Punjab to South India, Assam, Ceylon and Singapore and is distributed to the Malay Islands and South China.  It is not very common in Burma and as represented by the doubtfully distinct species, C. Procera, it is distributed to Persia and tropical Africa. Found in East and Hinterindien, Malayi archipelago, southern China.
 
Seen growing every where in Chennai. Survives even in xerophytic localities.
Excepting high altitude areas, the plant is of common occurrence all over India. The plant finds mention in Sangam Literature, Kalidasa’s Shankutalam and in Panchatantra.

Native: Afghanistan, Algeria, Burkina Faso, Cameroon, Chad, Cote d'Ivoire, Democratic Republic of Congo, Egypt, Eritrea, Ethiopia, Gambia, Ghana, Guinea-Bissau, India, Iran, Iraq, Israel, Kenya, Kuwait, Lebanon, Libyan Arab Jamahiriya, Mali, Mauritania, Morocco, Mozambique, Myanmar, Nepal, Niger, Nigeria, Oman, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, Senegal, Sierra Leone, Somalia, Sudan, Syrian Arab Republic, Tanzania, Thailand, Uganda, United Arab Emirates, Vietnam, Yemen, Republic of, Zimbabwe

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A large, hard much-branched milky shrub or small tree 8-10 ft. high, very pale in colour bearing unscented pale purple or white flowers with spreading corolla lobes. Bark thick, light yellow or ash-gray soft, deeply fissured. The branches, leaves and inflorescence covered with loose soft  white wool; Leaves opposite, sub sessile, ovate, cordate at base; Flowers beautiful lilac, rosy or purple tinted in umbellate lateral cymes. Fruits fleshy follicle, green, numerous seeds with abundant white coma.

Medium sized to large shrub; all parts of the plant contain milky latex and soft white tomentum. Lilac coloured flowers are found arranged in an inflorescence, which is an umbellate cyme. These are borne on the axils of leaves. The flower with its characteristic staminal corona is one of the key to identifying this plant. The fruit is a follicle and when dry seed dispersal is by wind.  Follicles recurved, 2 or 1 follicled, second more often suppressed, 3-4" long. The seeds with a parachute of hairs, is a delight for small children, who like to blow it and watch it float in the air.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Breeding System :
Highly cross-pollinated through insects such as monarch butterflies. Progeny genetically both divergent and different from its parents (chromosome number 2n = 22). Both animals and wind disperse seeds.

Phenology :
Flowers almost through out the year but most commonly from November to March in Central India. Fruits mature mainly between February and August.

Although its blooms can be seen all through the year, its flowers are particularly abundant during Ganesh puja season; in earlier days it  was the white flowered species that was used for this puja and other auspicious occasions; but it is no longer common.

Back to Top

Pollinators:

Seed Biology :
seeds numerous, flat obovate, 6 x 5 mm, with silky white pappus 3 cm or more long.

Seed storage behaviour is orthodox.


Seedling Germination :
The plant is propagated by seeds, it grows well under dry condition and requires little care.

The tree seeds freely, and natural regeneration is common. Vegetative propagation through half stumps assumes a special importance as compared with the entire stumps because they help in faster multiplication of the parent genotype with plus characters, as each plant gives rise to 2 half stumps. Stumps also help in propagating only 1 plant. Vegetative propagation through stem and root cuttings is very useful in large-scale multiplication of the superior genotypes.

Cultivation:

C. procera has been cultivated in South America and on the Caribbean Islands for the production of fibres at a spacing of 1-1.5 m. When cultivated, annual yields of up to 500 kg/ha are expected. A single harvest per season is preferable to a double (or triple) harvest; a single harvest would result in a net saving of energy input both on the farm and in the processing plant. Well suited for intensive energy farming in arid or semi-arid regions where frost is not a limiting factor.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests: 

Susceptibility to pests is relatively high. Aphids, grasshoppers and other insect pests eat or suck the leaves despite the toxic latex within.

This plant plays host to a variety of insects. The butterfly ‘ plain tiger’. Breeds here; its caterpillars feed on the leaves, pupate and metamorphose into adults. So too with another butterfly called ‘common crow’.  The shiny blue bee Xylocopa can also be seen visiting this flower. Besides a host of weevils, Hemipterans [bugs] live on and off the plant.
The caterpillar of the plain tiger butterfly, Danaus chrysippus, feeds on C. procera, which is common all along the Tihama coastal plains of Saudi Arabia. The milkweed food plant contains cardiac toxins and these are transferred to the tissue of the adult butterfly. These toxins afford excellent protection against attack from vertebrate predators, such as birds and lizards.

Parts used

Uses

A yellow bitter resin; a black acid resin; Madaralbum, a crystalline colourless substance; Madarfluavil, an ambercoloured viscid substance; and caoutchouc, and a peculiar principle which gelatinizes on being heated, called Mudarine are obtained.   It has long been used in India for abortive and suicidal purposes. Madar root-bark is very largely used there as a treatment for elephantiasis and leprosy, and is efficacious in cases of chronic eczema, also for diarrohea and dysentery
The elastic gum resin sap after sun drying yields a form of Gutta-percha; it is also used as a Tan and Dye; a Manna is said to exude from the plant;

  • The milky sap is well known in tanning.  It is made into a paste with the flour of the small millet( Penicillaria spicata), and is used previously to colouring the skin with lac dye.  Alone, it imparts yellow colour to the skin and destroys the offensive smell of fresh leather.  Tanners use the juice mixed with salt to remove the hair from the skin.  The natives are said to adulterate safflower with the powdered flour of the root. 
  • Medicinally, it was recommended for skin diseases, ringworm of the scalp and to destroy piles.  Mixed with honey, it is viewed as useful in the treatment of aphtha of the mouth and a piece of cotton-wool dipped in the juice and inserted into a hollow tooth is reported to cure toothache.  Strongly recommended in leprosy, hepatic and splenic enlargement, dropsy and worms.  The milk itself is a favourite application to painful joints, swellings; the fresh leaves also slightly roasted, are used for the same purpose.
  • A kind of latex is present in all parts of the plant. Used in tanning industry for deodouring, removing hair and imparting a yellow colour to hides.
  • Tannin or dyestuff: A macerated bark extract can be used for dehairing hides and tanning. Additional minor uses includes dyes.

 


Harvesting Practices
The sap or juice is collected by incision.  An open slit is made on the back of the plant and a pot tied to it, when the juice will flow into the pot; or it may be collected by cutting the bark and catching as much as flows out at once.  The juice extracted may either be exposed to the sun in a shallow vessel or left to dry in the shade; by the former process, the substance becomes little darker than by the latter.  When it has attained a tough consistency, it may be well worked up in very hot water with a wooden kneader or boiled; either process serves to remove an acrid property of the juice, as also all other matter but the gutta-percha itself.  It is believed that the more it is boiled and worked up, the harder it will eventually become when cool.

Chemistry
The chief obstacle to Calotropis gutta percha lies in its being a god conductor of electricity and therefore unfitted for cable purposes, otherwise it would at once assume commercial importance.

It coagulates by heat and again becomes fluid with cold.

The sap contains in addition to caoutchouc, two principles, analogous to alban and fluavil, of gutta-percha, is a point of some interest, as maddar gutta-percha has been recommended as a substitute for the commercial article.

The juice is described as a caustic, a purge for phlegm, depilatory and the most acrid of all milky juices.

Other Uses
  1. Bark yields a fibre which is white, silky, strong and durable, used for making fishing nets and lines, bow-strings and twine.  The dried root bark is a substitute for Ipecacuanha.
  2. Wood yields a light charcoal used in gunpowder and fire works.
  3. Stem, root bark, flowers and leaves are used in medicine.
  4. Powdered flowers in small doses are useful  in the treatment of colds, coughs, asthma and indigestion.
  5. Powdered root bark gives relief in dysentery  and emetic, febrifuge, anthelmintic, depurative, expectorant and laxative, and is useful in cutaneous diseases, intestinal worms, cough, ascites and anasarca. 
  6.  The dried whole plant is a good tonic, expectorant, depurative, and anthelmintic.
  7. Leaves are useful in the treatment of paralysis, arthralgia, swellings and intermittent fevers.
  8. Leaves, roots, flowers and latex are used in Ayurvedic practice, entering into many compound  preparations such as Kaccordi oil, Dhanvantaram ghritam, Vajrakatailam and Nagaradi tailam.  
  9. Among the Kondhs of southwestern Orissa, a paste prepared from the root is applied as a poultice to relieve rheumatic pain.
  10.  In the homeopathic system of medicine, it is reported  to be useful in the treatment  of elephantiasis, lupus and chronic rheumatism.
  11.  The Bhils of Maharashtra reportedly use the root extract in the form of a pill to kill guinea worms; they also use the latex from the plants for local applications to treat ringworm and other skin diseases.
  12. A mixture of the stem bark and black pepper is taken internally by the Paharia and Santhalis of Bihar to treat epilepsy.
  13. Active principles extracted from the roots, leaves and stalk include calotropin which is reportedly effective in fertility control.  
  14. Food: The bark and latex are used in brewing and to curdle milk.
  15. Fodder: Young pods, senescing leaves and flowers are eaten by goats, occasionally by sheep in times of need, and rarely by cattle and other livestock because they are slightly toxic. Nutritional analysis of shade-dried leaves of C. procera shows they contain 94% dry matter, 43% acid detergent fibre, 20% ash, 19% crude protein, 19% neutral detergent fibre, 5% magnesium, 2% oil, 0.59% phosphorus, 0.2% zinc, 0.04% iron and 0.02% calcium.
  16. Fuel: Stems produce a good charcoal, while the stem pith makes good tinder. Produces an effective and sustained smoky fire, suitable for drying fish. Charcoal has been used for gunpowder in India.
  17. Fibre: White, silky, strong, cylindrical, flexible and durable stem fibre used for various purposes, such as for making ropes, to form cheap cots, gunny bags, bow strings, fishing nets, and in the manufacture of paper, pulp and duplicating stencils. The floss from the seeds, which is about 2-3.5 cm long, white silky and strong, is used as an inferior stuffing for mattresses and pillows as well as for weaving into a strong cloth. The floss may also substitute cotton wool for surgical purposes. Strong inner bark fibres produce a binding material and are processed into fabrics.  It is also used to adulterate Indian Kapok.
  18. Timber: Stems are termite proof and used for roofing and building huts. The very light wood can also be used for fishing net floats.
  19. Latex or rubber: The liquid latex of C. procera can be used as a renewable source of hydrocarbons and intermediate energy resources. Latex contains 11-23% of rubber.
  20. Poison: The bark and the latex are widely used as arrow and spear poisons. The latex is cardiotoxic with the active ingredient being calotropin. Latex of C. procera is 80% effective in inhibiting the activity of the tobacco mosaic virus. The leafy branches are said to deter ants.
  21. Medicine: Compounds derived from the plant have been found to have emeto-cathartic and digitalic properties. The principal active medicinals are asclepin and mudarin. Other compounds have been found to have bactericidal and vermicidal properties. The latex contains a proteolytic enzyme called caloptropaine.  An infusion of bark powder is used in the treatment and cure of leprosy and elephantiasis. It is inadvisable to use bark that has been kept for more than a year. The root bark is an emetic, the flower a digestive, and a tonic is used for asthma and catarrh. Bark and wood stimulate lactation in cattle. Roots (extremely poisonous) are applied for snakebite. The milky sap is used as a rubefacient and is also strongly purgative and caustic. The latex is used for treating ringworm, guinea worm blisters, scorpion stings, venereal sores and ophthalmic disorders; also used as a laxative. Its use in India in the treatment of skin diseases has caused severe bullous dermatitis leading sometimes to hypertrophic scars. The local effect of the latex on the conjunctiva is congestion, epiphora and local anaesthesia. The twigs are applied for the preparation of diuretics, stomach tonic and anti-diarrhoetics and for asthma. Also used in abortion, as an anthelmintic, for colic, cough, whooping cough, dysentery, headache, lice treatment, jaundice, sore gums and mouth, toothache, sterility, swellings and ulcers.
  22. Soil improver: A source of green manure. The plant can help improve soil water conditions and also acts as a soil binder.
  23. Pollution control: C. procera is an ideal plant for monitoring sulphur dioxide emissions in the air
  24. Other services: A suitable indicator of exhausted soil.

 Miscellaneous Information

In large doses, the root bark causes nausea, vomiting and diarrhea.  Prolonged higher doses cause headache and leucorrhoea.  The latex exuded from the cut stem, roots, branches and leaves is toxic having a caustic effect on the mucous membranes and may cause secondary dermatitis.  It acts as drastic purgative and emetic.

In Mysore the branches are much sought after as a manure for paddy-fields.

The flowers are used in the worship of Mahadev and Hanuman.  

In Bengal the bluish corona of the flowers are carefully picked from the corolla and strung into garlands which are worn at certain religious ceremonies.  

The leaves are used as patri in the worship of gods.

When a Hindu is to marry a third time, it is believed that the third wife will soon die; in order to avoid such a calamity, the man is first married to this tree, which is then cut down.  This ceremony is believed to ensure the longevity of the fourth, but really the third wife whom he now marries.

Although its blooms can be seen all through the year, its flowers are particularly abundant during Ganesh puja season; in earlier days it  was the white flowered species that was used for this puja and other auspicious occasions; but it is no longer common.
