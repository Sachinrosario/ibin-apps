Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
It is a tall forest tree. Borneo tallow is obtained from a number of Shorea species, namely S. macrophylla, S. stenoptera, and S. robusta. The Shorea spp. grows wild in several parts of Southeast Asia, particularly Indonesia, S. robusta (sal) is widely distributed in North and Central India. It is a semideciduous, gregarious tree seldom quite leafless that grows up to a height of 18-30 m and a girth of about 1.8-2.1 m. The total forest of sal in India is estimated at 11,500 km2.

The main and almost continuous stretch of sal commences from Ambala in Haryana and extends along the sub-Himalayan tract and outer Himalayas as far as Darrang district in Assam and to Tripura. It is also found in the Garo hills, Nowgong, Kamrup, and the Khasi and Jaintia hills of Assam. To the south it is distributed from West Bengal through Orissa upto vishakapatnam in the south and through greater parts of eastern and southern MP up to Chhindwara and Hoshangabad in the west.The tree exhibits adaptability to varied agroclimatic zones. The most favourable soil is well-drained, moist, deep sandy loam. Excess clay in sub soil stunts the growth. It is also found ocassionally on limestone and on alluvial ground. In Central India it occurs on laterite, sandstone, mica schist, gneiss etc (1, 2, 6, 7).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
It is a semideciduous, gregarious tree seldom quite leafless that grows up to a height of 18-30 m and a girth of about 1.8-2.1 m. Bark reddish brown or grey, smooth or longitudinally fissured, leaves 10-30cmX5-18 cm., ovate oblong, coriaceous, shining when mature. The trees have clean and straight bole of 18-24 m. long clear bole has been recorded in 45 cm. Generally sal copices well up to a moderate rise; shoots are usually produced near the ground level. Strongest shoots are produced in stools 25-125 cm in girth. The number of shoots produced per stool varies greatly. As many as 20 shoots may be produced, but most of them are killed out, except for few which may survive up to 20-30 years (1, 6, 7).

The sal flowers, whitish in colour, appear in early summer. These come out in auxiliary racemose or large terminal panicles covered with white pubescence (1). Sal bears a profusion of pea sized fruit with five segments of fruiting calyx enveloping it and forming three big wings and two narrower, smaller segments around the fruit. The wings of the fruit are green when immature and turn brown on further maturing and drying (1, 3, 6, 7). The fruit is ovoid and varies according to species. Reddish to pale yellowish green in colour, indehiscent, one seeded with five, somewhat equal, 5-7cm. Long, wing like persistant sepals, seeds ovoid with fleshy unequal cotyledons (1).

Phenology :
The time of flowering and fruiting in sal varies from locality to locality depending on climatic conditions. High temperature results in early flowering and fruiting and in dry and warm localities the fruits fall early. The low temperatures will delay flowering and fruiting. The flowers open full from the end of February to well on in April and fruits ripen in June/July and fall as soon as they ripen (3, 7).

Seed Biology :
Sound fresh seed has a high percentage of fertility, but its viability is short and seldom lasts more than two weeks. About 450-1000 fruits weigh one kilogram. Each kernel weighs about 4 to 5 g (4).

Seedling Germination :
The seeds germinates soon after falling if moisture conditions are favourable. Germination is hypogeous. The radicle emerges towards the apex of the fruit and descends rapidly, soon forming a taproot. The cotyledonery petioles meanwhile elongate considerably, carrying the plumule free of seed and enabling the young shoot to make its way upwards (5).

Cultivation:

Sal regenerates naturally through seed and coppice. The problem of regeneration of sal forests has been causing considerable difficulty. Reproduction of sal, both natural and artificial, is affected by diverse factors so interdependent and variable that it is difficult to assign reasons for its success or failure in any particular locality. The most important single factor affecting natural regeneration is the time lag between the falling of seed and. the commencement of the monsoon. With timely rains and other conditions being favourable, natural regeneration of sal is good.


Artificial reproduction of sal is now being paid greater attention to than hitherto. Both direct sowing and transplanting have been employed with varying success. In any system, however, it is essential that fresh seed should be collected as soon as it falls to the ground and sown immediately. Line-sowings with field crops or with nurse plants have given satisfactory results in a number of places. Transplanting nursery raised seedlings with balls of earth or in baskets during the first rains or 2-3-year- old plants, after pruning the tap roots to a length of 45 cm. and the stems to about 2.5 cm. from the ground level, as early in the rains as possible, was quite successful in many localities. Air-layering has also been successfully carried out. Growth from cuttings, however, has been reported to be lanky (1).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

The insect pests of sal include a large number of defoliators and borers. Amongst the defoliators, Lymantria mathura causes considerable damage in North India and Maurilia iconic. in West Bengal. Freshly felled trees are usually attacked only after one month and the attack is heavy if the logs are stored in the sun. This indicates that drying accelerates insect attack. Major damage to both sapwood and heartwood is done by the members of the family Cerambycidae, especially Hoplocerambyx spinicornis and Aeolesthes holosericea.Other borers of the wood belong to a number of families, the important amongst them being Anthribidae, Curculionidae, Bostrychidae, Platypodidae and Scolytidae. The wood is also subject to the action of marine borers. Bark is damaged by insects of the families Arbolidae, Coccidae and Termitidae. It is reported that profuse exudation of resin may sometimes be a symptom of an attack by the borer Hoplocerambyx spinicornis. Principal borers causing damage to sal poles have been recorded to belong to the families Bostrychidae (powder post beetles), and Platypodidae and Scolytidae (sap and heart borers or ambrosia beetles). Different control measures have been recommended for certain borer attacks. Some wood boring molluscs have also been recorded on sal timber.Sal plantations in various localities are attacked by loranthaceous parasites, specially Dendrophthoe falcata and Macrosolen cochinchinensis. Eradication of the parasites by lopping the affected parts has been recommended. Lopping must be completed by the end of December when the fruits of the parasites begin to ripen and it is then that the birds eat them and distribute the seeds (1).


Diseases:

Over 150 species of fungi are known to cause damage on sal tree. Many types of rot particularly by species of Daldinia, Fomes, Ganoderma, Hexagonia, Lentinus, Polyporus etc. have been recorded. Great damage is done by fungus. Most important of these is Fomes lignos and Trametes cubensis (brown cubicle rot), Polystictus steinheilianthus (white spongy rot), Fomes lividus (white fibrous rot), Lentinus subnudus (white spongy sap and heart rot) etc. Hymenochaete rubigenosa cause unsoundness of sal. Hypoxylon mediterraneum is frequently associated with dying and dead sal. Fire, frost and suppression are some of the factors, which predispose sal to the attack of these fungi (1).

Economic Potential and Uses

The sal fat can be used as a substitute for cocoa butter in confectionery as a substitute for tallow, for textile sizing, soap, making, cosmetics, medicines and pharmaceuticals. The fat can also be processed to obtain 9,10-dihydroxystearic acid, which has potential uses in products such as waxes, polishes, cosmetics, paper coatings and multipurpose greases. They are also useful as thickeners in puddings and pie fillings. The presence of epoxystearic acid and dihydroxystearic acid in sal inhibits it for use as edible fat. However it is accepted in confectionery, shortening and as a. cooking fat.

A kind of oil is also obtained from sal fruit which is used for burning in earthen lamps. It is also misused for adulterating ghee. Further, during famine poor people grind the fruit into flour and eat it to ward off starvation.

Uses

Sal Starch: Sal seed meal is a potential source for the industrial production of starch. The starch is prepared by alkali extraction, followed by steeping the residual meal in water. Sal starch can be used as a substitute for potato or corn starch as the binder ingredient in pharmaceutical tablets.

Large quantities of sal resin is obtained from the sal tree and it forms one of the important resins of commerce. Resin is used in inferior quality paints, varnishes, burnt in religious ceremonies and in the manufature of shoe polishes, carbon papers, typewriter ribbons (1).

Wood: Sal tree is best known for its wood. The timber is widely used for sleeper railway tracks, as constructional wood for beams, flooring, railway wagons, carts and many other fabrications (2).

Harvesting Practices
They fruit during summer and the seeds ripen from June to July. Collection of sal fruits extends from the time of ripening and falling of fruits to the commencememt of mansoon (3). Sal fruits are collected by hand picking and by sweeping from the forest floor after cleaning the ground. The collected fruits are generally brought to the drying centres where they are spread in sun for few days to dry up. Average seed yield per dominant tree is 25 Kg. Each kernel weighs about 4 to 5 g (4).

Produce and Products

Seeds are eaten, Oil obtained from the seeds finds its use in confectionery, pharmaceuticals etc. Seed cake is used as animal feed. It is an potential source for the production of industrial starch. Wood is used for construction purposes. Resin of commercial value is obtained from the tree.

Chemistry

Utilization of Meal: The defatted meal obtained in solvent extraction contains 7.9-9.6% moisture, 7.9-9.2% proteins, 2.3-3.1% ash, 9.9-12.2% fiber and 54.1-58.4% carbohydrates. The meal proteins are rich in lysine (6.3%) but deficient in methionine (1.1%). Of the carbohydrates, starch (29.6-36%) and free reducing sugars (8.1-8.8%) are the major constituents. Being rich in starch, it is used as animal feed (1, 2, 8, 9, 10).

The oil is greenish white to whitish in colour (1, 11).

Processing Techniques

The fruits are brought to a central place and manually decorticated to obtain clean kernels. This is achieved either by beating the dry fruits with wooden sticks or by employing a controlled fire to dewing the fruits. Only the wings are readily combustible. The pods are then manually removed to obtain kernels. Mechanical decortication is also employed. Almost total decortication can be achieved, with yields of 66% kernels and 33% husks, by a manual or power-operated groundnut desheller of 1 to 10 ton/h capacity. A hand-operated portable decorticator has also been developed for this purpose. The kernels dried to 7-8% moisture can be stored very well without appreciable changes in the oil yield or free fatty acids content. The kernels contain about 45 to 70% fat. However, the kernels of Indian sal (S. robusta) yield only about 14 to 18.5% fat. The local method of extraction yields crude fat with an unpleasant odour. The commercial solvent extraction method using hexane is more efficient and preferable for obtaining sal seed oil. The seeds are flaked before extraction to achieve a higher oil recovery (2, 8, 10).

Other Uses

Sal is one of the primary host of tassar silk worm. It is also recorded as a host of kusumi strain of lac insect. The leaves are considered as medium to poor fodder. Leaves are also used for beedi making and are pinned together and made into platters and cup like articles for serving food. A paste made from the fruits is used in diarrhoea. Sal flowers provide honey (1).
