Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Bauhinia purpurea is a partially deciduous and medium sized tree which grows to a height of 30 to 35 feet and spreads at the same width. It is found in the sub-Himalayan tract ascending up to about 1,500 m elevation, Assam, Khasi hills and western Peninsula. In these regions, the tree is characteristic of mixed deciduous forests occurring on hill slopes, in valleys and along streams. In West Bengal and Assam, it is a characteristic tree of eastern sub montane semi-evergreen forest. The tree is cultivated in many areas for beautiful flowers. The tree is widely distributed in American Samoa, Fiji, Guam, Hawai, Florida (US), Papua New Guinea, Pitcairn Island, Samoa, China, Indonesia, Malaysia, Philippines, Japan, Taiwan, Thailand, Vietnam, Bhutan, Myanmar, Nepal, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Mauritius.

It is a tree of subtropical climate. The absolute maximum shade temperature varies from about 38° to 41°C, the absolute minimum temperature from about -1° to 10°C and the annual rainfall from about 1,000 to 2,150. mm. It grows on a variety of soils, The tree grows in part shade/part sun/full sun and grows in clay; loam; sand; slightly alkaline; acidic; well-drained Drought tolerant, high Aerosol salt tolerant soils (1, 4, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 19, 20, 23).

Availability : Plenty 
Status : Wild (cultivated in gardens for ornamental purpose) (1).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

In Bauhinia purpurea L., the trunk and branches droops as the tree grows. The leaves are 3-6 inch long and their arrangement is alternate, large and heart shaped. Leaf type: simple; Leaf margin: lobed; cleft; Leaf shape: orbiculate; Leaf venation: palmate; Leaf type and persistence: deciduous Leaf blade length: 4.5-11 cm long, 4.5-10 cm wide and Leaf colour: green. Flowers appear before leaves reappear. Flowers range in colour from pink to purple to maroon. Crown uniformity is symmetrical canopy with a regular (or smooth) outline, and individuals have more or less identical crown forms and the Crown shape is round; Crown density is moderate, Texture is coarse (1, 4, 8, 9, 18, 19, 23).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Flowers are large, rosy purple, in few-flowered terminal, brown-tomentose panicles, pedicels 5-13 mm. Long, stout, tomentose; bracts and bracteoles are small, tomentose, deltoid. Calyx is tomentose, tube is 7.5 to 10 mm. Long, limb twice as long as the tube, usually splitting into 2 reflexed segments, 1 emarginate, the other 3-toothed. Petals 3.8 to 5 cm. Long, oblanceolate, long-clawed, spreading, veined. Stamens usually 3 fertile, the others reduced to antherless filaments. Ovary downy, long-stalked; style long; stigma large, oblique. Fruit, dark pod to 30cm long, 2cm wide containing 12 to 16 seeds 15mm in diameter 1 - 2mm thick (1, 4, 5, 9, 19, 20).

Breeding System :

Bauhinia purpurea L. are self compatible and breed through autogamy, geitonogamy and xenogamy (6, 21).

Phenology :

Bauhinia purpurea flowers in early September to December. The pods ripen from January to March (1, 6, 19, 23).

Pollinators:

The flowers are pollinated by Hummingbirds and Butterflies (6).

Seed Biology :

Ripe pods are about 15-30 cm long and each contains about 10-15 seeds, suborbicular, flattened, 1.3 cm. in diameter, dark brown colour and smooth. About 4,500 seeds weigh one kilogram. The seeds keep well seeds kept for 14 months showed a fertility of 100 per cent (1, 19, 20, 23).

Seedling Germination :

The germination is epigeous, but shows a transition between hypogeous and epigeous form. The radicle emerges, while the plumule commences to grow and the young shoot to develop before emerging from between the cotyledons, the latter seperate very slightly and the young shoot extricates itself by arching or bending as in hypogenous germination, but after its emergence the cotyledons, which become green, seperate and are carried above ground on a short hypocotyl. They germinate readily and have high percentage of fertility (17, 20, 23).

Cultivation:

The tree is capable of growing under varying light and soil moisture conditions. It is classed as a moderate light demander and frost hardy tree. The plant is propagated by Seeds, grafting, cuttings, and air layerings.

Direct sowing is done in lines about 3 m apart. Seeds are sown about 6 mm deep. About 10-12 kg seed may suffice for sowing one hectare. Regular weeding of the lines and cutting of bushes likely to shade the plants are necessary. Planting of stumps prepared from one year old nursery raised seedlings also gives good results, though the growth of the plants from the stumps in the first season may be somewhat slow. Stumps prepared from about 15 months old plants with about 1-2.5 cm collar diameter perform well. Sowing in the nursery is done in March-April. Pre-sowing seed treatment hastens germination. The seeds are either put in hot water, allowed, to cool and then soaked for 12 hours or soaked in cold water for 24 hours before sowing; the former is reported to be better than the latter. Sowing is done in lines 25 cm apart. Irrigation and weeding are done as and when necessary. The seedlings appear in about 4 to 10 days and can be transplanted while still of small size during the first rains. The long taproot of the seedlings necessitates utmost care in planting out. Transplanting of about one month old seedlings in containers is advocated. The container grown seedlings are planted out in July. Planting is done in 30 cm3 pits and the containers are removed at the time of planting.

Once the plant is established it can be grown in full sun on well drained soil. Trees are very drought-tolerant and actually flower best on dry soils. Chlorosis can be a problem in alkaline or salty poorly drained soil. Problems include a tendency to show nutritional deficiencies, especially potassium; the weak wood which is susceptible to breakage in storms. Orchid-tree may need occasional pruning to develop a uniform shape when it is young when it is used as a landscapins plant. Branches tend to develop low on the trunk and droop toward the ground forming a large bush if left unpruned. Occasional pruning during the life of the tree will help maintain a nice, neat appearance (1, 6, 9, 11, 17, 20, 23).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

Borers, caterpillars and mites (1, 6).
Diseases:

  • Leaf spot disease.

  • Leaf scorch disease.

  • Clitoria yellow vein tymovirus – symptoms: Mottle, systemic vein chlorosis. Transmission- Virus transmitted by mechanical inoculation; not transmitted by seed.

  • Turnip rosette sobemovirus- symptoms: chlorotic vein-banding and severe stunting, necrosis of veins, leaf distortion and severe stunting ("rosetting"). Transmission- Virus transmitted by mechanical inoculation.

  • Cercospora bauhiniae has been recorded on the leaves (1, 6, 15, 16).

Economic Potential and Uses

Seeds yield 15 Per cent of the non drying oil.

Parts used

Seed, root, bark, stem, leaves and flowers. (1, 3, 10)

Uses (1, 3, 4, 10)

Seed: The seed yeids a non edible seed oil.

Wood: The wood is greyish red, soft and light (wt, 576 kg/m3) and the timber is used in manufacturing agricultural implements and matches. It is also suitable for scantlings and rafters in inferior construction work.

Bark: Bark is used in dyeing and tanning industries. The inner portion of the bark yeilds a fibre.

Flowers: Flower buds are eaten as vegetable and also pickled. They reported to be laxative and anthelmintic. The spectacular flower display makes orchid-tree a favorite for specimen plantings in landscaping. The flowers contain quercetin, iso-quercitrin, astragalin (m p 176 to 1780 C) and pelargonodin-3-glucoside and its triglucoside.

Leaves: The tree is lopped for fodder in Assam, West Bengal, Himachal Pradesh, Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Orissa, Punjab and its leaf fodder is considered to be of medium quality.

Harvesting Practices

The seeds can be harvested when pods are still on the plant itself which has to be dried and the seeds removed from the pods or it can also be picked from the ground during the summer months before they dehisce (20).

Produce and Products

Oil is obtained from the seeds. Fibre is extracted from the bark and it is used in tanning and dyeing industries. Leaves are valued as fodder. Apart from these the plant is known to have with medicinal values.

Processing Techniques

The processing of oilseeds is carried out in bullock-driven wooden ghanis (kohlus) and powder-driven ghanis (rotary mills). The ghani is a familiar feature of the rural landscape contributing to village economy. The ghani (kohlu) is devised on a pestle and mortar rotating system driven by a pair of bullocks. In semi-urban areas, where electricity is available, most of the bullock-driven ghanis have been replaced by motorized rotaries for crushing oilseeds. These rotaries take less space than the ghanis (22).

Other Uses

The root is carminative, but the root bark is poisonous. The stem bark is credited with astringent properties and is used in diarrhoea. A decoction of the bark is used to wash the ulcers (1, 3, 10).
