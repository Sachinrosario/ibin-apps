Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:

A tall, evergreen tree with spreading crown. It has a firm root system and more or less wind-firm. Frost hardy. It grows on variety of geological formations, sometimes nearly in dry localities and seasons. Under favourable conditions, the chir pine attains large dimensions and forms a straight cylindrical bole. Trees of height up to 54.9 m and breast height girth up to 3.4 m have been recorded in favourable localities of Tons valley of Jaunsar and Tehri Garhwal (U.P.).

Within the natural habitat of chir pine, the absolute maximum shade temperature at the lowest elevations is about 460C; in the pure chir zone, however, the absolute maximum shade temperature within most parts of the region of the pine falls below freezing point, and ground frosts often occur in winter. The mean annual temperature lies between 150 and 200C. The normal rainfall varies from about 900 mm to 3,000 mm. In most stations in the neighborhood of the chir zone, the rainfall ranges from 1,000 to 1,800 mm, while in the Tons valley, where the pine reaches its finest development, the rainfall is about 1,000 mm or in places even less.

Requires good drainage. Chir pines occurs on a variety of geological formations. In the outer Himalayas and the Sivaliks, it occurs on tertiary sandstones with occasional bands of clay or beds of conglomerate. In the Himalayan vallyes, considerable stretches of chir forest are found on quartzite; here the trees often attain large dimensions. Other common Himalayan formations on which the tree occurs are mica schist, gneiss and granite, and slates and shales, often with bands of quartzite.

Fires, grazing and browsing, removal of litter, lopping, clearing and cultivation greatly influence the distribution of chir. Fire appears to be an inevitable factor in the development of the tree. By withstanding repeated periodic conflagrations, the tree has evolved a characteristic fire-resisting capacity. It is the most fire-resistant species of its region.

A pronounced light-demander, but in hot situations a certain amount of side shade may be necessary for the young trees. By virtue of its needle-like leaves, long taproot and thick bark, has adapted itself to a habitat subject to intense heat and drought, often aggravated by fire. It is frost hardy, though severe frosts are rare in its natural habitat. Chir is not exposed to any injury by snow in most parts of its zone. However where locally heavy falls of snow do occur, it suffers considerable damage owing to its brittle nature.

 

Availability :

It is found in the outer ranges and principal valleys of the and on the ridges of the Siwalik hills flanking the Himalayas, from about 450 to 2,300 m. Its distribution in India is confined to Jammu and Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh. The total area under chir forest is estimated to be about 890,000 ha. In most parts of the area, it occurs more or less gregariously.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

A tall, evergreen tree with spreading crown. Branches more or less whorled, at regular intervals. Symmetry is lost as the tree matures and the crown becomes round, flat or spreading. Bark deep grey, often reddish, deeply fissured, rough, exfoliating in longitudinally elongated plates.Two types of branches are present: long shoot with unlimited growth; dwarf shoots with limited growth. Long shoots occur on the main stem as lateral buds in the axils of scale leaves, and terminate in an apical bud. Dwarf shoots are enclosed by a number of bud scales closely surrounded by a thick mat of hairs.Leaves in clusters of three, 20-30 cm long, triquetrous, finely toothed, light green, persisting on an average for a year and a half. Plant is monoecious. 

Flowers arranged in a form of cones. Male and female cones borne on different branches of the same tree. Male cones are modified dwarf shoots and appear in clusters on the lower branches of the tree. Number of cones is 140. Each cone arises in the axil of a scale leaf, replaces a dwarf shoot and is surrounded by several bracts. Female cones replace the terminal buds of the long shoot, solitary or 2-5 together, ovoid, brown and woody when ripe. The cone axis elongates at the time of pollination and the cone protrudes beyond the envelope of scales. After pollination the cone closes. Originally, the cones are pale green but about the time of pollination the colour changes to reddish purple. Seeds winged, wings long and membranous. Seed are shed nearly for 27 months from the time of initiation of the cone.

 

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Chir leaves (needles) are in fascicles of three. Buds sprout in the month of January and the needles generally persist for about one year and five months before they are shed in May or June of the following year. In several cases, however, and particularly in case of vigorous poles, they persist for a part or whole of the second year. In dry hot or dry localities, or in exceptionally dry seasons, the trees are nearly deciduous and may shed all except the new needles of the current year.

Phenology:

Male flowers appear in January and ripen from February to April when pollen showers take place. Female flowers appear in February. Shortly after pollination, the cone scales close. The cones ripen in the spring of the third season, 24 months after the appearance of the female flowers. At this stage, the cones are light brown, 10-20 cm long and 6-12 cm in diameter; the scales are hard, thick, reflexed and tightly closed. The cones begin to open in April or May, sometimes as early as March or as late as June, depending on weather conditions. Dry open cones persist for a few months to a few years on the tree.

Good seeds occur, on an average, once in every three to four years, depending on the locality.

 

Pollinators:

Pollen grains are dispersed by wind. The surrounding area becomes clouded by the yellow powder (also known as sulphur shower).


Floral and seed biology:

Seed Germination & Propagation: Natural regeneration is plenty which takes place through seeds. Under ordinary forest conditions, trees less than 30-years old seldom bear cones. The cones begin to open during April-May the third year i.e. about 24 months after their appearance and the seeds get dispersed during April-July. Under natural conditions, the seeds germinate as soon as sufficient moisture is available. Food stored in the seed breaks down during germination and is the only source of energy for the germinating embryo. Seed germinates 20-25 days after sowing. The radicle emerges and penetrates into the soil. Hypocotyl elongates, straightens and carries above the ground the remnant of the seed alongwith the cotyledons. Cotyledons persist for a long time and are shed after the juvenile leaves and the long shoot have grown considerably.

Artificial regeneration is required in the areas where coniferous forests have been consumed by fire and in the abandoned sites of former shifting cultivation. In areas with twisted trees, artificial regeneration through the seed obtained from straight-grained stock would be preferable to cheaper natural regeneration from the existing badly twisted trees as this character is inherited through the seed.

Artificial regeneration can be done through transplanting nursery-raised seedlings or by direct sowing. Mature cones are collected from trees during March-April, avoiding the trees with a twist. Cones are placed in the sun to loossen the scales and thereafter the seeds are threshed out.seeds give 80-90 per cent germination with a plant survival of 37 per cent.

Seeds are sown in nursery during March – April in shallow drills 15 cm apart. Seedlings are pricked out in July, 15 cm apart in lines about 23 cm apart. One or two year old seedlings are transplanted at the beginning of the rains; in second year the seedling in the nursery are spaced at 30 cm X 40 cm. During transplanting particular care is necessary to avoid injury to the long tap-root. For artificial regeneration for extensive purposes direct sowing is preferable, since the Himalayan chir does not stand transplanting well. For direct sowing, seeds does not require any special preparation or treatment and are sown at the commencement, or a little before the breal of the monsoon. Germiantion and plant survival percentages are normally high.

MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:

Chir pine is affected by number of rusts and wood-rot diseases.

Cronartium himalayense Bagchee, with its aecial stage as Peridermium himalayense Bagchee, attacks stems, causing blisters on the bark; the affected tree may die. Peridermium orientale Cooke - attacks needles, but the damage is not very serious.

Hysterium pinastri Schr. - diseases of nursery seedlings; the needles are attacked, and badly affected seedlings die.

Coleosporium campanulae Lev. - needle rust, frequent, but the damage is not serious. Among the wood-rotting fungi

Fomes annosus Cooke - wood rotting fungi

Fomes pini Lloyd syn. Trametes pini Fr. - wood rotting fungi

Poria monticola Murr. attack the living trees

Fomes pinicola Cooke - recorded on wood.

Polystictus abietinus Fr. - recorded on wood.

Lenzites spp. - recorded on wood.

Ganoderma lucidum (Leyss.) Karst. – root rot

Polyporus schweinitzii Fr. – root rot

Ganoderma applanatum (Wallr.) Pat. – heart rot

Dothistroma pini Hulbary – needle blight

Lophodermium pinastri (Schrad.) Chev. – needle cast

Septoria pisi Westend - sooty mould

Capnodium pini Berk. & Curt. – sooty mould

Cercospora pinidensiflorae Hori & Nambu – needle blight

Dendrophthoe falcata (L.f.) Ettingsh - mistletoe

Insects & Pests -

Chlorophorus strobilicola Champion destroys the cones.

Larvae of species Anomala, Granida, Hilyotrogus and Popillia damage seedlings in the nurseries.

Beetles and larvae of the species Cyrtepistomus, Athalia and Pygaera defoliate and beetles belonging to the genera

Aetheomorpha, Apphthona, Colasposoma gnaw the needles.

Beetles and larvae of several insects of the family Scolytidae bore into the bark and young branches.

Dead and fallen wood is attacked by a large number of borers, particularly the species of the genera Anthaxia, Capnodis and Criocephalus.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Fuel: Wood is a good fuelwood. Timber from the twisted useless trees is used for making charcoal. Calorific values - sapwood: 4,967 cal., 8,941 Btu; heartwood: 5,063 cal., 9,114 Btu.

Wood generally quite strong for its weight, but strength depends upon the straightness of the grain. Even with a slight twist, difficult to saw. Badly twisted wood can not be chopped easily even for fuel billets.

Wood extensively used for constructional purposes, such as rafters, posts, boarding, door and window frames and other house fitments. Also used for cheap joinery and furniture, packing cases, truck and bus bodies, cores of laminboards and electric transmission poles. When treated with preservative, the wood can be used for railway sleepers with a life of about 17 years. Also used in fair quantities for miscellaneous work in construction of railway carriers and wagons. Charcoal is used for fireworks.

Parts Used: Barks, Whole tress and seeds.

Other uses:

Cork - The cork of the bark can be used as a cheap insulating material.

Bark - Thick bark can be used for knife work and small articles of decorative and ornamental value and utility.

Seeds - Seeds eaten by the inhabitants of hilly areas.

Oleoresin - Oleoresin from chir is the main source of turpentine oil in the country. The Indian turpentine is chiefly used as a solvent, especially for thinning paints and varnishes. Also used in pharmaceutical preparations, perfumery industry and in the manufacture of synthetic pine oil, disinfectants, insecticides and denaturants. It is one of the most important basic raw material for synthesis of terpene chemicals which are used in a wide variety of industries, such as adhesives, lubrication additives, synthetic resins, solvents, plasticizers, paints, varnishes, soaps, perfumery, cosmetics and paper and rubber chemicals. Turpentine oil is valued in medicine and is included in the Indian Pharmacopoeia and the Indian Pharmaceutical Codex under the name Oleum terebinthinae.

Rosin - Rosin is obtained as the solid residue in the distillation of the turpentine oil from the oleoresin. It is finely aromatic and occurs in the form of transparent or slightly translucent brittle lumps with a glassy structure. It is used in paper, soap, cosmetics, paint, varnish, rubber and polish industries. Employed as an ingredient of printing inks, casein glues and as a binder in plastics, dry battery and insulating compositions. Also has application in linoleum and roofing cements. It is utilized in the manufacture of fireworks, match compositions, shell explosives, insecticides and disinfectants. Also used as a flux in soldering and tinplating. Applied to reduce slipping and also to bows of musical instruments for ensuring proper contact between bow and the string. Employed in brewing and in mineral beneficiation as a frothing agent.

Rosin oil and Rosin spirit - On destructive distillation rosin yields 3-10 percent of a lighter fraction, called rosin spirit or pinoline, and 80-85 per cent of a denser fraction, known as rosin oil. Rosin spirit is used as an illuminant and as a substitute for turpentine oil in the varnish industry. With lime forms rosin grease, used as a lubricant in wagon and trolley wheels and various other types of machinery. Oil is used in printing ink, varnishes and antiseptics. Sometimes used as an adulterant for oils, especially boiled linseed, olive, rape and sperm oils. Also suggested using as a binder for the production of micanite from mica splittings.

Needle oil - Pine needle oil has antibacterial activity.

Pine wool - The needles, after the extraction of the essential oil, can be converted into a fibrous material called pine wool. It is considered vermin-proof and is used for stuffing pillows, cushions and mattresses. Also suitable for use as a packing material for fruits.

Miscellaneous Information

In pure chir belt, absolute maximum temperature varies from 32 to 380 (90 to 1000 F) whereas minimum absolute temperature falls below freezing point in most parts of the region.

Pines have tap root system. Primary root soon becomes arrested while the long roots continue to grow and bear clusters of dwarf roots. Some of the roots branch dichotomously, form coralloid masses, have an ectotrophic mycorrhiza and are called mycorrhizal roots. Majority of fungi forming this belong to families Boletaceae and Agaricaceae (P. Maheshwari and Konar 1971). These fungi occur loose in the soil and can infect directly. A seedling with mycorrhiza acts as an inoculum for the spread of infection tot he adjoining seedlings. Excessive mortality has been recorded when good-quality seedlings have been grown in soil other than forest soil.

Chir pine is characterised by twisted fibre in some localities, the twist may being either left-handed or right-handed. Twisted chir, even when the degree of twist may be small, is unsuitable for sawing into planks or sleepers for which the wood is principally used. Plant is a source of turpentine.


Plant shows mycorrhizal association. Mycorrhizal associates found are Amanita berkeleyi (Hook. F.) Bas, A. emilii Riel., A. gemmata (Fr.) Bert., A. vaginata (Bull. ex. fr.) Vitt., Lactarius sanguifluus (Paulet ex Fr.) Fr., L. laccata, Scleroderma verrucosum, S. arcolatum, S. dictysporum, Rhizopogon sp, Suillus spp., Astrcus hygrometricus, Amanita vaginata, Lepista nuda, Suillus sibricus, Boletus edulis, Cantharellus cibarius, Scleroderma texense, Rhizopogon rubescence, Thelephora terrestris

Wood: Sapwood distinct from heartwood. Sapwood white to creamy white; heartwood light red when first exposed, turns reddish brown with age, with well marked annual rings and dark lines along the grain denoting longitudinal resin canals. Wood has resinous odour when freshly cut. Straight or nearly straight to strongly twisted and uneven grained, medium coarse-textured and moderately hard and heavy, average weight 575 kg/m3 (36 lb/ft3) at 12 per cent moisture content. Specific gravity 0.58 – 0.68, 529-609 kg/ c m.

A non-porous wood. Growth rings distinct on all the three surfaces. Transition from early wood to late wood usually abrupt, with conspicuous, very much darker and denser late wood. Resin canals of the vertical type scattered, large and distinct to the eye appearing prominent dark lines or resin streaks on longitudinal surfaces. Rays very fine, not visible to eye, but seen under the lens as fine, numerous, closely-spaced lines.

The wood structure is similar to that of kail (Pinus wallichiana A. B. Jacks.) except that in the later the transition from early to late wood is gradual.
