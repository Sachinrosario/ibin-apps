Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

A plant of subtropical climate. Tree with smooth and thin bark, found in Goa, Diu, Daman, Dadra, Nagar Haveli. Distributed in deciduous forests, often cultivated in gardens as an ornamental. Indigenous in coastal forests from Bombay to Malabar and from the Sundarban, Andaman and Nicobar islands, in eastern Himalayan tracts.

In its natural range of distribution the rainfall varies from 1200 m to 2500 mm annually. The maximum and minimum temperatures in its zone of distribution vary from 320 to 200 C respectively. It can be grown on a variety of soils, from sandy loam to gravelly.The tree is often planted on foot of Himalayas, Bihar, Orissa, Bengal, Konkan, North Karnataka and east coast of south India.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Tree with thin, smooth, grey bark, armed with small conical dark-coloured prickles. Leaves 6-12" long, deciduous, petioles 4-6" long, unarmed, readily disarticulating; stipules lanceolate, very caducous. Leaflets membranous, broadly rhomboid-ovate, acute or acuminate, more or less stellately pubescent when young, glabrous when mature, base truncate or rhomboidal. Stipels thick, roundish, gland like, persistent.

Flowers appear before leaves, in dense racemes, arranged in clusters of 1-3 on a puberulous or tomentose rachis, peduncles stout, woody, reaching 6" long, pedicels ¼ in. long; bracts small, triangular, tomentose, deciduous bracteoles 1/6 in. long, subulate, tomentose. Calyx (before expansion of the flower) tubular, 5-toothed at the tip, clothed with deciduous tomentum, mouth very oblique, the upper segment subulate, the lateral two similar but smaller, the lowest one longer and doubled over the others so as to form a blunt point to the bud, the calyx soon splitting don the back to the base and appearing like a spathe. Corolla bright red. Standard broad; wings and keel-petals subequal. Stamens much exserted. Pods stalked, subcylindric, distinctly torulose, glabrescent. Seeds 4-8, subreniform, brown.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

 


Breeding system and varietal improvement:

Phenology:

 Flowering takes place during February-May, while the fruits ripen in May-July.

Older leaves are shed in the autumn. New leaves appear after the flower.


Pollinators: A variety of birds, rosy starlings, babblers, drongo, tailor bird, bulbuls and sunbirds are visitors to the coral tree, contributing to the pollination.

Floral and seed biology:

Seed Germination & Propagation: The plant is easy to propagate, which is done by cuttings, stumps (of 50 cm or more length) or by seeds. 

Fairly large and thick branches take root readily when struck in the ground. Seeds from ripe pods are sown in nursery beds. One-year-old saplings are dug out, the roots and tips are pruned and the stumps are planted in pits of 30 cu cm spaced at the distance of 3-5 m.



MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:

The plant is subject to white spongy sap rot caused by Polyporos anebus.

Other uses:
Leaf fodder - The leaves and tender shoots are eaten as a pot herb. The leaves are valued as cattle fodder and about 2 tonnes of fodder are reported to have been obtained in one lop from 40 trees of about 6 years of age. The leaves are equally relished by cattle, sheep and goats.

Green manure - Loppings are used as green manure and are reported to contain (on air-dried basis) nitrogen 1.46%, potash 1.81% and phosphoric acid 0.5%.

Bark fiber - The bark provides fiber suitable for cordage.

Medicinal - The leaves are considered a laxative, diuretic, anthelmintic, glactagogue and emmenagogue. Fresh juice of leaves is used for relief of earache and as an anodyne in toothache. The bark is astringent and used as an febrifuge, anti-bilious and in collyrium. The seed oil is edible.

Bark - It yields tannin.

Miscellaneous Information

Live fences and hedges - Used to support vine, pepper, grape, betel and other climbers in orchards. It is well adapted for live fences and hedges; also in soil conservation work for wattling on slopes and debris stabilization.
