Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Herbaceous Summer annual, coarse, erect with much variability in growth characteristics; culms solid or sometimes with spaces in pith, 0.6–5 m tall, depending on variety and growing conditions, 5 to over 30 mm in diameter, either dry at maturity or with sweet insipid juice. The branches appear only after the main stem has headed out. The side branches are smaller than the main stalk and they appear much later. Prop roots may grow from culm nodes; bud at each node from which a tiller may grow.

Sweet sorghum is a grass of Old World origin. The name "sweet sorghum" is used to identify varieties of sorghum, Sorghum bicolor (L.) Moench, that are sweet and juicy. It is a native of Africa, a drought-resistant, heat-tolerant member of the grass family. Main center of distribution of cultivated sorghums is in Africa, having been cultivated in Ethiopia for more than 5,000 years; possibly cultivated sorghums were also developed independently in India and China. Now sorghums are widely distributed throughout tropics, subtropics, and warm temperate areas of the world. (1, 2,3,9,17-30)

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

Leaves broad and coarse, similar in shape to those of corn but shorter and wider; blades glabrous and waxy; sheaths encircle culm and have overlapping margins; panicle erect, sometimes recurved, usually compact in most grain sorghums and more open in forage types; seed covered by glumes that may or may not be removed by threshing; prop roots may grow from culm nodes; bud at each node from which a tiller may grow. REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

The inflorescence is panicle has many shapes and sizes. The peduncle carrying the inflorescence either straight and erect, or wavy and erect or goose necked. Inflorescence consists of many primary branches and further divides to form laterals of second and third order. Internodes of these are very short and have the appearance of being in whorls. The joints of the rachis bear paired spikelets. One is fertile and sessile, the other one is staminate or sterile and pedicellate. In the last joint of rachis, are found two sterile spikelets grouped with single fertile spikelet. The reproductive phase is indicated by a characteristic swelling of the apex of the stalk. The swollen area is enclosed by the terminal leaf sheath called the 'boot', whose blade, the 'flag', is usually small and inconspicuous. Emergence of the flag is followed by the ushering out of the boot. Next the inflorescence proper makes its appearance. When the head is completely clear, the peduncle further elongates, elevating the head to a height characteristic of the variety. About six days are required for the emergence of the flag, nine days for, the ushering out of the boot, five days for the complete clearance of the head and five days for the elongation of the peduncle. The anthesis in the inflorescence is basipetal. Time required for all the flowers in a panicle to open averages 7 days and maximum blooming is on third or fourth day. Flowers begin to open 24 hours after complete emergence of the panicle from the boot, occasionally before it is fully emerged. In the panicle or the second flower of the uppermost branch opens first. Usually, the flowers in any horizontal plane within the panicle open at the same time. The anthesis proceeds downwards to the base at a rate of two to five cm per day. Of the paired spikelets, the sessile opens first then pedicelled. Blooming takes place mainly during morning hours, the maximum flower opening is between 12 midnight to 2 a.m., and it continues upto 8 am.,or even 10 am. ,in the morning. Cloudy weather delays flowering, Usually the stigmas become visible before the filaments of the anthers elongate. The period during which the flowers remain open ranges from thirty to ninety minutes. The pollen germinates immediately after pollination and its viability is for a short period. The stigma becomes receptive sometimes even before the flowers open and remain receptive for 8 to 16 hours depending upon the weather conditions. Fertilisation takes place 6 to 12 hours after pollination. (1, 2,3,8,17-30)

(2n = 20) An important warm-season annual, developed by crossing cytoplasmic male-sterile sorghum lines with improved cvs of sudangrass. The extent of natural cross-pollination av-erages about 6 per cent. Sometimes it varies from 0.6 to 50 per cent depending up on the place of production and variety. Percentage of out-cross usually more in loose panicles than in compact ones. In India large scale experiments and tests towards exploitation of hetrosis resulted in release of CHS series of hybrids. Apart from this Hybridization was carried out with both non-sweet, dwarf and sweet, tall female lines successfully which resulted in the hybrid Madhura. (1, 5)


Phenology :

Sorghum is a photo and thermo sensitive short day plant. Stem is erect and solid, can grow upto an height of 1-7 meters. Terminal growth of the plant ends up in an inflorescence. (1, 2,3,9,17-30)

Seed Biology :

Seed are covered by glumes. And seeds may be white, yellow, red, or brown; panicle with up to 6,000 spikelets. Seeds 25,000 to 61,740/kg; grass sorghum 120,000 to 159,000/kg. (3)

Seedling Germination :

Freshly harvested seed may show dormancy. Hard textured seed may be scarified to improve germination. Seeds germinate best between 20–30°C. (3)

Cultivation:

Season: Date of planting depends on use for which crop is intended. In tropics, sorghums may be planted nearly anytime. The Karif crop is sown during June-July and harvested during October-November. While, rabi crop is planted in October-November and is harvested during february.

Land Selection:

Many different soils are used for the production of sweet sorghum, but a soil that has good physical characteristics and good fertility produces the best yield. In general, loam and sandy loam soils are best for the growth of sweet sorghum for syrup production. Soils high in organic matter are thought to have a detrimental effect on syrup quality. The field should have good surface drainage and the soil should be deep and moderately to well drained. Shallow soils or soils very low in organic matter may be droughty. Ample moisture during the growing season is important for good yields of stalks and juice. Clayey soils usually produce poor stands, poor yields, and poor syrup.

Land preparation: Crop sown on well-prepared, firm, moist seedbed if the plant is transplanted. Otherwise, the land for sorghum is not as thoroughly prepared as for some other crops, unless it is weedy. A fine tilth is not attempted, as a firm and compact condition of the soil in the root range is believed to be favourable. In alluvial soils, the field may be ploughed 2 or 3 times or narrow furrows 7-12 cm, deep may be made with a wooden rod having an iron point.

Fertilization: Generally fertilizer with 30–60 kg/ha P and 60–120 kg/ha K is used. Good responses of N fertilizer up to 200 kg/ha have been obtained. And about 6 tonnes/ha of farm yard manure and compost for dry land and 12 tonnes/ha for irrigated fields.

Seed Rate

In dry-land conditions, seed normally sown in rows 75–100 cm apart at rate of 3–9 kg/ha; higher seed rates used for more humid areas. In good rainfall or under irrigation, seed should be close drilled or broadcast at rate of 20–35 kg/ha.

Spacing: Non-tillering cvs are usually spaced 10–14 cm apart in rows, whereas profuse tillering varieties are spaced 30–45 cm apart.

Sowing: Seed planted to depth of 1.5–5 cm depending on soil texture and moisture.

Weeding: Weed control by chemicals or mechanical means important as crop grows slowly in early stages. Cultivate or harrow once after plant emergence and later as required; usually 1–3 cultivations necessary. Shallow cultivation essential to prevent damage to surface roots. Constant roguing necessary of off-type plants before flowering for both open pollinated and hybrid seed production.

Cropping pattern: Many type of cropping pattern exists in the cultivation of sorghum(jowar). Crops like pearl millet, wheat, and barley, or an early rice or pea crop is rotated once in 2-4 years in dry land condition. While, in Nilgiris and Bangalore, early sorghum is followed in the same year by horse gram or niger on red soil and Bengal gram or coriander on black soils.

Irrigation: In parts of South-India, sorghums are generally irrigated on the day of sowing. Second irrigation is given 3 or 4 days later the next is given 3 weeks later and then the subsequent irrigation given at an interval of 10-15 days. (1, 3,6,9,16)

Sorghum midge

Contarinia sorghicola (Coq.)

Injury is caused by the larvae which feed on the ovary, preventing normal seed development. Small, malformed, sterile and useless grain results, and infested heads appear blighted or blasted; in some cases no grain is formed and the florets are empty.

Cultural control practices include early planting of the crop, avoiding close proximity of early and late maturing varieties, prevention of Johnson grass or other hosts from producing blooming heads in or near sorghum fields before the crop blooms, and destruction of crop residues. Insecticides have also teen effective in breeding nurseries anti in seed production fields. Control has also been obtained with DDT and BHC sprays.

1

covered smut (kernel smut or grain smut)

Sphacelotheca sorghi (Link)

In infected earheads, the individual grains are replaced by greyish to brown smut sori; the head may be affected wholly or only partially. The infection is seed-borne and attacks the seedling, finally reaching the earhead.

The disease can be practically controlled by proper seed disinfection. In addition to the conventional methods of seed treatment with sulphur and copper compounds, organomercurials and organic fungicides are useful in controlling the disease.

2

Loose smut

SPhacelotheca cruenta (Kuhn)

The affected plants are usually shorter than the heathly plants by about 30 cm., with thinner stalks and marked tillering. The ears appear much earlier and are distinctly more loose than the healthy ones. Besides the essential organs of the spikelets, the sori may appear in other parts of the plant also.


Seed treatment methods employed for covered smut are recommended for the control of this smijt too.

3

Rust

Puccinia purpurea Cooke

The rust affects the crop at all stages of growth, though the incidence is more at the time of heading or later. The lower leaves are first infected and later the infection spreads to other leaves. Sori develop profusely on both surfaces but more on the lower surface. Plants exhibit varying extent of premature drying of the foliage.

Resistant selections have been found in shallus and milos

4

Downy mildew

Sclerospora sorghi (Kulk.)

Infection takes place at the seedling stage and the leaves become pale yellow in colour, covered on both surfaces (usually on the lower surface) by soft, white, downy growth of the conidial stage of the fungus. Ultimately partial or complete shedding of the leaves takes place, resulting in stunted growth.

Infection being seed-borne, seed treatment is helpful in eliminating the oospores sticking to the seeds. Eradication of diseased plants before oospore formation and growing of resistant varieties have been recommended. Incidence of the disease is less in irrigated fields. Poorly drained areas should be avoided.

5

Grey leaf spot

Cercospora sorghi Ellis & Everh

The disease appears as greyish, velvety spots on the leaves; many of them may be formed contiguously giving rise to strips or patches. Depending on the variety of t. he host, the spots may be red, purple, brown or straw-coloured.

The disease may be controlled by the use of tolerant or resistant varieties, sanitation and proper crop rotation.

6

charcoal rot, stalk rot, blight or hollow stem

Macrophomina phaseoli (Maubl.)

Both seedling blight and stalk rot of immature plants are caused. In older plants no external symptoms are seen till they reach maturity; premature ripening takes place and the heads are poorly developed, causing considerable loss in the yield of grain.

Liming of soil and increasing its organic content are some of the measures recommended. Cultivation of resistant varieties is also recommended.

7

Ergot or sugary disease

Sphacelia sorghi McRae

Turbid drops of honey-dew protrude from the infected spikelets.

Application of fungicides.

Economic Potential and Uses

Sorghum, with large juicy stems containing as much as 10% sucrose, used in manufacture of syrup; sugar can be manufactured from sorghum. Industry for producing jaggery (traditional unrefined sugar) and khandsari (partially refined sugar) from sugarcane is a Rs. 30,000 million ($ 860 million) rural industry in India. About 40% of sugarcane produced in India is diverted for these purposes. Considering this, it was felt that sweet sorghum could become an attractive alternative feedstock to sugarcane for jaggery and syrup production. (5)

Uses

Grain sorghum is a staple cereal in hot dry tropics, the threshed grain ground into a wholesome flour and for production of starch.

Stalks used as animal feed.

Stems containing as much as 10% sucrose, used in manufacture of syrup and jaggery.

Seed is used as food, in brewing "kiffir beer". (3)

Harvesting Practices :

The last step in producing the crop and the first step in obtaining high quality sorghum syrup is harvesting the crop at the proper stage of maturity. As the crop matures, changes in the composition of the juice and soundness of the stalks influence the syrup quantity and quality. Since the sugar content usually continues to increase as maturity approaches, the best harvest time for most varieties will be before the plants are mature. Judging the right time for harvesting is essential. Early deheading helps to increase the sugar level in the juice earlier in the fall and reduces the chances of lodging before harvest. The proper stage to dehead is critical as many new branches will form on the top of the sorghum plant and will produce seed. These branches form about 2 weeks after the first head has been removed. The best stage of maturity to start deheading is at the milk stage of the seed. Research shows that the Brix reading (sugar level) will decrease when deheaded after the milk stage and be lower at harvest in those plants not deheaded earlier. Average Brix levels at harvest when deheaded at the following stages: Bloom-19.4, Early Seed-19.4, Milk Stage-18.4, Soft Dough-16.7 and Hard Dough-14.6. The head should be removed at least below the top node and at least 2 nodes from the top. (3,7)

Processing Techniques:

Processing Sweet Sorghum for Syrup 

(7)

Juice Extraction:

A horizontal 3-roller power mill with crushing capacity to provide enough juice to keep ahead of the evaporation process is desirable. However, vertical mills are adequate for many small operations. To set the mill rollers initially, evenly space the feed roller 3/8-inch from the top roller. The feed roller is not intended to grind the cane but to mash it so that the extraction roller can grind it. The space between the extraction roller and the top roller should be 1/16-inch. The rollers should be tilted so that the front opening is at least 20° below the rear opening to let the juice flow quickly away from the extraction roller. Usually, in small mills the speed of the top (large) roller is 10 to 12 revolutions per minutes (RPM). For large mills, the speed may be adjusted to 6 to 8 RPM. As a general rule, juice is lost if the crushed stalks contain visible juice and are not broken at the joints as the stalks come from the mill.

Filtering and Settling the Juice: As the raw, pea-green juice runs from the mill, it should be strained through a wire screen into a juice box. This straining removes the larger pieces of suspended matter such as stalk fragments. The juice should then be run through a pipe or hose to the juice settling tanks where it is strained through a fine mesh screen or fine nylon cloth as it enters the tank. Use 2 or 3 tanks, each big enough to hold the juice from 2 to 3 hours of milling. Let the juice settle from ½ hour to 3 hours before drawing it off, depending on its quality. Holding it for more than 3 to 4 hours without refrigeration or without heating (as described later) may cause it to ferment and spoil. Do not draw it from the bottom of the settling tank, but at least 1 inch above the bottom of the tank so the settled material is not drawn into the evaporator. A separate opening in the bottom of the tank will be necessary for cleaning out the tank after drainoff.

For larger operations, the juice is heated in the settling tanks, enzymes are added, and skimmings are removed before the juice is moved into the evaporator pan. More details about preheating and enzymes are presented later.

Pre-Heating the Juice: Pre-heating the juice prior to evaporating reduces the amount of skimmings left in the juice and lessens the time for cook off. After the juice has been squeezed from the cane, it can be placed into large stainless steel containers, pre-heated to 160° to 180°F, and allowed to settle for 1 or 2 hours. During this process, many of the impurities in the juice will rise to the top and others will precipitate to the bottom of the tank. The juice is then drawn from about 2 inches above the bottom of the pan (above the settled material) until the skimmings on top of the juice reach that level. Clean out the impurities from the tank, and it is ready to be refilled with fresh juice.

The Evaporation Process: As the extracted juice is heated, some undesirable properties in the juice such as proteins and non-sugar substances are coagulated. These coagulated materials (skimmings) will rise to the surface and need to be removed. Continuous flow pans are somewhat more efficient but require more skill in their operation. Regardless of the evaporation method used, the boiling juice must be continuously skimmed to produce high quality syrup. To enable the juice to evaporate uniformly from the pan, you need to control the temperature along the pan’s length. boiling of the juice starts about 25 percent of the length of the pan from the juice end and remains at a constant temperature until the skimmings are completely removed before reaching the last 25 percent of the pan. As the syrup density increases, the boiling temperature will rise gradually. Evaporation should be done as rapidly as possible but not so fast that you let the syrup burn. Juice cooked off too fast will be dark and contain small, dark particles of non-removed impurities. Remove the syrup from the evaporator when it reaches 226° to 230°F or when it reaches a density of 78° to 80° Brix with a syrup hydrometer or sugar refractometer. Care should be taken that no part of the pan should be dry for more than an instant or the syrup quality may be impaired. The semi-syrup in the center of the pan must be allowed to follow closely behind the syrup being moved to the outlet. As the finished syrup is removed from the pan, it should be cooled and strained before being placed in a large container. Cool the syrup to 140° to 160°F as it is removed from the pan.

The finished syrup is thoroughly sterilized by boiling. If canned above 150°F in clean containers, it will not spoil or ferment. It takes from 6 to 12 gallons of raw juice to make one gallon of sorghum syrup. The finished syrup will weigh about 11.5 pounds per gallon.

Jaggery is also produced similar to syrup production and Jaggery of excellent quality was prepared whenever the brix of sweet sorghum juice was at least 15 degrees and when the ratio of sucrose to reducing sugars was at least 9. While,excellent quality syrup could be made when the brix of raw juice was at least 14 degrees, which was more or less throughout the year.

Other Uses

Sorghum is known to have varied medicinal uses, it is reported to be antiabortive, cyanogenetic, demulcent, diuretic, emollient, intoxicant, and poison, sorghum is a folk remedy for cancer, epilepsy, flux, and stomachache. The root is used for malaria in southern Rhodesia; the seed has been used for breast disease and diarrhea; the stem for tubercular swellings. In India, the plant is considered anthelminthic and insecticidal, and in South Africa, in combination with Erigeron canadense L., it is used for eczema. In China, where the seeds are used to make alcohol, the seed husk is braised in brown sugar with a little water and applied to the chest of measles patients. The stomachic seeds are considered beneficial in fluxes. Curacao natives drink the leaf decoction for measles, grinding the seeds with those of the calabash tree (Cresentia) for lung ailments. Venezuelans toast and pulverize the seeds for diarrhea. Brazilians decoct the seed for bronchitis, cough and other chest ailments, possibly using the ash for goiter. Arubans poultice hot oil packs of the seeds on the back of those suffering pulmonary congestion.

A decoction of ca 50 g seed to a liter of water is boiled down to ca 1/2 liter as a folk medication for kidney and urinary complaints. (3)

The graining has Cooling, Aphrodisiac, Indigestible, Constipating, Improves Appetite and Taste, Useful in " Kapha ", Biliousness, Diseases of the Blood, Piles, Ulcers, Tumors (Ayurveda). (30)

Miscellaneous Information

Parasites -Sorghum is the host for a number of species of the genus Striga (Scrophulariaceae), of which S. asiatica Kuntze and S. densiflora Benth. are the most important in India. These parasites cause considerable damage to sorghum crops, especially in Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu. Striga spp. produce numerous minute seeds which are disseminated by wind, water, crop refuse, etc. The seeds generally do not germinate unless stimulated by the presence of a host plant. The parasite is totally dependent on the host in the early stages when it is underground and penetrates the roots of the host by papillae from its haustoria. Later, on emergence from the soil, the parasite develops shoots and leaves, but is dependent for water and nutrients on the host during its entire life. Wide spacing, intercultivation and weeding of the parasites before flowering are useful methods of control. Other means of control include spray applications of selective weedicides and growing of resistant. (1)

Sorghum contains hydrocyanic acid and the alkaloid hordenine. Sometimes plants accumulate toxic levels of nitrate. Varieties differ considerably in HCN poisonings. Danger is slight when grain is nearly mature. Young plants and suckers are dangerous, particularly when suffering from drought. HCN is destroyed when fodder is ensiled or cured as hay. In India the intoxication is known as jowar poisoning. Plants may contain 0.07% of the alkaloid hordenine. (3)

How to prevent the cattle from being poisoned ?

  • Avoid the cattle from feeding stunted and green Sorghum plants.

  • Silage the Sorghum plant before being fed to the cattle. (22)
