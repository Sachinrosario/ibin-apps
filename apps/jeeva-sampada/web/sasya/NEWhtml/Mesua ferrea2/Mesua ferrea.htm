Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:

Medium to large-sized glabrous, handsome, evergreen tree with a rounded crown, often buttressed. Evergreen to semi-evergreen forests of North-East India, Western Ghats, Andamans, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh and Myanmar at 60-1,200 m, sometimes to 1,500 m. Up to 45 m or more in the Western Ghats, where it sometimes forms part of the emergent layer in tropical rain forest. Elsewhere of moderate size up to 24 m where it occurs in the second storey as in Assam and Arunachal Pradesh.

Conservation/ Threat Status : 

Wild/ naturalised/ cultivated

Lower risk - least concern

Availability: Large supplies from east zone, particularly from Assam and south zone. Exact origin unknown, but it is believed to be the Malay Peninsula. Cultivated in Sri Lanka, Myanmar and in the Nilgiris.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Medium-sized glabrous tree; trunk straight, erect; bark smooth, ash-colored, peeling off in white flakes exposing a reddish brown surface, fibrous and red inside and exuding an aromatic resin, young branches twiggy, slender. Leaves opposite, decussate, oblong-lanceolate, acute or acuminate, young leaves deep crimson above and silvery beneath, afterwards shining above, glaucose and pruinose beneath, rounded or acute at the base and with close, inconspicuous nerves.

Flowers very fragrant, axillary or terminal, solitary or in pairs, subsessile, 2.5-10.2 cm across, resembling a rose; buds subglobose; bracts 0. Sepals 4, orbicular, cupped, puberulous outside, persistent, and the inner pair much longer than the outer. Petals 4, pure white, spreading, obovate-cuneate, with crisped and undulate margins often torn. Stamens very numerous, golden-yellow, much shorter than the petals, slightly united at the base into a fleshy ring; anthers oblong. Style twice as long as the stamens; stigma peltate. Fruit ovoid with a conical point, surrounded by the enlarged sepals; pericarp tough, semi-woody, at length 2-halved. Seeds 1-4, angular, smooth, chest-nut-brown. Seeds up to 2.5 cm across, dark brown and shining. The magnificent white flowers with a central mass of golden stamens.

 

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

 

Breeding system and varietal improvement:

Phenology:  

Flowers during February-April in Western Ghats, February to June Eastern India. Fruits July-September in North-East India, May-January in Western Ghats.

New leaves in February in NE India, from December-February in Western Ghats, Andamans, Sri Lanka, Bangla Desh and Myanmar at 60-1,200 m, sometimes 1,500 m.

Floral and seed biology:

Seedling Germination: Natural production is generally profuse on account of abundant seeding. Artificial propagation may be done by direct sowing or by transplanting nursery raised seedlings. Transplanting is preferable under top canopy shade and may be carried out from first to the third rainy season after sowing. A spacing of 6 ft. is recommended. The rate of growth is slow. It is likely that the tree grows faster in plantations than in natural forests.

Propagation:Grown from seeds. They lose their viability quickly and therefore are sown immediately. Germination in about 10-14 days. Two-three year old saplings are best for transplanting. It deserves to be widely grown in gardens in areas of good rainfall. Earliest leaves emerging from the germinating seeds are dark blue in Tirunelveli and pink in the Silent Valley. There is a belief that blue leaves indicate trees whose heartwood is black, while the pink and pale green ones indicate dark red to reddish brown heartwood.

 

MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:

The tree is affected by brown cubical rot (Fomes dochmius Berk & Br.) and white sap and heart rot [Ganoderma lucidum (Leyss.) Karst.]. it is also attacked by beetles, Xyloborus discolor Blandford, X. interjectus Blandford and a few others as well as by a buprestid borer, Chrysochroa sp.

Diaporthe curvatispora Wakef. – leaf infection, Sibsagar (Assam)

Rosenscheldiella indica Anahosur – leaf infection, Coorg (Karnataka)

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts Used:Bark, Flowers, fruits and seeds.

Fuel: Wood good fuel. 

Timber used for railway sleepers, bridges and posts, beams and construction work. Suitable for electric poles and currier’s cutting blocks. Also used for boat-building, well construction, mines, agricultural implements, crushers, bearings, tool handles, golf club heads and walking sticks. It can be used for gunstocks, musical instruments and cabinet work. Timber is very hard. It is used for railway slippers.

Harvesting Practices:

 

Produce and Products:

Chemistry:

Calorific values - sapwood: 4,956 cal., 8,921 Btu.; heartwood : 5,051 cal., 9,090 Btu.

Processing Techniques: Heartwood very refractory to treatment, side and end penetration practically nil. Slow and difficult to season. Liable to cracking, warping and splitting.

 

Other uses:

Seed oil suitable for soap making but has not been used widely due to its objectionable color. Refined oil suitable for use as soap stock. 

The kernel meal, freed from oil, rich in nitrogen and phosphorus and may be used as manure.

Fragrant stamens of mesua flowers reported to be used in Malaya for stuffing pillows and cushions. Dried flowers, along with other aromatics used in preparation of perfumed ointment.

Known under the name otto of nagkesar, the oil used for perfuming soaps, particularly in conjunction with sandalwood oil.

Fruit sometimes eaten. Flowers astringent and stomachic. Bark and unripe fruits astringent, aromatic and sudorific. Seed oil used as an embrocation in rheumatism and for skin diseases.

Flowers - Flowers are used in medicine and cosmetics. Also used to scent the stuffing of pillows.
Stem bark, leaves, roots and pericarp of the fruit are medicinally important.

Miscellaneous Information


 Wood: Sapwood greyish or pinkish-white fairly sharply demarcated from the brick-red heartwood, occasionally with darker streaks on the longitudinal surface, very hard; very heavy, average weight 1065 kg/m3 (66 lb/ft3) at 12 per cent moisture content. Specific gravity 0.93 – 1.24 air-dry, 56-67 lb./ c. ft. Somewhat lustrous; straight to interlocked-grained; medium- to coarse-textured, with a smooth feel.

One of the hardest and strongest of the Indian timbers.

A diffuse-porous wood. Growth rings indistinct. Pores just visible to the eye, distinct under the lens, moderately large, scanty, irregularly distributed, exclusively solitary, in oblique or radial groups, partly filled with tyloses and reddish gummy deposits; vessel lines distinct on longitudinal surfaces. Soft tissues in narrow, wavy reddish-brown concentric bands, often ending abruptly. Rays fine to very fine, just visible under the hand lens, as numerous, closely-spaced lines.

The fragrant blossoms are used for stuffing pillows in bridal beds. One of the five arrows of Kamadeva, The Indian Cupid, is tipped with mesua wood. A sacred tree, grown around temples. The oily seeds are strung like beads on a piece of split bamboo and when lighted burn like a candle. The nagas make bows from the wood. Also used for golf club heads, country pipes, as props in Kolar gold mines. The leaves and flowers are used against snakebite. It is a sacred tree of India.
