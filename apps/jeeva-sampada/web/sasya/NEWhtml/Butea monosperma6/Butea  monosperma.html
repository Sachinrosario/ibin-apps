HABIT & HABITAT

Deciduous medium-sized tree, commonly found throughout India. Grows gregariously in open grasslands and scattered in mixed  forests up to an altitude off 1000 m. Thrives well on both poor and rich soils, drought and saline conditions.

As an exotic, it has successfully been acclimatized in Israel, although it is occasionally cut back by frost. It has also been introduced in Zimbabwe.

B. monosperma is a tree of tropical and subtropical climate. Found throughout the drier parts of India, often gregarious in forests, open grasslands and wastelands. It is a characteristic tree of the plains, often forming pure patches in grazing grounds and other open places, escaping extermination owing to its resistance to browsing and its ability to reproduce from seed and root suckers. In its native habitat, most of the rain is received during the monsoon season, while the autumn and summer months are generally dry. The tree is very drought resistant and frost hardy, although the leaves turn white and fall off.

Soil type: It grows on a wide variety of soils including shallow, gravelly sites, black cotton soil, clay loams, and even saline or waterlogged soils. Seedlings thrive best on a rich loamy soil with pH 6-7 under high temperature and relative humidity.

Biophysical limits
Altitude: Up to 1 500 m, Mean annual temperature: -4 -49 deg. C, Mean annual rainfall: 450-4500 mm.


Availability : 

Palas is a native of India and frequently referred in sanskrit literature.  When one has to travel in Western Railway from Delhi to Mumbai route via Kotah during the beginning of summers, they can get a spectacular view of Salmon-red flowers of palas.  Similarly the traveler who is going to Delhi from Vijay Wada, by train, can see the spectacular palas blooms in the dry deciduous forests of Madhya Pradesh.

Native: Cambodia, India, Indonesia, Japan, Laos, Myanmar, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Thailand, Vietnam

Exotic: China, Papua New Guinea

Starting from Burma in the East, it is found growing throughout the Indian subcontinent, especially in Indo-Gangetic plains from 150 m to 1000 m or so above mean sea level. It is quite happy in black cotton soil as well and it is accordingly quite common from Sri Lanka north-wards i.e. the Indian Peninsula, especially the Decan Plateau.

Palas is found all over Indian subcontinent, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka and Myanmar. In India the species has a very wide distribution all over the country except in the moist and arid regions. It grows in parts of Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Haryana, Karnataka, Orissa, Madhya Pradesh, Punjab, Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal and some parts of Saurastra in Gujarat.  It extends to Assam and in Garo Hills up to 300 m. It is most common in the mixed deciduous forests of Central  and Southern India.

It can be grown in waterlogged area, on black cotton soil, on saline soil and also on badly drained ground.  The species occurs on sites with impeded drainage.  The species can also com up on highly degraded sites under adverse conditions.   It is a drought hardy and good coppicing species.  

Status : 

Not threatened. The tree is planted in borders of farm houses.  Common in open deciduous forests and on hilly tracts.

It is recommended for reclamation and afforestation of inundated, badly drained, waterlogged or saline banks and also in arid zones.  Leaves are lopped for feeding buffaloes.

Erosion control: In India, farmers frequently use B. monosperma to stabilize field bunds.

Ornamental: Butea monosperma is planted as an ornamental because it flowers with a profusion of bright orange, rarely sulphur-coloured flowers.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

The bole of dhak is seldom straight and the branching too follows no particular pattern. Growth-wise it is quite slow growing and attains a height of about 5 to 8 m and diameter of about 20 to 40 cm (at breast height) when mature at the age of about 50 years or so. The bark of dhak is fibrous and bluish gray to light brown in colour. It exudes a kind of red juice if and when injured. That dries into very useful gum.

One of the most beautiful trees of the Indian subcontinent. The tree is aptly named  as the “flame of the forest” on account of the scarlet to orange-red flowers adorning  leafless canopies. Tree has somewhat crooked trunk up to 15 m in height and 1.6-2.0 m in girth. Bark is rough varying from slate-gray to pale brown. Leaves are long-petioled (10-23 cm) swollen at the base, tri-foliate,  leaflets coriaceous, broadly obovate form a cuneate or deltoid base.  Flower buds dark brown.  Flowers are bracteate, bracteolate, bisexual,  bright orange–red,  15 cm long  born on terminal or axillary racemes. Yellow and white flowering trees have also been reported  Calyx is 5-toothed, fleshy, brown tomentose. Stamens are diadelphous (9+1). Pollen : ovule ratio – 9719 : 1. Ovary is superior and  bears 5 or 6 ovules. Stigma is globose, wet and papillate. Papillae are unicellular. The style just below the stigma is solid but the remaining part is hollow. 

A large number of flowers abscise. Fruit a silky-tomentose, single seeded samara 10-13 cm long, seeds flat, reniform, 3.3-3.8 x 2.2-2.5 cm.  Stamens are diadelphous (9+1).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Breeding System :
Flowers are ornithophilous.

The species is an outbreeder and self-incompatible; Self-pollen tubes are inhibited  in the upper part of the style.  

The reproductive period lasts for four months starting from the emergence of inflorescence in February until fruit dispersal in May.  Anthesis in B. monosperma occurred between 0900 to 1030h.  Anthers dehisced one day prior to anthesis between 0700 to 0830h.  Stigma becomes receptive 1-2h before anthesis and retains receptivity for 15-18h. Cross-pollination resulted in 23.34% fruit set.  The cross-pollinated pistils showed that pollen tubes reach either the first or the second ovule from the stylar end.  Pollen tubes failed to enter ovules located in the lower part of the ovary. Low fruit set is the result of premature abscission of flowers due to the damage caused by flower borers (insects), nectar thieves and lack of pollination  (Tandon, Shivanna & Mohan ram 2003 in press). 


Phenology :
Deciduous tree, starts shedding leaves by December and becomes bare by January. Flowering begins in January/February, bud break occurs in February and continuous till March/April. March end and April beginning is the peak of flowering.  Pods mature by May/June and disperse before the onset of monsoon. Leaf primordia appear in April-May.  

Leaves are shed during the dry season. At the beginning of the rainy season, the leafless tree flowers abundantly and is very conspicuous in the forest. At the end of the flowering period, new leaves develop, which are initially a pale bronze-tinged green. 

Pollinators:
Flowers produce copious amount of nectar. Freshly opened flowers are brightly coloured and attract large number of birds. Flowers are ornithophilous. Visits of at least seven types of birds, squirrel, Giant Asian honey bee and thrips have been recorded .  Purple sunbirds are the most effective pollinators.  Recent studies have documented the involvement of squirrels in pollination of this species  Nectar is the major floral reward. Low fruit set is the result of premature abscission of flowers largely due to damage caused by nectar robbers and flowers borers (Tandon, Shivanna & Mohan ram 2003 in press).


Seed Biology :

The fruit of palas is a flat legume — a pod, nearly 15 cm long and 3 to 5 cm wide. Young pods have a lot of hair — a velvety cover. The mature pods hang down like peculiar legumes.

Pods are the dispersal units and are shed before they dehisce.  Seeds germinate readily with availability of water following monsoon rains.

Only one seed is set in a fruit out of 5/6 ovules; the specific epithet monosperma refers to this feature. The ovule located in the first or second position in the ovary from the stylar end  develops in to the seed; the remaining ovules abort.  Seeds are flat, reniform and readily germinate with the availability of water. Under laboratory conditions 68% seeds germinate in 7 days.  Germination is enhanced by overnight soaking the seeds in water.

Seeds are separated by opening the pods and are dried in shade and stored in dry cool place in air tight containers or in woven sacs, which are lined inside with polythene sheet.  The viability is up to one year but seeds used just after collection gives better germination.  The number of seeds is 500 to 600 per kg and germination percentage is about 75% to 85%.  Germination commences within a week and is over within 15 days.

Seed storage behaviour is orthodox; no loss in viability during 2 years of hermetic storage at room temperature. There are approximately 500-1500 seeds/kg.


Seedling Germination :

Natural regeneration by both seed and root suckers is profuse. Artificial propagation is chiefly from direct-sown seeds, sown 25-30 cm apart in lines 3-5 m apart. The taungya system is often used, as weeding during the 1st 1-2 years is essential to the proper development of the plants. Root suckers and nursery seedlings can also be used for propagation. Because of the good coppicing power of this species, it is also a reliable method of natural propagation.

Germination, which starts in about 10-12 days, is completed in 4 weeks. Fresh seeds have a good germinative capacity (about 63%) at optimum germination temperature of about 30 deg. C. Germination is hypogeous.

Seeds develop a very long taproot at early age. Seedlings die back to the ground level, 2-3 times due to frost and draught or heavy weeds.  

Seeds can be directly sown into polythene bags.  At least 15days before planting the seedlings should be hardened by regulated watering.

Root suckers are produced by damaging the root by opening small trenches near the trees.  Germination takes place within a week and is over within 15 days. Seedlings can be maintained for one year and stumps may be prepared for planting.   

Vegetative propagation is through stump planting.  Stumps with about 4 cm of shoot and about 23cm of root, obtained from one year old seedlings, are very successful.  Stumps are prepared at the outbreak of monsoon rains.  These give high percentage of survival(80%) and satisfactory growth.


Cultivation:

Butea monosperma is moderate in its demand for light. Although it can withstand some shade, dense shade suppresses its growth. The trees pollard and coppice well and produce root suckers freely. They can also withstand heavy annual lopping. Well suited for silvopasture at wide spacing (10-15 m) in extensive tracts of grassland (for example, in central and western India, where it is managed by repeated coppicing on a roughly 5-year rotation). Coppice shoots are also cropped in intermediate years for the larger leaves. Under dry land conditions and in its natural habitat, coppice management yields roughly 100 kg/tree of air-dry fuel wood every 5 years. If allowed to grow, trees attain a height of 3-5 m and dbh of 15-20 cm in 10 years. Plantations can be established on irrigated as well as rain fed land.

Planting is carried out in July-August and is followed by 52 to 80 percent establishment.  Young seedlings suffer badly when shaded by competing weeds, as the species is a moderate light demander.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests: 

B. monosperma is an important host for  the lac insect (Laccifer lacca Kerr). The tree is used for the production of ragini type of lac insect. Bihar, Madhya Pradesh and West Bengal are the important states for lac production in India 

Plocaederus obsesus Gahan - wood borer

Enarmonia stirpicola Meyrick - bores the pith of shoots and feeds on buds and flowers.

Aspidiotus orientalis  Newstead - feed on sap.

Sinoxylon sudanicum Lesne and  Basitropsis hamata  borers of the sapwood

Trachys bicolorI Kerr. and larva of Taragama dorsalis  defoliage the tree.

Holotrichia serrata (Fabr.) and Coptosoma ostensum  Dist. are also serious pests.  

Seedlings and saplings are browsed and damaged by cattle. Rats and porcupines feed on fleshy roots, killing the sapling.

Insect pests attack different parts of the tree. Several defoliators belonging to the families Arctiidae, Eucosmidae, Lasiocampidae, Lymantriidae, Noctuidae, Notodontidae, Pieridae, and Sphingidae have been recorded. Insects of the family Coccidae feed on the sap. The larvae of some insects of the family Lycaenidae feed on the flowers.

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Bark,  leaves, flowers and gum

Uses

A reddish juice that oozes from the cuttings of bark, when exposed to air hardens into glassy ruby-red gum known as "Bengal kino" or  butea gum

  • It is an astringent and used as haemostatic, aphrodisiac, depurative and tonic, and in treating diarrhoea, haemorrhoids,   haemoptysis, haematemesis, diabetes, leprosy, skin diseases, ulcer, pharyngodynia, general debility, hyperacidity dyspepsia, fever, hoemorrhage from stomach and bladder. Infusion is applied to bruises and inflammation.  The gum is a powerful astringent; it is given internally for diarrhea, dysentery, phthisis and hemorrhage of the stomach and bladder.
  •   Gum kino is  a substitute for genuine gum kino  of  commerce obtained  from Pterocarpus marsupium  Roxb. Gum is also used for dying and tanning.  
  • Gum yielded by the Indian plants used locally in tanning and in precipitating indigo.
  • The bark contains an excellent tanning agent.
  • Dye: Flowers yield a bright yellow coloured dye.  This is obtained in the form of a decoction or infusion from dried flowers.  It is very fugitive.  The dye is rendered less fugitive by adding alum, lime or alkali that deepens the colour to orange. This dye is widely used for colouring saris and other fabrics.  It is also used in holy festival.

Chemistry
The main constituent of the flower is butrin (1.5%) besides butein (0.37%) and butin (0.04%). Also contains flavonoids and steroids. Later studies proves that isobutrin slowly change to butrin on drying.

Other than these in flowers, coreopsin, isocoreopsin, sulphurein (glycoside) and other two with monospermoside and isomonospermoside structures are also identified. Roots contain glucose, glycine, glucosides and aromatic compounds. Tetramers of leucocynidin are isolated from gum and stem bark. Seed contains oil. The bright colour of the flower is attributed to the presence of chakones and aurones.

The gum contains leucocyanidin, its tetramer, procyanidin, gallic acid and mucilaginous material.

 Miscellaneous Information

It is a sacred tree, referred to as a treasurer of the gods, and used in sacrifice related rituals. From its wood, sacred utensils are made. The flowers are offered as in place of blood in sacrifice rituals to goddess Kali. The dry stem pieces are used to make sacred fire. It is an anthropogenic tree of several castes. 'Chakradatta' mentions the use of its gum in external astringent application. The bark is also used in snake bite.  When seeds are pounded with lemon juice and applied to the skin, it act as a rubefacient.  Arab horse dealers put one seed into each feed of corn to keep their horses in condition.

Butea is named after botanist John Stuart, who was the Earl of Butea and Monersperma means one seed.  The battlefield of Plassey is said to have taken its name from the palas tree.  The wood resembles teak and hence the English call it bastard teak.

In semi arid areas, when the entire landscape becomes devoid of any colour in the summer, the flaming orange flowers create an impression of patches of fire, and hence the name 'Flame of the Forest'.

Dhak is a very useful plant in a number of ways. Its leaves are essential for various rites in Hindu homes, like yajnas, havans, etc.
