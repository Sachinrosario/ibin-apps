Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

A graceful, spinous bamboo, distributed throughout the moist parts of India, up to an altitude of 1,250 m, partuicularly near river banks. Also cultivated in the plains of North-West India and on the hills of Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu and Karnataka. The plant is very common in the moist deciduous forests and along streams and water courses, and forms impenetrable thickets, extending over vast areas. Though it prefers moist localities, it is not particular about the soil and tolerates even clayey and inundated soils. The best growth is found on alluvial soils along streams.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Rhizomes short, stout, knotty. Culms dense, reaching 24-30 m in height and 15-17 cm in diameter, green, hollow, purplish green when young, turning golden yellow, with prominent nodes and long internodes, lower ones rooting, often sub-angular, flexuous. Leaves linear or linear-lanceolate, 7-18 cm X 2-20 mm.

Flowers gregariously once in 30-45 year. Flowers in large panicles, sometimes occupying the whole culm. Caryopsis oblong 5-8 mm long, grooved on one side.

Floral and seed biology:

Seed Germination & Propagation: Propagation by direct sowing or entire planting. Best time for sowing is April. Best time for planting is July to October.

Depending upon the direction of folding of the first leaf, either to the left or to the right, the seedlings have been classified as left- and right-handed. It has been observed, in recent trails, that the seedlings classified as right-handed ones in morphology are expected to produce more bamboos. It is also recommended to grow the left- and right-handed seedlings separately.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Wood used as fuel.

Wood: Culms are durable and possess great strength which progressively decreases from bottom to top.

The bamboo is employed in construction of houses, ceiling, flooring, frames of mud-built houses, scaffolding, partitions, household items, furniture, boxes, ornamental vases, mats, hats, wicker-work, bows and arrows, fish-traps, walking-sticks, rafters, ladders, tent poles and shafts for tongas. It is also used for artificial limbs and splints. Building-boards of good strength, possessing resistance to water, termites and fungi, have been manufactured from the culms, using resin and various fillers. These boards can be employed for light partitions, ceiling-boards, moulded furniture such as desks and dressing tables, drawers, sewing machine covers, travelling-requisites, wardrobes, flat mats, etc.

The culms yield good quality of paper pulp and are highly exploited for this purpose.

Other uses:

The young, tender shoots although considerably acrid, are relished but may be poisonous to both humans and cattle, as they contain benzoic acid and a cyanogenic glucoside. The young sprouts are cooked as vegetable. The tender shoots are picked or steeped in oil for direct consumption or made into curries. They promote the appetite and help in digestion. The young twigs are also eaten by elephants and buffaloes.

Leaves - The leaves are reported to be palatable and are used for livestock rations in the hilly areas during winter months.

Seeds - Seeds are a reputed food during famine. Over-all nutritive value of the seeds is better than rice and wheat.

Soft shoots, leaves, roots, bark, black soot deposited on the culms are medicinally useful. Hollow internodes accumulate a substance, called tabashir or banslochan, used medicinally.

The stems and leaves are used in Ayurvedic system of medicine.

Stem - Concretions of silica in stems is medicinal.
