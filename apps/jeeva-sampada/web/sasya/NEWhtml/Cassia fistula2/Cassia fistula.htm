Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Small trees found throughout the greater parts of the India up to 4,000 feet Himalaya, Burma, Indo-China, Java and the Philippines.
Availability :

Almost throughout India.
Conservation/ Threat Status : Common

Back to Top

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

A small or medium sized tree, occasionally reaching a height of 60 feet and a girth of 5 feet. The bark is smooth and grey or greenish-grey on young trees. The tree is very conspicuous in the hot weather when the flowers appear. These are large and bright yellow and occur in long pendulous bunches (racemes). At the time of flowering the tree is leafless or nearly so, the first flowers appearing as the last of the old leaves are falling and the flowering continues until the fresh foliage has been developed. The fresh leaves are often of a rich coppery colour. During the cold weather the tree is usually conspicuous from its pods, which are 1-2 feet long, and ¾-1 inch diameter and dark brown in colour.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:


Breeding system and varietal improvement:
The pods when rips should be broken up and the seeds extracted. It is advisable to sow plenty of seeds even if only a few plants are required as much of the seed lies dormant in the soil for a year or more without germinating though some usually come up in a few months after sowing.

Phenology:

The tree transplants readily but sometimes does not appear to grow well after transplanting. Such plants as a rule eventually recover and grow well. Under favorable conditions the trees should start flowering in about five years from seed

Pollinators:

Floral and seed biology:
Seedling Germination : Germination may be hastened by pouring very hot water (nearly boiling) on the seed and leaving the seeds to soak for a day or two. Some of the seeds will probably be found swollen; these should be removed and sown at once (they must not be allowed to become dry). The hot water treatment can then be repeated on any unswollen seeds, if necessary two or three times.

MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
The tree is very subject to defoliation by caterpillars. It is advisable to watch young plants and remove any caterpillars found eating the leaves. Older plants are often badly attacked but although the plants look unsightly for a time they usually soon recover and appear none the worse.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
ORNAMETAL FLOWERING TREE
Grown as avenue tree.
Wood products: The timber is hard and durable but being available in small size only, it is used for posts, crates and similar purposes in village rather than in the timber trade.
Non-timber products: To a small extent the bark is used for tanning.
As a showy medicinal and ornamental flowering tree.

Parts Used:

Bark, wood, flowers, fruit pulp
Fruit pulp is excellent and safe purgative.


Harvesting Practices:


Produce and Products:


Chemistry:
Fruit pulp contained proteins (19.94) and carbohydrates (26.30 %); arginine, leucine, methionine, phenylalanine, tryptophan, aspartic and glutamic acids isolated from pulp (J. Inst. Chemists, Culcutta 1980, 52, 85; Chem. Abstr. 1980, 93, 66152 c); a new dimeric proanthocyanidin CFI isolated along with (-) epiafzelechin, (+) catechin, kaempferol, dihydrokaempferol and 1,8-duhydroxy-3-methylanthraquinone; its structure determined (Indian J. Chem. 1982, 21 B, 626).

Processing Techniques:

Other uses:
Flowers yield a yellow dye, bark is the source of brown and black dye. Bark yields deep red colour dye after boiling with alum. The pods are divided into 1-seeded cells by thin transverse partitions. The seeds are immersed in a dark-brown sweetish pulp, which is much used medicinally being laxative in small does and purgative in larger doses.
