HABIT & HABITAT:
A procumbent annual, with small long petioled leaves. It is a cosmopolitan weed growing on waste ground, throughout India, and extends to other tropical and sub-tropical countries and it is a cultivated species found in Gardens,

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Annual growing to 1 mt. height. It is a frost tender. Leaves small, long petioled, oblong ovate or grounded, usually 2-lobed at the apex. The flowers are monoecious (individual flowers are either male or female, but both sexes can be found on the same plant) and are pollinated by Wind. The plant is self-fertile.

Seed Biology :

Seed Germination :
Seed - sow late spring in situ. An earlier sowing can be made in a greenhouse and the plants put out after the last expected frosts. Germination is usually rapid and good if the soil is warm. A drop in temperature overnight aids germination.

Cultivation:
Soil: The plant prefers light (sandy), medium (loamy) and heavy (clay) soils and requires well-drained soil. The plant prefers acid, neutral and basic (alkaline) soils and can grow in very acid soil. It cannot grow in the shade. It requires moist soil.
Prefers a well-drained fertile soil in a sunny position. Requires a hot sheltered position if it is to do well. Tolerates a pH in the range 4.3 to 7.5. Plants should not be given inorganic fertilizers, since it causes toxicity to the plants. This plant was cultivated by the ancient Romans and Greeks for its edible leaves.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES
Hymenia recurvalis and other caterpillars are serious pests of amaranth. Cletus sp., Asparia sp. and Lygus lineolarus bug (Cabbage looper), Trichoplusiani, (European comborer), Ostrinia nubilalis (Corn earworm), Heliothis zea (Cowpea aphid), Aphis craccavora, (Striped blister beetle), Epicauta uittata, a weevil Conotrachelus seniculus and Spinach flea beetle, Disonycha xanthomelas, Lygus bugs, and more specifically the Tarnished plant bug, are presently considered to be the most important insect pest of grain amaranth. Lygus bugs are cosmopolitan pests. They are very familiar pests throughout the world. They feed on hundred of plant species. In fact, the tarnished plant bug has been recorded on 32 hosts. Lygus bugs damage grain amaranth by feeding on the meristematic tissue, developing floral buds, immature blossoms, and developing embryos. This feeding causes localized wilting and tissue necrosis, abscission of fruits, morphological deformation of fruit and seed, and altered vegetative growth have severely damaged grain amaranth yields by piercing the developing plant/leaves/seed and sucking out the juices. Leaf miners have also been found on grain amaranth. Serious damage has been caused by Spider mites. Stem weevil is a major pest of amaranth; its grubs damage foliage and roots and cause the plant to wilt. Stem borer (Lixus truncatucus) is another problems in Africa and Asia in early sown plants, which causes high degree of lodging in plants. Leaf rotters also cause considerable damage during rainy seasons. Seedbeds should be guarded against ants and termites. The other pests damaging the crop are Hypolixus nubilous (Egypt), Rhachi creagra (Costarica), Chrotogonus (Pakistan), Hyphurus (India), Geocoris (California), Thysanoptera (Hawaii), Haplothrips longisetosus (India), Diabnotica barberi and Spodoptera exigna. Peggy Hass (1983) noted that in South Mexico a weedy type of amaranth is used as a guard crop for corn; some pest insects that normally attack corn prefer the amaranth, so they will eat the weedy amaranth and leave the corn alone. Meloidogyne incognita, (Chitwood) root- knot nematode, a serious pest of Celosia also slightly affect Amaranthus species. Soil fungus, damping off, leaf blight, white rust and mycoplasma and virus have been identified as the serious diseases of grain amaranth in India, Pandy et al. (1985) while studying the leaf surface mycoflora of Amaranthus paniculatus grown in Almora hills, observed that of 24 fungi spp. found on leaves of this crop, Fusarium oxysporum and Nectria cinnabarina were of restricted occurance while Alternaria alternata, A. amaranthi, Phoma glomerata, P. hibumica, Phyllostica spp, (leaf spot), Aspergillus luchunsis and Rhizoctonia spp were commonly isolated. The fungal population was higher in the pre flowering stage of the host. Sealy et al (1988) screened a total of 126 accessions of species and cultivars of Amaranthus against Pythium myriotylum and suggested that accessions that exhibited weighted percent mortality of <20% be selected for use in breeding programme against damping off disease. Bialoskorski et al (1982) also observed Pythium aphamidermatum damaging amaranth crop in Brazil. McLean and Roy (1988) reported Colletotrichum domatium damaging Amaranthus hybridus plant in Mississippi, USA. The other species damaging the crop are C. trancatum and C. capsici, Choanphora cucurbitarum, causing Wetrot is the most troublesome disease of amaranth in Africa. Albugo bliti, causing white rust is a serious disease in Southeast Asia.

Prof. Sumer reported that in coastal areas of Peru amaranth exhibited some evidence of viral disease-perhaps because of higher humidity in these locations. No disease problems have been noticed in Guatemala. Reddy et al (1980) reported casual organism Xanthomonas amaranthicola a bacterial leaf spot disease of amaranths in India).

Sharma et al. (1981) and Naseema et al. (1983) reported that Aspergillus flauus, A. niger and Rhizopus stolonifer were externally as well as internally seed borne in most of the seeds of Amaranthus gangeticus and A. caudatus and are the major storage fungi of amaranth crop in India.

Sammons and Barnett (1987) reported that tobacco ring spot virus damaging Amaranthus hybridus in California, USA. A severe mosaic disease caused by a str. of Cucumber mosaic virus in Amaranthus caudatus was reported in Himachal Pradesh by Sharma and Chowfla (1987) damaging the crop. The other viruses affecting the crop are Ivy Vein clearing virus, alfalfa mosaic virus and beet western yellow virus.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used: 
Plant.

Uses:

  1. Plant is cooling, emollient, and mildly astringent.
  2. It is used as a pot-herb, and is said to be rich in potassium nitrate.
  3. Leaves - raw or cooked as a spinach. The leaves are used as a potherb in order to remove poison from the system.
  4. Seed - cooked. Used as a cereal substitute in cakes, porridge etc . Very small but easy to harvest and very nutritious. The seed can be cooked whole, and becomes very gelatinous like this, but it is rather difficult to crush all of the small seeds in the mouth and thus some of the seed will pass right through the digestive system without being assimilated.
  5. An edible dye is obtained from the seed capsules.

Harvesting Practices
Amaranth grains mature much earlier and the plant dries up quite late. If the heads are allowed to remain till the plant dry up, heavy shattering of grains is noticed which leads to heavy grain loss. The heads are cut when the plant is still somewhat green and start weathering and kept for sun drying for 6-7 days in the threshing yard. Threshing is done by beating. The produce is threshed and winnowed like other cereals. Unusually the harvesting is done early in the morning when the plants are somewhat wet due to night dew to avoid grain shattering in cutting heads.

Other Uses :

  2. It is also being used as astringent:
  3. A fluid extract of the plant is used as an astringent internally in the treatment of ulcerated mouths and throats, externally as a wash for ulcers and sores.
