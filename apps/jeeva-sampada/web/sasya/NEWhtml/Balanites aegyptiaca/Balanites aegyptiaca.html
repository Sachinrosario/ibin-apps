Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

B. aegyptyiaca is a thorny bush or small tree that is semi deciduous, shedding some of its leaves in the dry season. It is a slow growing, persistent tree that can live for more than 100 years. The desert date is native to the African woodlands along the southern boarder of the Sahara and is thought to have originated from the Nile Valley, it is common in the drier regions of northern Africa, but is now found in most of dryland Africa extending down to Katanga and Tanzania in the south. It is also common in Israel, the dryer parts of Pakistan, India and Arabia. In India it is often found associated with Salvadora scrub, or in association with Anogeissus pendula, Acacia catechu and in sandy and saline areas with Prosopis cineraria, Capparis decidua, and Maytenus emarginatus. It is generally found in "Desert Thorn Forest" and "Ravine Thorn Forest" ie the drier parts of penensular India, Westeren Rajasthan and from South-East Punjab to West Bengal and Sikkim. The desert date is very drought resistant requring a rainfall of 200-900 mm and it survives in temperature up to 400 C, Altitude of 0-2000 m, in India it is found at an altitude of 450 m. It prefers dark clay soils but will tolerate sandy soils that are subject to periodic flooding. Waterlogging is not tolerated and it has some sensitivity to salinity (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 11, 12, 13, 16, 17, 18)

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

The adult form is a multibranched, spiny shrub or tree up to 10-12 m m high and diameter at breast height is 45 cm. Crown spherical, in one or several distinct masses. Trunk short and often branching from near the base. Bark dark brown to grey, deeply fissured. Branches armed with stout yellow or green thorns up to 8 cm long. Leaves compound and spirally arranged on the shoots, dark green with 2 firm coriaceous leaflets; leaflets obovate, asymmetric, 2.5-6 cm long, bright green, leathery, with fine hairs when young and then woody and are upto 8 cm. (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 12, 14, 16, 19)

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Flowers are small, inconspicuous, hermaphroditic, fragrant, Green-yellow flowers and occur individually or in bunches of five. Calyx: sepals 3 mm long, pubescent outside, silky within. Petals oblong-obvate, glabrous. Disc cupular, with 10-lobed, glandular margins. Ovary silky-villous; style short, conical. The fruit is ellipsoid, narrow drupe, 2.5-7 cm long, 1.5-4 cm in diameter. Young fruits green and tormentose, turning yellow or pale brown and glabrous when mature. Pulp bitter-sweet and edible.up to 4 cm long, green. Each fruit weighs approximately 10-15 g and forms from one or several fused carpels called a drupe. (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 12, 14, 16, 19)

Breeding System :

B. aegyptyiaca is a cross pollinated crop.

Phenology :

Flowers appear in April before the rainy season and the fruits ripen and fall in December and January. The tree begins to flower and fruit at 5-7 years of age and maximum seed production is when the trees are 15-25 years old. (1, 12, 16)


Back to Top

Pollinators:

Pollinated by insects. (16)

Seed Biology :

Seeds are pyrene (stone) is 1.5-3 cm long, light brown, fibrous and extremely hard. It makes up 50-60% of the fruit. There are 500-1500 dry, clean seeds per kg. And the seeds are dispersed by ingestion by birds (e.g. hornbills) and larger animals (baboons, ruminants). Seed storage behaviour is orthodox and moisture content for storage should be 6-10%. Cleaned, dried and free of insects, the seeds will remain viable for about 1 year at ambient temperature, 2 years at cool temperatures and several years in hermetic storage at 30 C. (1, 16)

Seedling Germination :

Seeds that have passed the digestive tract of ruminants are said to germinate well without pretreatment. Fresh seeds need no pretreatment but seeds that have been stored will normally need manual scarification. Soaking in hot water for 12-18 hours, soaking in water for 24 hours at room temperature or boiling for 7-10 minutes and left to cool in the water are methods that have been reported to improve germination.

Germination: Germination rates are quite high and occurs in 1-4 weeks and the seedlings are kept in the nursery for about 12 weeks. Germination per centage is 6. (12, 16).

Cultivation:

Propagation: The desert date can be grown from seed or from cuttings.

Seed Treatments: Fruit turns from green to yellow when ripe, each containing 1 pit. These can be stored for up to a year if kept air dry and insect free. When ready to plant, soak the fruit overnight in lukewarm water until the pulp can be removed and the pit extracted. Recommended pretreatments include: intestinal scarification; boiling 7 to 10 minutes and cooling; soaking 12 to 18 hours in hot water; soaking for 24 hours in warm water; and soaking overnight in warm water.


Seedling Management: Does not withstand transplanting well because of the deep tap root. For best results plant in a container with the seed vertical (stem end down). Plants should remain in the nursery for 18 to 24 weeks before outplanting at the beginning of the rainy season. Because of the vigorous tap root, direct sowing at the end of the dry season is recommended. Average rooting success from stem cuttings is about 60 to 70%. Seeds passed through the intestinal tract of ruminants germinate particularly well and can be gathered where livestock are kept overnight.


Growth Cycle: Slow growing but very resilient. Fruit and foliage appear at the height of the dry season. It produces seed in August and September. The first fruit is harvested between years 5 and 8 with the yield increasing until year 25. It can live to more than 100 years.


Management Systems: Requires weeding and protection from browsing up to the initial fruiting period (at least 3 years). Weeding is important due to slow growth, as high grass can compete for light. Weeds can also impede regeneration and grass fires can destroy young plants.

It coppices vigorously. Roots spread far, and throw up suckers at a considerable distance from the trunk (1, 6, 11, 12, 14, 16, 17).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

The seeds are commonly attacked by borers. It can be controlled by short boiling (which may also serve as a pre-treatment before storage), insecticides or storage in Carbon di oxide, may kill present insects and further attack is prevented by storage in air-tight containers. (12, 16)

Economic Potential and Uses

The fat content of the kernels is between 40-60%. It was used in perfumery in ancient times. Today, it is used for cooking. The kernels produce edible oil used for cooking and lubricant. Oil is reported to be used in the treatment of skin diseases, burns, excoriations and also freckles. It is slightly aperiant. It can also be used in the production of soap. The oil extraction process produces an oilcake suitable for animal feed. It has a high protein (36.8%) and low fibre content (5.9%) (1, 6, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18).

Back to Top

Parts used:

Fruits, leaves, bark, roots, shoots, resins, trunk and seeds.

Uses:

Seeds: Seed kernels are used for cooking oil and medicines.

Fruits: The fruit pulp though bitter, is edible. It produces fruit even in dry years which makes it a highly appreciated food source in dry areas. Pounded fruits make a refreshing drink which becomes alcoholic if left to ferment.

Wood: B. aegyptiaca has fine-grained dense and heavy heartwood, it is easily worked and takes a good polish. Although valued for furniture it may be twisted and difficult to saw. The wood is durable and resistant to insects making it good for tool handles and domestic items such as spoons. The wood is good for firewood and high quality charcoal. The stem of the tree contains steroidal saponins which have been shown to have an insect antifeedant and molluscicide properties.

Leaves: Protein rich leaves and shoots are an excellent source of fodder. The leaves make very good mulch and the tree is nitrogen fixing, it is also valued as firewood since it produces almost no smoke and has a calorific value of 4600 kcal per kg. In the periods of food shortage people cut the newly growing succulent shoots with the leaves and cook and eat them like cabbage.

Fruits, leaves, bark, roots, shoots, resins, trunk and seeds all have medicinal uses.

Root cuttings readily form a live fence (1, 6, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18).

Harvesting Practices

Fruits are harvested when they turn yellow and the flesh becomes soft and sweet. In areas with abundant hornbills, fruits will not persist long on the tree. Discharged stones can be collected under the trees, but they are often prone to insect (seed borer) attack. Consequently, usually only a fraction of the fruits can be collected. Due to the prolonged fruiting season several collections is be necessary. The fruits are collected by spreading a tarpaulin under the tree and shaking the branches until the fruits are released. Climbing is inconvenient because of the long branch thorns (16).

Produce and Products

The Balanites tree is used locally for many products: the wood is used for making tools and furniture, the fruit for sweets and alcoholic beverages, and the kernels for cooking oil and medicines. The stem of the tree contains steroidal saponins which have been shown to have an insect antifeedant and molluscicide properties. Oil cake is used as animal feed.

Processing Techniques

PRE-TREATMENT

The outer fruit pulp must be removed as soon as possible to avoid fermentation. If extraction of seed is not possible in the field, the fruits should be kept dry and spread in a thin layer during temporary field storage. The fruit pulp can be removed after soaking the fruits in water. Alternatively the fruits may be fed to livestock (cattle) and the stones picked from the droppings. The latter method will kill possible insects in the seeds. After extraction the stones are dried in the sun before storage.

The nuts are shelled to release the kernel. Shelling has been noted as a problem, one solution that has been used to improve efficiency is to simply saw the nuts in half to release the kernel.

Every ton of whole fruit processed yields half a ton of hard woody shell which is highly combustible and produces a high quality charcoal.


OIL EXTRACTION

Oil is obtained by simple cold expression methods.

The kernel yields a highly stable, golden yellow oil suitable for cooking.

The oil extraction processes produces oilcake suitable for animal feed (17, 18).

Other Uses

The fruits have been used in the treatment of liver and spleen diseases. An emulsion made from the fruit or bark is lethal to fresh water snails that are the host of miracidal and cercaria stages of bilharzia flukes, schistosomiasis and to a water flea that acts as a host to the guinea worm. The roots are used for abdominal pains and as a purgative. Decoction of root is used to treat malaria. Roots boiled in soup used against oedema and stomach pains. Roots are used as an emetic. The bark is used for soar throats and as a remedy for sterility, mental diseases, epilepsy, yellow fever, syphilis, heartburn and toothaches. Gum from the wood is mixed with maize meal porridge to treat chest complaints.

It also has important pesticidal properties, a solution from the fruit is lethal to the guinea worm and mollusca, and therefore can be used to treat drinking water. This solution can also kill fish which are then safe to eat (1, 6, 8, 11, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19).

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

The economic value of Balanites aegyptiaca is the extraction of oil from the kernel, in addition, the fruit pulp is eaten raw or made into cakes and also used for the preparation of an alcoholic drink. Its leaves were used as herbs and the wood was used in the manufacture of ships. The main potential for industrial utilization of Balanites lies in extraction of the group of chemicals known as saponins.

The deep rooted Balanites tree lives for more than 100 years, for 75 years annually producing crop of 125 kg of ripe fruit or 10,000 fruits per year which equals about 100-150 kg, or 55-80 kg of seed (12, 15, 18).

Export Potential

Balanos oil as well as balanites seeds are being traded in small quantities in the international markets. In early 2002, 100ml of the oil was retailing at $20 in the USA. The entrepreneurs could start businesses to process the oil for local and/or international markets (17).
