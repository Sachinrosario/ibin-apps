 Other Biological Details
HABIT & HABITAT
It is of shrubs and small trees distributed in drier parts of Mysore, Coimbatore and Salem districts & is extended South to Madura & north to Kolhapu, also in south India, parts of Malaysia, Australia, New Zealand and Polynesia, extending up to Hawaiian archipelago and also introduced elsewhere. The tree is highly branched and not more than 3 mtrs.  Sometimes after effective thinning operations, the height of unbranched stem may grow above 3 mtrs. The trees are root parasites of grasses, herbs, shrubs or trees. In South India both wood and oil find their use in incense, perfumes and medicine. Apart from this, wood is put into use in carving.
Sandal trees grow mainly on ferruginous loam overlying metamorphic rocks chiefly gneiss. They can tolerate shallow, rocky, ground and stony or gravelly soils avoiding saline or calcareous, soils and not exacting to depth of soil. It is not extracting to depth of soil. It is not found in black soils. The trees grown on poor soils produce sandal wood giving a better yield of oil. The tree flourishes best between altitudes of 600 and 1050 m though it may go up to 1350 m and descend as low as 360 m. The formation of heartwood will be at its best at an altitude of 600 to 900 m and rain fall of 85-135 cm.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
It starts flowering from an early age of 3-4 years but good seeds can be obtained from them when they are 20 years old. The formation of heart wood commences when the tree is about 10 years and it continues. The heart wood formation is maximum when the tree is between 30 and 70 years of age attaining a girth of 40-60 cm. The trees with a girth of 60 cm or above are harvested by uprooting and not by cutting. After uprooting, the tree top and branches which have no heart wood are chopped off. Dressing and removal of sapwood is done in the depot. The yield of heart wood varies from locality and the age of the tree. Trees with a girth of 50-60 cm yield 19.5 kg heart wood in North Coimbatore, 38.0 kg in Salem and 21.7 kg in Sirsi.

Cultivation:  
Seed beds of size 10 m x 1 m are prepared with sand and red earth in the ratio of 2:1. Nematicides in the.' form of Ekalux or Thimet at 500 g per bed is mixed with the soil. The seeds at the rate of 2.5 kg is then spread uniformly over the bed covered with, 2 cms of sand, watered and mulched with straw. The straw is removed when leaves appear. To prevent fungal infections the beds are sprayed with 0.25% Dithane once in 15 days. Application of 0.02% Ekalux solution at the rate of 0.02% once in a month avoids nematode attack. The beds art watered once or twice a day depending on the climatic conditions. Seedlings at 4-6 leaf stage are transplanted to polybags (size 30 x 14 cm) containing sand, red earth and farm yard manure with ratio of 1:1:1 along with one or two seeds of red grain {Cajanus cajan}. The seedlings of Cajanus serve as primary host. After transplanting seedlings are kept in shade for a week, weeded and watered regularly with care to avoid excess moisture. Host plants are pruned periodically to check their growth so that they do not hamper the growth of Sandal seedlings. To avoid Nematode attack, 2 g of Ekalux is also applied per polybag. The polybags should not be kept in one place for more than two months to avoid roots penetrating the soil.
Transplanting :Healthy Sandal seedlings having a height of about 30 cm are planted in pits of 50 cm3 size at a spacing of 3 m x 3m at the onset of monsoon. Miscellaneous secondary forestry species are planted either in the same pit or separate pits in a quincunx pattern. Some of the species which have proved as good hosts for Sandal are Casuanna equisetifolia, Acacia nilofica, Pongamia pinnata, Cassia siamea, Alk'izia lebbeck and Wrighlia tinctoria. Direct sowing
(i) Dibbling seeds into bushes
This method is adopted in open scrub jungles with lot of bushes. Seeds are sown during monsoon usually with the help of a holiow bamboo pole of 4-6 cm diameter, 1.5 m length and having a hollow metal piece attached at one end. The pole is introduced at the base of bushes, soil is raked and through the hole 4-5 seeds are transferred.
(ii) Trench mound technique
This technique involves sowing Sandal seeds mixed with seeds of other species preferably those which act as hosts for Sandal on the mounds of trenches (3 m x 30 cm size). These trenches are generally aligned along contours in rows of 6 m apart with a distance of 3 m end to end, between one trench to another along the same row.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Wood, Seed.

Uses

  1. The wood is bitter; cooling, dry; exhilarating, alexiteric, antipyretic, aphrodisiac, useful in heart diseases; "Kapha", thirst, biliousness, burning sensation, cold in the head, strangury, bronchitis, vaginal discharges, smallpox (Ayurveda).
  2. It is tonic to the heart and the brain; aastringent to bowels, laxative; useful in inflammations, gleet, gonorrhoea, headahe (Yunani).
  3. The wood ground up with water into a paste, is commonly applied to local inflammations, to the temples in fevers, and to skin diseases to allay heat and pruritus. It also acts as a diaphoretic.
  4. In cases of morbid thirst, the powder of the wood is recomended to be taken in coconut water.
  5. The seeds contain an oil which is used in skin diseases. Oil is also good remedy for gonorrhoea.
