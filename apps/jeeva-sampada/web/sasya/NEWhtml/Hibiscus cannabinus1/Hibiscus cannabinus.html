Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
The plant is herbaceous in nature and is apparently indigenous to India, some consider it to be a native of tropical Africa, where numerous types are found growing wild. In India, it has been under cultivation since long and is found distributed throughout the country up to an elevation of 3,000 ft. in the lower Himalayas. The plant is cultivated mainly as a fiber crop in the drier tracts of Deccan, comprising Andhra Pradesh, parts of Karnataka, Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, and Bihar. In other areas, it is cultivated on a limited scale, as a supplementary crop in mixture with others. The important centres of cultivation for fiber purposes are Srikakulam, Visakhapatnam, Krishna and Guntur districts in Andhra; Coimbatore and Arcot in Tamil Nadu; Belgaum, Bijapur and Dharwar in Karnataka. Outside India, particularly in countries where conditions for jute cultivation are not favourable, attempts have been made to cultivate the plant.

HS and HC denote varieties. A warm and humid climate suits both HS and HC, but both grow in drier rainfed areas, the latter being more drought-resistant. In areas with 500-900 mm of rainfall, HC suits better by virtue of its shorter duration and faster growth. Neither of them can stand prolonged water-logging. Heavy, continuous rains and low temperature are harmful. It thrives best in well-drained, sandy loams soil, neutral in reaction and containing considerable quantity of humus it does not grow under water logged conditions. The alluvial soils, medium black soil of the Deccan and the red loam and laterite soils of South India are well suited for cultivation (1, 5)

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
An erect woody to herbaceous annual with straight, slender, glabrous or prickly stem, 8-12 Ft. high. Flowers axillary, large, 3-4 inches in diam., yellow with crimson centre, capsules globose, pointed, bristly, seeds large, brown, nearly glabrous. The cultivated forms are 2.5-4 m tall, with well-developed tap-roots. Stems straight and slender, green, red or purple. Leaves alternate, petioles long, sometimes prickly, red or green, lamina variable in shape and colour, in some cultivas all leaves are cordate, in others all deeply divided, in others lower leaves cordate with upper leaves deeply palmately 5-7-lobed, lobes oblong-lanceolate, sinuous, dentate. Root a deep-penetrating taproot with deep-seated laterals. (1, 4).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Flowers solitary, large to 10 cm in diameter, short-stalked, axillary, yellow with purple centers, sepals 5, petals 5, stamens numerous, connate, ovary superior, fruit a many-seeded, hairy capsule about 1 cm long. Epicalyx of 5-10 linear bracteoles less than half length of sepals, calyx persistent, bristly, connate below with prominent gland on mid-rib, tips lanceolate, petals 5, thin, spreading, pale yellow, often with crimson spot at base, staminal column with short filaments and small one celled anthers, ovary superior of 5 united carpels, style after emerging from staminal tube tipped with 5 stigmas (2, 4).

Breeding System :
Flowers usually open before daybreak and begin to close about midday. The stigmas do not emerge from the staminal tube until the anthers begin to burst. Self-pollination occurs when the corolla closes. Some cultivars are entirely self-pollinated, but up to 4 per cent cross-pollination has been observed in others (4).

Phenology :
It requires short days for flowering. Flowering commences towards the end of the monsoon period (October-November) when the length of day begins to decline.

Pollinators:
Self-pollination occurs.

Seed Biology :
Seeds numerous and are tetrahedral in form with a strongly adherent coat difficult to separate from the kernel. Seeds are brown, glabrous, wedge-shaped, 5 mm long, 3 mm wide, weight 25g/1000 seeds (2).

Seedling Germination :
Seeds germinate in 3-10 days, depending on soil moisture and temperature. Seed retain viability for about 8 months under ordinary storage conditions. They may germinate in 72 hours, but take longer at low temperatures (4).

Cultivation:

H. Cannabinus is tropical crop, thriving best in a humid climate with a temperature range of 60-80°F. during the growing season. It requires a rainfall of 20-25 in. distributed evenly over a period of 4-5 months, followed by a dry period. It is rather sensitive to frost and the limits of its cultivation lie between 45°N and 300 S latitudes.

Besides temperature and rainfall, the length of day also influences the growth and maturation of the plant. Longer days favour rapid vegetative growth and flowering starts when the length of day begins to decrease. This photoperiodic influence is not a limiting factor in India and the cultivation period in most producing areas coincides with the long summer days.


Soil is prepared in much the same way as in the case of jute. HS varieties when sown broadcast, requires 12 to 17 kg of seed per hectare. Seedlings, when 10-12 cm tall, are manually thinned in two installments to a spacing of 15-18 cm between plants. When raised in rows, seed is drilled 30 cm apart, the seed requirement being 10-12.5 kg per hectare. Seedlings are manually thinned to 12-15 cm apart in the rows. A minor adjustment in spacing is permissible on the basis of the fertility of the soil, rainfall and the nature of the soil. Variety 'HS 4288' (stem with bristles) and 'HS 7910' (stem without bristles) is sown best in mid-April to mid-May, roughly north of 22oN, early sowing giving better yield. Variety 'AMV 1' is suitable for areas south of 22oN and can be sown closer to shade the surface of the soil to conserve moisture (22.5 cm X 10 cm) and can be sown till June as in Andhra Pradesh. Dry sowing is possible in anticipation of rain-fall, specially where drizzles do not precede the actual monsoon rains. Variety 'HC 583' requires 17-22 kg of seed per hectare for broadcast sowing and 15-17 kg for line-sowing (30 cm X 12-15 cm). April sowings are best for yield and quality.

Both HS and HC compete well with usual weeds and, unlike jute, can permit delayed weeding. However, two weedings and thinnings are extremely useful, specially when done timely. The use of a wheel-hoe in the row crop, at least twice, within 45 days of sowing leads to better crop.

Promising Varieties: The non-bristled stem, equals 'HS 4288' in yield and the cultivators prefer it to the bristled types. 'HC 867' has excelled 'HS 583' in yield at places and possesses better resistance to the spiral borer, Agrillus acutus (1, 5, 6).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

Among the insect pests reported, particular mention may be made of Agrilus acutus, which produces an elongated gall on the stem. Endrin EC 20 (0.04) is effective, but is to be replaced by Methyl Parathion. The fiber from damaged plants is useless. Seeds in storage are affected by Spermophagus tessellatus.

Root-knot nematode, causes dwarfing and chlorosis in extreme cases. Its control, with chemicals is costly, therefore rotation is advocated.


Diseases:

A large number of diseases caused by fungi are reported from various countries. The more serious diseases recorded in India are dry root rot caused by Macrophomina phaseoli, leaf spot by Cercospora hibisci, leaf blight by Phyllosticta hibisci and stem rot by Diplodia hibiscina. Recently a brown rot caused by Volutella sp. has been recorded on most types grown in India. In Russia, a large number of fungi belonging to the genera Alternaria, Fusarium, Penicillium and Trichothecium have been found on seeds. Fusarium affect seed tissues and consequently germination (1, 5).

Economic Potential and Uses

The oil is used as a lubricant and for lighting. It is suitable for soaps and for the manufacture of linoleum, paints and varnishes and for illumination. Refined oil is edible and may be used as salad oil and for cooking purposes. The cake left after oil extraction is used as fertilizer. Fiber is extracted from the plant which is suitable for the manufacture of paper and pulp. It is widely used in the manufacture of rope, cordage, fishing nets, gunny bags etc.

Parts used

Seeds, plants, leaves

Uses

It is mainly cultivated for its best fibers which resemble and substitute for jute fibers. Fiber strands, 1.5–3 m long, are used for canvas, sacking, carpet backing. Tender leaves of H. cannabinus are used as cattle fodder, especially for cows and buffaloes in milk. Seeds are used as feed for cattle and poultry. They are sometimes used for human consumption after roasting and crushing. They are stomachic, appetising, aphrodisiac and fattening, they are also employed externally as poultice for pains and bruises (2, 10).

Harvesting Practices
The crop is ready for harvesting 3-5 months after the sowing. For fibre purposes, the harvesting is done at the flowering stage before seed setting; if delayed, the fibre obtained is coarse with little lustre. The plants are cut close to the ground with a sickle or pulled out by the roots, tied into bundles of 30 to 40 stalks, left on the field for a few days to dry and then steeped in water for retting. Retted bundles are removed and the bark peeled off from the root upwards. The strips are gently beaten with a mallet or stick and rinsed in water to separate the fibre from adhering tissue, The clean fibre is washed, dried in the sun and made into bundles or hanks for marketing.

For seed production plants grown for fibre give a low seed yield. They are tall and seeds are difficult to collect. For seed purposes, late planting in July-August with wider spacing is advocated so that a crop of stunted growth with profuse branches is raised. The seeds start ripening from the lowest capsule upwards in succession and harvesting is done when the capsules in the middle tier become mature. The seeds are threshed out after drying by beating and cleaned by winnowing. The average yield of seed is 700-800 lb. per acre and even yields up to 1 ton per acre have been recorded (1).

Produce and Products

Oil is obtained from the seeds, which is used for cooking, soap manufacture. Plants are used for fibre extraction, which is used in the manufacture of paper, ropes, cordage, bags, sacks, floor matting. Stalks left after fibre extraction is used as fuel.

Processing Techniques

Oil can be extracted from the seeds by milling, cooking and hydraulic pressing. For better yields solvent extraction is suitable.

Other Uses

Hibiscus is reported to be anodyne, aperitif, aphrodisiac, fattening, purgative, and stomachic, kenaf is a folk remedy for fever and puerperium. The leaves are considered aperient. The leaf infusion is used for coughs. Ayurvedics use the leaves for dysentery and bilious, blood and throat disorders. Powdered leaves are applied to Guinea worms in Africa. In Gambia, the leaf infusion is used for coughs. Seeds are applied externally to aches. Peelings from the stems are used for anemia, fatigue, lassitude, etc. Medicinally, juice of the flowers with sugar and black pepper is used in biliousness with acidity. The juice of the flowers mixed with sugar and black pepper is a remedy for biliousness (1, 2, 3, 9).

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

India is the largest producer with about 850,000 acres producing about 633 million lb annually. It has valued more as fibre crop than the oil crop. The area under mesta has been increasing since 1952 and is around 0.24 million hectares, of which more than 80 per cent is under HS types. The annual average production is around 1.08 million bales (1 bale=181kg). Within the jutebelt, the yield of mesta is high in West Bengal, Bihar, Assam and Tripura. Outside the jute belt, the highest acreage is in Andhra Pradesh and the areas assuming importance are Dandakaranya, Orissa, eastern Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Karnataka and Uttar Pradesh. Other states grow these types over small areas, mostly for home consumption. Being similar to jute in spinning characteristics, Mesta is used on fairly large scale by the jute manufacturers. Consumption of Mesta by Indian mills received stimulus, largely due to its cheapness compared to jute.

Export Potential

In the earlier days, bimli fibre obtained exclusively from H. cannabinus was being exported in small quantities under the name Deccan Hemp or Bimli patam Jute, the main importing countries being are U.K., France and Germany, where the fibre was used for mixing with jute (1)
