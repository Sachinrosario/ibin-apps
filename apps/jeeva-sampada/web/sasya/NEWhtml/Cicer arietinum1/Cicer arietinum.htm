Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:                                                               
 An erect or spreading much branched annual herb, 30-50 cm height. It is considered to have originated in the tracts lying between the  Caucasus and the Himalayas, from where it it spread in to South Europe, Iran, Egypt and India. It is grown mainly in India, Pakistan, Myanmar, Bangladesh and also in Ethiopia, Mexico, Spain, Morocco, Turkey and Iran. In India Madhya Pradesh has the largest area under this crop followed by Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh, Haryana and Maharashtra.

Cultivation:
Climate: It is exclusively cultivated as rabi crop throughout India, especially in northern states. However, severe cold and frost are in the Himalayan region above 500m. It is primarily a crop of low rainfall areas but gives good yield under irrigated conditions as well.
Soil:It is grown an a wide range of soil types ranging from sandy loam to clayey loam. This is reflected in various types of plant habit. the same cultivar varying a great deal when grown on different soils. In northern India, it is grown ion moderately heavy, grey and brown alluvial soils of the upper Gangetic basin. In Maharastra and Deccan plateau, black cotton soils as well as some red soils are utilized. In western parts of Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan lighter soils, mostly sandy loams, are used successfully for gram cultivation.
Preparation of land: Gram does not require thoroughly prepared bed. A coarse tilth surface; a fine tilth even suppresses egrmination. Ploghing is done one to four times depending upon the soil. As chickpea is a deep rooting species, the plant grows well in good depth. The depth of ploghing varies; deep tillage is recommended for comapct and badly aerated soils, also to reduce the incidence of wilt disease. Loosening the siol to the depth of 25-30 cm has remarkable effect on reducing incidence of wilt disease and increasing yield.
Seeds and Sowing:To control seed borne diseases, seeds should be treated before sowing with Captan or Thiram at 2.5 g/kg seed or with Bavistin 2 g/kg seed. Seeds with good germination capacity should be used. Seeds are sown by placing them in furrows or by broad casting.
Sowign time: The planting time has profound effect on cultivation of gram. In India soqwing time extends from late late September to mid December or later, the most successful period being October. Third and fourth week of October is the best time for sowing in the semi arid northern plains, where as crop should be sown during first fortnight of October in Central India. In the peninsular zone where the crop is grown under rainfed conditions, the ideal time for sowing is the second fortnight of October. For humid sub-tropical conditions os terai the first fortnight of November is the right time for gram planting.
Seed rate: In broadcast chickpea the seed rate varies according to local tradition, but does not differ much from that in row planting. In general, by this method slightly more seed is needed and this is co-related with the specific weight of seeds of a variety. According to size, a seed rate of 75-80 kg/ha is sufficient for bold seeded desi varieties and 40-50 kg/ha for small seeded desi varieies, whereas for kabuli varieties 90-100 kg/ha seed rate is needed.
Irrigation:Gram is primarily grown as rainfed crop. Plantlets emerge about 10-12 days after sowing and depending upon the winter rains, the crop is irrigated once or twice, especially the Kabuli varieties, as these are not as drought resistnat as desi varieties.
Weeding: Crop yeild losses due to weeds have been estimated from 30-50 per cent. Hand weeding at 30 and 60 daya after planting has been very effective in controlling weeds.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES
Pests:
 Compared to other grain legumes gram suffers from remarkably few insect pests problems due to secretion of an extremely acidic exudate.
Gram pod borer: The most damaging of the pests, the gram caterpillar Helicoverpa armigera (Hubner) is a cosmopolitan polyphagus insect living on many major crops. The pest occur throughout the gram growing areas.
Gujhia weevil: Tanymecus indicus Fausthas been reported from many states as a sporadic pest.
Gram cutworm or Greasy cutworm:Agrotis ypsilon Rottl. and A. flammatra (Schiff.) besides Euxoa spinifera (Huli) cause considerable damage to gram.
Gram semilooper: Plusia orichalcea Fabr., P. nigrisigna Walker, Autographa nigrisigna Walker are also reported on gram from several states.
Chickpea podfly: Leriomyza obtusa M. is reported from some regions. The larvae feed on seeds and young leaves.
Aphids:Aphis crasssivora Koch infests the plants throughout the country, but usually on late sown crops, particularly in Uttar Pradesh, Haryana and Punjab.
Pulse beetles: Gram pulse or dhal is succeptible to attack by a number of beetles resulting in 15 % damage to stored pulse.

Diseases:
  It is attacked by several diseases during different stages of growth, the most important being wilt and blight.
Wilt: Predomimant among the wilts and roots is Fusarium wilt caused by F. oxysporum f. sp. ciceri (Padwick) Snyder & Hansen, known as Ukehra or Soka in Punjab, this disease is soil borne. It attacks the root system which turns black and ultimately dries up. Typical symptoms are sudden dropping of leaves and blach internal discolouration of roots. The development of disease is influenced by soil type, pH, organic matter content, soil humidity and soil temperature.
Dry root rot: Caused by Rhizactonia bataticola is more serious in central and southerm India where the crop is under higher temperature (30°C) in the post flowering stage.
Wet root rot: Caused by Rhizactonia solani is more prevalent in the seedlimg stage.
Collar rot: Caused by Sclerotium rolfsi is associuated with is high soil moisture content, presence of undecomposed orghanic matter near the soil surface, low soil pH and temperature of 28-30°. Normally the disease is at seedling stage, but in the irrigated crop it can occur at any stage provided temperature is not low.
Stem rot: Caused by Sclerotinia sclerotiorum is prevalent in Punjab, Haryana and Uttar Pradesh in India. It is more serious in where cool weather, Relatively more rain and heavy dew occur.
Foot rot:Caused by Opeculella padwickii is also soil borne disease. The infected plants show drying from top downwards. Leaves turn yellow and finally brown. The disease has been reported from Noethern and Central India.
Ascochyta blight:Among the diseases of gram, more serious cause of crop loss is blight caused by Ascochyta rabiei (Pass)Labr.
Rust: It is caused by Uromyces cicerisarietinum (Grogn.) Jacz. & Beyer. It is common in North India in hilly and sub montane regions and is considered the second most wide spread disease of gram. It appears chiefly on the undersurface of the leaflets as small, round or oval, brown postules which tend to coalesce. Occasionally postules occur on stem and pods too. The severly infected plants dry prematurely.
Powdery mildew:Caused by endophytic fungi, Levillula taurica (Lev.) Arn. and Oidiopsis taurica (Lev.) Salmon. Itis reported from central India.
Viral and Bacterial diseases: Some viral diseases reported from India are Chickpea stunt caused by Pealeaf Roll virus. Other viral diseases, Alfalfa Mosaic Virus (AMV) and Cucumber Mosaic Virus have also been reported.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Seeds, Tender shoots and leaves,

Uses

  • Split, dehusked seed or dhal consisting of only the cotyledons is the main form in which chickpea or gram is consumed in India. The flour of the dhal called besan or Gram meal is also used in Indian cuisine in numerous ways.
  • The tender shoots and leaves are used as vegeatble. They are prepared as spinach, boiled in soups and currries or fried with spices as a side dish.


Harvesting Practices
The crop matures in 95-170 days after sowing depending on variety grown. It matures in about 150 days in Punjab and Uttar Pradesh and 120 days or less in Deccan and further south. Harvesting is done when most of the leaves and pods have turned brown. Dry crop should not be allowed to remain in the field as shattering of pods result in great loss. The crop is either uprooted or often cut with a sickle, so that the roots of the plant are left behind to enrich the soil. The plants may also be pulled up in the green stage when the seeds are used as green vegetable or as a snack. The harvested palnts are dried for about a week and threshed by tampling them under the feet of bullocks or by beating them with sticks. Threshing machines are also being used in some states. Winnowing is done traditionally by wind, seiving and fanning is also done.
Yield:The average yield of the rain-fed is 500-600 kg of grain and 800-1,000 kg of dry fodder per hectare, being the highest in Punjab and the lowest in southern India. under irrigated conditions yields of 1,500 to 2,000 kg of grain per hectare are quite common. While yields up to 3,000 kg per hectare or above have been obtained under favourable conditions.

Produce and Products
Gram dhal, besan (flour)
