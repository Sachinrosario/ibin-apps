Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Mimusops elengi L. is a small to large evergreen tree found in the Deccan Peninsula and Andaman Islands and frequently cultivated in gardens for ornamental purposes, it is grown also as an avenue or shade tree throughout the greater part of India. The tree attains large dimensions in the moist evergreen forests of western ghats, in the eastern ghats, it is found in dry areas, often on laterite, and is comparatively small in size. In Andamans it attains a height up to 35 m: with a bole 6 m. long x 2.5 m. girth. In its natural habitat the obsolute maximum shade temperature varies from 950 to 1150 F., the obsolute minimum from 500-620 F and the normal rainfall from 30-150 inch or more (7, 8, 9).

Availability :  

Status : Wild

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Mimusops elengi L. is a small or large sized tree with a dense crown of shiny coriaceous leaves. Bark dark grey and fissured. Stem errect, terete or slightly compressed, wiry, green, young parts pubescent, older parts glabrous internodes 0.4-1 inch long. Leaves simple, alternate, evergreen, leaves stipulate. Petiole 0.1-0.5 inch long, flattened above, lamina 1.7-2.3 inch by 0.5-0.8 inch elliptical lanceolate, acute or acuminate. Fruit is an ovoid orange-yellow one seeded berry, about 1 inch long (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10, 11, 12).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Flowers are small, creamy white, star-shaped and fragrant. Flowers solitary, or aggregated in inflorescences; in cymes, or in panicles. The terminal inflorescence unit cymose. Inflorescences axillary (sometimes on old stems); cymose bunches. Flowers bracteolate; small (often), or medium-sized; regular (usually), or somewhat irregular to very irregular; cyclic; tetracyclic to polycyclic. Perianth with distinct calyx and corolla; 2 whorled, or 3 whorled; Calyx 4-8; 1 whorled or 2 whorled. Corolla 3–6, or 8, or 10; 1 whorled, or 2 whorled. Androecium exclusively of fertile stamens to including staminodes. Staminodes 2-5; when present, external to the fertile stamens; petaloid, or non-petaloid. Stamens 4–15; isomerous with the perianth, or diplostemonous, or triplostemonous; opposite the corolla members, or both alternating with and opposite the corolla members. Pollen grains aperturate; 3–6 aperturate; colporate; 2-celled, or 3-celled. Gynoecium 4–14 carpelled. Carpels isomerous with the perianth, or increased in number relative to the perianth. Gynoecium syncarpous; superior. Ovary locular. Gynoecium stylate. Styles 1; attenuate from the ovary; apical. Stigmas 1; dry type; papillate. Ovules 1 per locule; ascending; hemianatropous to anatropous; unitegmic; tenuinucellate (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10, 11, 12).

Breeding System :

Phenology :
The fragrant white star-shaped flowers appear from February to April and the fruits ripen the following year from February to June or later (7, 8).

Pollinators:

Seed Biology :
The seeds are elliptical, compressed, 0.6-0.9 inch by 0.4-0.5 inch, brown, smooth, shining with a hard testa and a soft whitish albumen. The seeds retain their viability long (8).

Back to Top

Seedling Germination :
Germination is epigeous. The hard testa splits exposing the albumen, the radicle emerges, the hypocotyl elongates by arching and in straightening carries the cotyledons above the ground. The primary root is long and tapering. The growth of the seedlings is slow, a maximum height of about 3 inch attained by the end of the first season (8).

Back to Top

Cultivation:

The tree is a shade bearer and appears to reproduce well under shade. It is best propagated by sowing seeds singly in baskets and planting out seedlings in the field, usually after two years in the rainy season. The rate of growth is slow. It is cultivated in gardens as ornamental plant. It is also grown as a avenue or shade tree throughout the greater part of India (7, 8, 12).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

Arrhenothrips ramakrishnae causes or induces fold galls on the leaves of Mimusops elengi (22).

Diseases:

Seedlings of Mimusops elengi were known to be affected with collar rot disease, the disease causes about 20% mortality. Development of a discolored area at the collar region of the seedlings was the first symptom of the disease. Later, the collar region decayed leading to wilting and death of seedlings within a week of the appearance of the initial symptoms. Two species of Cylindrocladium (C. quinqueseptatum [Calonectria quinqueseptata] and Cylindrocladium camelliae) were responsible fro the disease. The tree is also susceptible to the attack of a wound fungus, Fornes senex, which affects the wood, the heartwood is ultimately destroyed and affected trees are liable to be dislodged by wind (7, 19).

Economic Potential and Uses

The seed kernels yield 16-25% of a fatty oil, used for edible and lighting purposes. The crude oil has a reddish brown colour with an unpleasant odour and is more or less tasteless. The refined oil is almost colourless and odourless but it acquires a yellow tint on exposure to air. Not much is known of the flower and its fragrance outside of India. The essential oil of Mimusops elengi flower is a pale yellow, mobile liquid of very delicate, sweet and extremely tenacious floral odour. A honeylike heavy-sweet undertone, is quite persistent, and this essential oil could, if it were made regularly available, certainly find uses as a modifier of countless floral fragrances (7, 8, 9, 13, 14, 18).

Parts used

Bark, flower, fruit and seed

Uses

The ripe fruit is edible, it is sometimes used for making preserves and pickles. The tree is lopped for fodder. Young twigs are used for cleaning teeth. The caducous corollas of flowers which fall in showers from the tree are valued for making garlands and sometimes for stuffing pillows. They retain their fragrance long after drying. An infusion of the flowers is used as a cosmetic after bath. The flowers are also used in distilling an otto used in perfumes and as stimulant. The bark contains 3-7% tannin, it is used in some parts of India for dyeing and tanning purposes. The bark and flowers are reported to contain a saponin and an alkaloid. The tree also yields a gum. Bulletwood is used for building purposes, piles, bridges, boats, oars, masts, spars, agricultural implements, carts, rice pounders, crushers and oil mills. It is also used for furniture, cabinet work, panels, marline spikes, belaying pins, tools, turnery, picture frames, musical instruments and walking sticks. It has been recommended for wooden bearings. Saponins from Mimusops elengi and M. littoralis seeds and crude extract of Ammi majus were 86-100% effective against Phytophthora palmivora in vitro and the saponins were 100% effective against Colletotrichum capsici. (7, 8, 12, 13, 20, 23).

Harvesting Practices
Seeds are collected from the well grown trees after June-July. Freshly fallen fruits are collected from the ground, the pulp is removed immediately and the seeds dried in sun. There is no organised way of seed collection or production.

Produce and Products

Fruits are edible. Fatty oil is obtained from the seeds. Flowers are used for garland making. Wood is valued as timber. Leaves are fed to livestock. Apart from that plant have lot of medicinal value.

Processing Techniques

On a commercial scale oil is obtained from the seed by expression or by extraction by solvents the former being a common practice in the villages. There are three traditional methods of expressing oil from seed. (1) Boiling method (2) Chappa method and (3) Bullock Ghani method. In boiling method the depulped seed is boiled in water for some time and then dried. Thereafter it is roasted on a hot pan until the shell become crisp and friable. The whole seed is crushed into powder and it is boiled in water for some time after which the oil floating at the top water is separated by decantation. In the Chappa method the powdered kernel is steamed over a vessel of boiling water and pressed between the two limbs of chappa. In bullock ghani method the powdered kernel is sprinkled with little hot water and pressed in a Bullock-Ghani (9).

Other Uses
A decoction of the astringent bark or flower is taken to treat fever and diarrhoea. A snuff made from dried flowers induces copious defluxion from the nose and relieves headache and pains. The leaves pounded with Nigella seeds are applied as a hot compress or burned and smoke inhaled to alleviate the discomfort of an ulceration nose. The leaves are burned and smoke inhaled to treat asthma, affection of the nose and mouth. The juice of the leaves is dropped into sore eyes to treat eyeache. A decoction of the bark with tamarind bark is used as a lotion to treat skin affections. An infusion of the bark is used as a nasal wash againts mucous discharge. The bark is used as a component in a poultice to treat leucorrhoea and pimples. A decoction of the bark is gargled as a dental strengthener to fix teeth loosened. It also to treat sore throat or relaxed uvula to strengthen the gums. A tincture of the bark is employed as an embrocation to treat rheumatism and distended abdomen. A decoction of the bark is used to treat blennorrhoea, sprue, gonorrhoea and itch. Pounded seeds are pasted with oil or ghee and used in suppositories in case of ohstinate constipation, especially for children (7, 12, 13, 14, 16, 17).

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

The sweet fruit of this species is eaten, and the tree furnishes an excellent timber. A pleasant perfume is obtained from the flowers, and the seeds yield a drying fixed oil. Bark and root are reported astringent and tonic and are employed as such in India. The juice of the unripe fruit and the bark are used to fix colours in silk dyeing.
