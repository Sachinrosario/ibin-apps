Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Woody under shrub, found nearly throughout  India mainly as an escape from cultivation. Found in west coasts and tropics in India.

Plant is also found in Srilanka, Malaysia & Africa.


Availability :  

The plants furnishing indigo are quite numerous; chief among them are Indigofera tinctoria, Linné, cultivated in India and the East India Islands; Indigofera Anil, Linné, growing in the West Indies, Central and South America, and Indigofera argentea, Linné, cultivated in Egypt and the French colonies in Africa. To these sources is to be added the commercial product called Woad, prepared from the leaves of Isatis tinctoria and I. lusitanica (Nat. Ord.—Cruciferae), plants which were formerly much cultivated in France and Germany, and supplied the demand for indigo before it was introduced into Europe from foreign countries.

Northern Bihar may be described as the head-quarters of the indigo indsutry, some of the districts of Lower Bengal, such as Jessore and Nadiya, still continue to produce the most highly prized qualities.

These plants are perennial shrubs with an erect stem reaching a height of 1 to 2 meters. The French and Guatemalan indigo differ in size and shape of the leaflets and pods (14.1-3). Prior to the development of synthetic aniline and indigo dyes, the indigo species were grown commercially in the East Indies, India, and parts of North, South, and Central America for export and domestic use. Popularity and economic value of the plant reached a peak during the Middle Ages, when indigo was the most important dye plant for blue color in the western portion of the world.

Status : Not threatened.

Common in waste lands and open forests.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

Woody under shrub up to 2 m tall, branchlets,  faintly angular when  young, oppressed, pubescent, stipules 2 mm, leaves up to 7 cm, long leaflets 7-11, ovate-oblong, 1-3 x 0.5-1.5 cm, opposite, apex apiculate to acute, margin entire, glabrescent above, appressedly puberulous below, petiolate  up to 1.5 mm. Bract setaceous, 1 mm; racemes axillary, spike like; flowers 5 mm.across, peduncle very slender, pedicel 2 mm; calyx hairy, lobes lanceolate; corolla pink,s tandard sub-arbicular, stamens diadelphous(9+1); ovary hairy, 3.5mm, style 0.5 mm; Pod straight or slightly curved, 2.8 x 0.2 cm, cylindric, slightly falcate towards tips, torulose, thick, seeds ca.10, globose.

Seed Biology :

Seedling Germination :

Mainly through seeds.  Direct seeding into the well prepared field.

Most seeds can be sown directly in the garden in the spring. Cover large seeds with 1/4 inch of soil or sifted compost. Press small seeds into the soil surface or barely cover with soil. Always tamp the soil after sowing the seeds and keep evenly moist until germination. Be patient. Medicinal herb seeds often take a long time to come up.


Cultivation:
The land should be thoroughly prepared, sowing commences about February.  For this purpose seed drill is used, specially constructed for indigo purpose.

As the land become exhausted by constant indigo cultivation, where renovation is not accomplished by annual inundations from the rivers, it becomes essentially necessary to manure.  The best material is the refused material after indigo extraction.  This is dried and carefully stacked, as it is not only of value as a manure for the higher lands but it is the fuel used in the succeeding season for the boilers.

By the time the plant is a few inches above ground, weeds will have grown to such extent as to necessitate weeding.  

In exceptionally dry seasons irrigation is necessary.

Indigo is ripe when the flowers begin to appear.  This generally takes place about June-earlier or later according to the nature of the season.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts used
Leaves for the dye

Uses
The leaves contain a colourless soluble glucoside known as Indican which oxideses to form the insoluble indigo.  This dye has a deep blue colour and a remarkable permanency and strength.  

  • It is used for dyeing a wide variety of fabrics.
  • Indigo finds use in dyeing and printing cotton and rayon and for dyeing wool.
  • It has been employed in the preparation of pigments for paints, lacquers rubber and printing inks.

Harvesting Practices


Produce and Products
Indigo

The species were being cultivated in India, China and other countries in the East as the source of indigo, but was replaced by other species (I. suffruticosa, I. arrecta)

Natural indigo contains in addition to indigotin, varying proportions of other dyes such as indirubin, indigo brown, indiglutin etc. The indigotin content of different grades of of natural indigo are: 70-90% best, 40-50% medium, ca. 20% low.  Indigo is used in dying and printing cotton and rayon and for dying wool. It has also been used in preparation of pigments for paints and printing inks.

The residual parts of the plants left after extraction of the dye are rich in nitrogen and are used as manure.

Indigo was one of the most important dye used since early times for dyeing purposes. It was exported from India until the end of the last century. Its important has declined since the advent of synthetic product.

Chemistry:

The Indigo of Commerce varies greatly in quality and purity and its value, therefore, depends upon the proportion of actual colouring matter contained.  Indigo contains 33 to 52 percent of colouring matter called Indigotin.  The commercial article contains, however, in addition the substances known as indigo-red, indigo-brown, indigo-gluten and a number of brown resinous products which have not as yet been fully determined.  It is insoluble in water, alcohol, ether and essential oils, or in dilute acids or alkalies hot or cold.  But it is soluble in sulphuric acid forming what is known as Extract of Indigo or Sulphate of Indigo.  Creosote, phenic acid, alcohol, fixed oils adn aniline, also dissolve small quantities at a boiling heat, but on cooling Indigotin is re-deposited; chlorine and hypchlorates destroy its blue colour.

The colouring matter of the Indigofera species, indigotin, is present in the form of glucoside, indican, particularly in the lamina and to a small extent in the midribs or rachis.  The dye is extracted from the leaves by fermentation when indican hydrolyzes into glucose and indoxyl, the latter on oxidation yields indigotin.

Natural indigo contains, in addition to indigotin, varying proportion of red dye, indirubin, a resinous impurity, indigo brown, indigluen, and other substances.  The indigotin content of different grades of natural indigo are as follows; best grades, 70-90, medium grades, 40-50, and low grades,c. 20 percent

Plant contains 10% saponins.

Processing Techniques


Indigo-blue does not pre-exist as such in these plants, It is developed by the decomposition of the bitter glucosid indican (C26H31NO17), a colorless chromogene existing therein. This substance also occurs sometimes in pathological urine. When acted upon by diluted acids or ferments, it is said to absorb water and to be hydrolyzed into indigo-blue (C16H10N2O2), and a saccharine principle indiglucin (C6H10O6), which undergoes further decomposition by fermentation. However, oxidation also plays an important part in the production of indigo-blue. The plants are collected during the flowering season, and are kept immersed in water in soaking vats or cisterns. At a temperature of about 30° C. (86° F.), fermentation soon sets in, lasting from 12 to 15 hours. When the liquid becomes of a yellowish-green color, it is drawn off and stirred briskly for about 3 hours. The liquid now turns deep blue, and the indigo, being insoluble in water, and of a greater specific gravity, soon falls to the bottom. The supernatant liquid is removed, the thick indigo sediment is heated to the boiling point of water to prevent further fermentation, then strained, pressed, and cut into cubical blocks and carefully dried. Three hundred kilograms of the indigo plant yield about 1 kilogram of indigo (S. P. Sadtler, Handbook of Industrial Organic Chemistry, 2d ed., 1895).

Commercial indigo occurs in hard, porous, brittle lumps or cubes of a dark-blue color and devoid of taste or odor. Indigo which is firm, dense, not easily broken, and which has a dull or greenish or grayish hue, is of inferior quality. Its specific gravity varies between 1.32 and 1.45. Commercial indigo contains from 20 to 80 per cent of indigo-blue, the average being about 45 per cent; the remainder consists of indigo-brown, indigo-red, indigo-gluten, water, and varying quantities of mineral matters. The ash should not amount to more than 8 per cent. When indigo is rubbed with a hard and smooth body, the surface of friction assumes a copper-like lustre. Heated to 287.7° C. (550° F.), indigo sublimes as a violet-colored vapor, although not without decomposition, and condenses on cool surfaces in the form of small, acicular crystals of a copper hue. Indigo-blue is not affected by the ordinary solvents, such as water, alcohol, ether, diluted acids and alkalies. It can be crystallized, however, from aniline and oil of turpentine, and is also soluble in chloroform, glacial acetic acid, paraffine, castor oil, nitrobenzene phenol, and similar solvents. Concentrated, especially fuming sulphuric acid, readily dissolves indigo, forming deep-blue solutions. According to the relative quantities of indigo and sulphuric acid, and the temperature maintained in the reaction, either indigo-mono-sulphonic (sulpho-purpuric) acid (C16H9[SO3H]N2O2), or indigo-disulphonic (sulphindigotic) acid (C16H8[SO3H]2N2O2), may be obtained. The monosulphonic acid is insoluble in water; its sodium salt is soluble, and is called indigo-purple or red-indigo carmine. The disulphonic acid is soluble in water, and is the substance commercially termed Saxony blue or Chemnitz blue. The sodium or potassium salt of this acid is the true indigo-carmine or soluble indigo of commerce, and occurs in the form of a pasty mass or as a powder; in the latter case it obtains the commercial name indigotine (S. P. Sadtler, Handbook, 1895, p. 447). The name indigotin is also often given in text-books to the pure indigo-blue (C16H10N2O2).

Indigo-blue is converted by oxidizers, e. g., nitric acid, into yellow-red, crystallizable isatin (C16H10N2O4), which, when distilled with caustic potash, yields aniline (C6H5NH2). When indigo is acted upon by reducing agents in alkaline solution, e. g., by dextrose, or sulphurous acid, or hydrogen sulphide, zinc, etc., a solution of a yellow color is yielded, containing alkali-soluble indigo-white (C16H12N2O2). This solution, exposed to the air, precipitates indigo-blue again. This reaction is made use of in dyeing; the cloth, being saturated with the alkaline solution containing indigo-white, is exposed to the air, whereby the indigo-blue formed is firmly fixed in the fiber

 

There are two methods of extraction:

1)Wet plant Process

Which involves the use of fresh or green leaves.  

2) Dry leaf/plant process

Indigo-green by adding potash in small portions to an alcoholic solution of an alkaline hyposulphindigotate.  The precipitate thereby thrown down after washing with alcohol, was dissolved with oxalic acid, the filtrate being next treated with carbonate of lime to remove the oxalic acid.  By filtration the lime is separated and by evaporating to dryness the purified green dye is obtained.  This substance is soluble in water and may be used direct as a dye.

Other Uses 
  1. The residual parts of plants left after the extraction of the dye are rich inavailable nitrogen and are used as manure for cereals, oil seeds, sugarcane and tobacco.
  2. As a medicinal plant, indigo has been used as an emetic.
