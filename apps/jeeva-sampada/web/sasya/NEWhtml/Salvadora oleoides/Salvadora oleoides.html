Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Pilu belongs to the family Salvadoraceae and genus Salvadora of which two species (Salvadora oleoides and S. persica) exist in India, they are natives of India, evergreen shrub or small tree. They are distributed in the semiarid, arid and desert regions in Egypt, India, Israel, Pakistan and Sudan. Its distribution outside India is in Arabia, Syria, eastern Africa and northern Sri Lanka. In India, it is distributed in arid and semiarid regions of Rajasthan, Gujarat, to some extent in Punjab, Haryana, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu. These trees grows luxuriously in the dry hot desert. In Rajasthan, the trees are found in the districts of Jodhpur, Barmer, Jalore and Pali. It grows well in the dry, sandy, and saline tracts of northern India. It thrives well in arid climate of tropics and subtropics. It is sensitive to frost and is well adapted to a wide range of edaphic and topographical conditions. Sandy to sandy loam light soil is suitable for growing pilu. It can tolerate salinity to a great extent. It grows well in inland non-saline soils to highly saline soils along the sea coast. In the coastal areas it grows both in inter-tidal and above tidal regions. The plant is capable of withstanding frequent inundations by seawater. The tree is slow growing and can tolerate temperature range of -3°C to 45°C and pH 6.5 to 8.5 (2, 3, 4, 5).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

S. oleoides is a small olive like tree (5 m height, 1 m diameter), much branched with short twisted hollow trunk and drooping branches. Sometimes trees are multistemmed in saline patches, thus give bushy appearance. Bark, grey to whitish grey. Leaves linear-lanceolate, coriaceous, entire and thick. . Wood is light red or yellow, moderately hard with a small irregular purple heart wood.

Salvadora persica : It is known as salt bush, mustard tree and tooth brush tree. It is a slow growing, small, evergreen tree or a large shrub (3-5 m) with drooping branches, short bole and a dense round crown. The bark is deeply cracked and dull grey to grey-white in colour. Leaves are somewhat fleshy and arc variable in shape, elliptic-ovate or ovate-lanceolate, 2-5 cm in length (1, 3, 4, 6).

Flowers sessile, greenish white, in errect compact terminal or axillary paniculate spikes, often clustered and shorter than leaves. Calyx cup shaped, divided half way or nearly half way into 4 rounded, obtuse lobes. Corolla as long as or little longer than calyx. Stamens 4, filaments free or connate. Ovary superior, 1-2 or imperfectly 4 celled, style short, stigma 2 fid or subentire, ovules 1-2 in each cell (1, 3, 4, 6).

Phenology :
The plant needs about 4 years to start bearing but it will be determined by the method of propagation and management practices. Flowering continues for a long period from January to June. On ripening fruits turn yellow when they are picked by hand (4).

Pollinators:

Seed Biology :
Fruits are aromatic drupe, globose, usually yellow when ripe. Seeds are 1-4 mm in diameter, sub-globose, smooth and brown or greenish yellow in colour.

Seedling Germination :
Seeds cannot be stored for long as they do not retain viability. No pre-treatment is needed for germination. The seeds germinate in 2-3 weeks. Fresh seeds have a germination percentage of 65- 70%. The species is known to be viviparous (4).

Cultivation:

Natural regeneration of S. persica and S. oieoides is by seeds, coppice, natural layering and mostly by root suckers. In nature the seeds are dispersed by birds. For raising seedlings, fruits are collected from the tree in the month of June, immediately depulped, washed and dried.

To sow in a nursery bed having 2 m2 surface area, around 2-3 kg of seeds are required. Salinity inhibits seed germination and the salinity of the water used for watering should not exceed 15.6 dSm-1. Fresh seeds germinate readily and are transplanted in polybags to raise the seedlings. The seedlings develop a long tap root system. The nursery is raised during winter and 6-month-old seedlings can be transplanted in the following rainy season. Saline water up to 60 dSm-1 can be used for irrigating the plantations. For raising plantations in difficult sites, 2 to 3 year-old nursery raised seedlings, with a deep taproot of nearly 50 cm are the best. It is suggested to raise the seedlings in large polybags (50 x 25 cm) of thick gauge. Fresh seeds are also sown in May-July at 3-5 mm depth. The seedlings require one full year to reach 20 cm height for planting out. The plants can also be raised by root suckers and air-layering.

One-year-old plants can be used for planting in the beginning of monsoon so that plants get sufficient time to establish. Once established they do not need any special care. Planting is done at 6 x 6 m for which pits of 60 x 60 x 60 cm are prepared in summer, refilled with a mixture of FYM and soil (1: 1). Since the plantation is natural no efforts have been made to evaluate the nutritional requirement of the plants. However, to obtain high quality produce these standards will have to be worked out. In plantations for seed production, regular irrigation is essential during the summer months. From the fourth year onwards the plants yield seeds. As the fruit matures the quantity of pulp increases and oil content of the seed decreases.

Micropropagation: Success in micropropagation of S. persica and S. oleoides by using explant tissues from young seedlings and selected mature trees has been reported. Several workers have developed a complete method for micropropagation through tissue culture technique. The plantlets produced through tissue culture are transferred into 1:1 sand: soil mixture in polybags, and kept in a greenhouse at a high humidity (80%). The humidity is gradually reduced which helps in the successful acclimatization of the plantlets, and in obtaining higher rates of survival. Acclimatized plants are transferred into larger polybags containing the same 1: 1 (sand: soil mixture) for further hardening. Hardened plants are field planted in the rainy season. In difficult sites the plants can be transplanted only after they have grown in the nursery for at least 2-3 years. A spacing of 5 x 5 m is maintained between the rows and between the plants (400 plants per hectare). Intially plants are watered regularly (3, 4, 5, 7).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

Larvae of several beetles defoliate trees. Thrips are also known to attack the species. A first report of Termite (Amitermes belli) as a pest of S. oleoides, in which it was found eating heartwood and sapwood at a height of 2-4 ft was recorded from Rajasthan (4, 12).


Diseases:

Both seedlings and mature plants are affected by witches' broom disease caused by phytoplasma, viral and crown gall diseases. Fungi such as Cercospora udaipurensis, Fames sp., Placosoma salvadorae and Septogloeum salvadorae also damage the leaves of this tree (4).

Economic Potential and Uses

The seeds contain 40 to 50% of greenish brown pungent oil. The oil is relatively rich in lauric and myristic acid and low in unsaturated fatty acids. The oil is similar to coconut oil and very valuable for soap making. It is also used for making candles. Medicinally, the fat is used in the treatment of rheumatic pains and it is employed in preperation of suppositories. It is also used as a base for ointments. The seed cake or meal left over after expressing fat is used as livestock feed and it is also valued as manure (3, 4, 8, 9).

Uses

Fruits are sweet and usually enjoyed by local people. The fruit pulp contains glucose, fructose and sucrose. The leaves make excellent feed for animals. Fed to cattle, they are said to increase the milk yield. Wood is used for building purposes, agricultural implements, perdian wheels and knees of boat. Wood is also mixed with deodar and pine scrapwood and is used for burning bricks. Egyptians use wood for making coffins. In eastern tropical Africa its leaves are eaten as vegetable and also used in the preparation of a sauce. Tender shoots and leaves are eaten as salad. Farmers also keep them on border of their fields as a windbreak and fence (3, 4, 7).

Harvesting Practices
The seeds should be harvested about three months after the flowering season. The seeds are collected by cutting the seed-bearing small twigs of the trees. The twigs are dried, and. beaten against a hard surface to separate the seeds. On an average a 10 year old tree is expected to give about 10 kg of seeds and a 20 year old tree about 35 kg of seeds. One hactare of S persica plantation will yield 4 tons of seeds in the 10th year and 14 tons of seeds in the 20th year (3, 4).

Produce and Products

Fruits are eaten. Oil is extracted from the seeds which is of industrial importance. Seed cake is used as manure and livestock feed. Leaves are used as fodder. Wood is employed in building construction.

Processing Techniques

Seeds contain 40-50 per cent of greenish yellow fat. Crude fat, obtained by pressing decorticated seeds in high pressure expellers with the accompaniment of heat or in ghanis, It could be used for soap making, but the undesirable colour and odour of the fat have prevented its direct use for this purpose. A process for the purification and preferential separation of non-glyceride constituents from the fat has been patented. The process consists of maceration and percolation of the coarsely powdered seed with dilute ethanol at ordinary temperature and subsequent extraction of the fat from the alcohol extracted seed powder. This is followed by the conventional alkali refining, deodorization and bleaching of the fat (3).

Other Uses

A decoction of the leaves is used in asthma and scurvy and a poultice is used on painful tumors and piles. Its shoots and leaves are used as antidotes for poisons. Leaves are also used to relieve cough and are given to horses as a purgative. Seeds are used as a purgative, diuretic and a tonic. In rheumatism, the seed oil is also used as an external application. Stem bark is used for gastric trouble and also as an ascarifuge. Root bark is used as a vesicant. Fruits are employed in the treatment of enlarged spleen, biliousness, rheumatism and low fever (3, 4, 7).

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

Salvadora (pilu) is a very common tree found growing luxuriously in Indian deserts, especially in Rajasthan and Gujarat. Fruits are eaten by local inhabitants and seeds are rich in oil (40 to 50 per cent), which is non-edible but has lot of industrial value. It is generally found growing in highly saline regions of desert and makes an excellent food for camels. It is an effective plant for shelter belts and windbreaks in deserts as it makes an almost impenetrable growth through root suckers around the stem and seedlings, which spring up under the shade of canopy (4).

Miscellaneous Information

Cultivars: There are no specific cultivars for cultivation. This tree is not exploited commercially to its full potential. This plant is gaining more significance recently for its seeds rather than fruit pulp which is rich in glucose, fructose and sucrose. However, natural variants are available which should be surveyed and exploited for commercial purpose. The trees bearing sweet fruits with higher oil content and lower levels of compounds like dibenzyl thiourea, dibenzyl urea and thiouglucoside glucotropaloin, which make its oil non-edible should be selected. S. persica is more salt tolerant than S. oleoides. Both the species of Salvadora are preferential halophytes, which store the excess salt their leaves. The excess sodium and chlorine are eliminated through accumulation in senecent leaves. The species are known to drastically restrict their water loss, which considerably raises the temperature on the leaf surface (4).
