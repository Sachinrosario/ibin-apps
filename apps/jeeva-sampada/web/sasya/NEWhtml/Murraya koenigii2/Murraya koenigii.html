DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:
Large shrubs or small trees, up to 6 m high; branchlets rather cylindric; bark green or greyish. Leaves up to 50 cm long; leaflets 15-27, ovate to lanceolate, 2-8 x 1-3 cm, acuminate at apex, margin glandular-crenulate, acute and oblique at base, glabrous except the puberulent midrib, thin membranaceous, foetid-scented; petioles and rachis densely white pubescent.
M. koenigii is frequently cultivated in homestead gardens of Southern India for its aromatic leaves. It has been observed that the plants in cultivation are different from those found in the wild in having a tree habit; smaller leaves with very small, membranous and highly odoriferous leaflets and petiole which often has a reddish tinge almost up to the point from which the first (basal) leaflets arise. In the wild forms the leaves are larger, sometimes reaching up to 50 cm long and have more number of leaflets which are coriaceous and less aromatic. The cultivated plants widespread in home gardens in Southern India could probably be a traditional ‘land race’ of this species. (Nair & Nayer, 1997).
REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:   

Floral and seed biology:
 Flower many, in dense cymes, scented; pedicels puberulent. Calyx saucer shaped; sepals 5, fused at base. Petals 5, linear, 6-8 x 1-1.5 mm, obtuse at base, greenish-white, glabrous. Berries subglobose, ca 9 x 10 mm, purplish to black when ripe, 2-locular; pulp whitish. Seeds 1 or 2, ovoid-oblong, green. It is fruits and flowers between March and July.

Economic Potential and Uses
UTILIZATION:
 Fresh leaves of this plants are used as an ingredient in curries and chutneys. They also yield a highly odoriferous essential oil which is used as a fixative for soap perfume. Leaves, stem, bark and roots are tonic, stomachic and carminative. Infusion of leaves is applied against vomiting, diarrhoea and dysentery. Berries are edible. Wood is used for making agricultural implements.
NBPGR:
Plants are used in the preparation of a tonic.  Bark and roots are used as stimulants and applied externally to cure eruptions and bites of poisonous animals.  Green leaves are eaten raw as a cure for dysentery.  As decoction with bitters, it acts as a febrifuge and is also used in snakebite.  Leaves are frequently used in curries.  Fruit are edible.


Chemistry:
NBPGR:    All parts of the plants, especially the leaves are rich in carbazole alkaloids.  Those include members with (i) C13-skeleton-murrayanine, mukoeic acid, mukonine and mukonidine (ii) C18-skeleton including girinimbine, koenigicine (Koenidine) and (iii) C23 – skeleton containing mahanimbine, mahanimbinine, murrayazoline, murrayazolinine, murrayazolidine, cyclomahanimbine, bicyclomahanimbicine, murrayacabazole, bases isolated recently include mukoline, mukolidine (C13 group, from roots), mukonicine (C18 leaves) the biogenetically significant mukonal (C13 group from stem bark), mahanimboline (C23 root bark), iso-murrayazoline (C23 stem-bark). The leaves gave a coumarin glucoside, scopolin also, essential oil from leaves contained β-caryophyllene, β-gurjunene, β-elemene, β-phellandrene, β-thujene as major constituents.

Murraya koenigii (Linn.) Spreng. leaves on hydrodistillation gave 0.5% essential oil. On examination by GC-MS the oil was found to contain 28 constituents. The major constituents of the oil are cis caryophyllene (11.74%), dipentene (11.3%), a-eudesmol (9.61%), isocaryophyllen (8.41%), ss-elemene (7.09%). (Chowdhury, 2000) CA. 2001,35:141945.
The essential oils of the leaves and fruits of Murraya koenigii Spreng, were analyzed by GC and GC/MS. Forty-eight constitutents of the leaf oil representing 95% of the oil and 42 constituents of the fruit oil accounting for 98.5% of the oil have been identified. The major constitutents of the leaf oil were a-pinene (9.0%), a-phellandrene (6.1%), b-phellandrene (50.1%), (E)-b-ocimene (7.1%) and b-caryophyllene (4.9%). The main constituents of the fruit oil were a-pinene (48.1%), b-pinene (7.1%), myrcene (3.1%), b-phellandrene (26.0%), g-terpinene (3.0%) and b-caryophyllene (3.0%). (Mallavarapu et al., 2000) CA. 2001,135:65986.
Five carbazole alkaloids were isolated from the CH2Cl2 ext. and their structures were identified to be euchrestine B (1), bismurrayafoline E (2), mahanine (3),mahanimbicine (4), and mahanimbine (5) based on 1H and 13C NMR and mass (MS) spectral data. (Yukari et al., 2001).
Two compounds were isolated from stem bark and identified as murrayazoline and murrayacine. (Aspollah et al., 2001) CA. 2002,136:2961.
Two new alkaloids, 1-formyl-3-methoxy-6-methylcarbazole and 6,7-dimethoxy-1-hydroxy-3-methylcarbazole have been isolated from the leaves of Murraya koenigii. 6,7-dimethoxy-1-hydroxy-3-methylcarbazole was found to be active against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria and fungi. (Chowdhury et al., 2001) CA. 2001, 135:192863.
Five antioxidative carbazole alkaloids were isolated from the CH2Cl2 extract and their structures were identified to be euchrestine B (1), bismurrayafoline E (2), mahanine (3), mahanimbicine (4), and mahanimbine (5) based on (1)H and (13)C NMR and mass (MS) spectral data. (Tachibana et al., 2001)
Two new carbazole alkaloids named murrayanine and 8,8' '-biskoenigine were isolated from Murraya koenigii. Compound 1 was a novel carbazole alkaloid with a rare phenylpropanyl substitution. Compound 2 was a symmetrical dimer of the carbazole alkaloid koenigine and showed antiosteoporotic activity in the CAT-B model with IC(50) 1.3 microg/mL. (Wang et al., 2003)

Pharmacology:  

Root extract was devoid of anti-inflammatory activity in rats even at sublethal dose of 2.3 g equiv./ml; girinimbine found to be present. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989)f.
Koenoline exhibited cytotoxic activity against KB cell culture. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989)g.
Stem bark extract (1.0 g/kg) reduced carragenin-induced inflammation. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994)l.
Marmesin-1''-O-b-D-galactoside showed moderate to appreciable activity against Vibrio cholera, Salmonella typhimurium, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Candida albicans, Aspergillus fumigatus and Trichophyton mentagrophytes. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994)m.
Yuechukene exhibited anti-estrogenic effects in rat uterotrophic and mice vaginal assays and was found to bind to rat and mice estrogen receptors. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994)n.
Feeding the leaves of the title plants to rats produced hypoglycemia. There was an increase in hepatic glycogen concentration and in glycogenesis, as evident from the increased activity of glycogen synthetase, and a decrease in glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis, as evident from the decreased activity of glycogen phosphorylase and gluconeogenic enzymes. (Khan et al., 1995).
The status of lipid peroxidation was investigated in rats fed M. Koenigii (curry leaf). Concentration of malondialdehyde showed a significant decrease, while hydroperoxides and conjugated dienes were significantly increased in liver and heart of both the experimental groups. SOD and catalase activity was found to be increased in liver and heart of both the spices administered groups. Glutathione levels in liver, heart and kidney were lowered in rats administered these spices. Glutathione reductase, glutathione peroxidase and glutathione S-transferase activity showed a sharp increase in the experimental groups compared to the controls. (Khan et al., 1996)a.
Reduction in total serum cholesterol and LDL + VLDL, an increase in the HDL, lower release of lipoproteins into the circulation and an increase in the LCAT activity. (Khan et al., 1996)b.
9-formyl-3-methylcarbazole from roots displayed weak cytotoxicity against both mouse melanoma B16 and adriamycin-resistant P388 mouse leukemia cell lines. (Chakrabarty et al., 1997) CA.1999, 128:32368.
Status of lipid peroxidation was studied in rats induced high fat diet and Murraya koenigii. The study revealed that these species alter the peroxidation (thiobarbituric acid reactive substances) level to a beneficial extent. Histological studies also focus on modulation of hepatic functions to near normal level. (Khan et al., 1997).
Ethanol extracts of leaves and the chloroform sokluble fraction showed promising antibacterial activity. (Nutan et al., 1998) MAPA. 1998, 8802: 1001.
Murrayanol showed an IC50 of 109 mg/mL against hPGHS-1 and an IC50 of 218 mg/mL against hPGHS-2 in antiinflammatory assays, while mahanimbine displayed antioxidant activity at 33.1 mg/mL. (Ramsewak et al., 1999) CA.1999.130: 231916.
Xanthotoxin, isobyakangelicol, phellopterin, gosferol, neobyakangelicol, byakangelicol, byakangelicin and isogosferol are reported as minor furocoumarins of Murraya koenigii seeds. (Adebajo et al., 2000)
The antioxidative properties of the leaves exts. of M. koenigii using different solvents were evaluated based on the oil stability index (OSI) together with their radical scavenging ability against 1-1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) and five active compounda were identified as antioxidant. (Yukari et al., 2001).
The essential oil from the leaves of Murraya koenigii Spreng. was studied for its antifungal activity which showed very good activity against Rhizoctonia bataticola and Helminthosporium oryzae with ED50 0.1112% and 0.1214% respectively. The chief chemical constituents of essential oil are b-caryophyllene (29.0%) and b-gurjunene (21.0%), which are mainly responsible for the activity. (Srivastava and Singh.,2001) (CA.2002, 136:196887).
Feeding of diet containing various doses of curry leaves (5, 10 and 15%) to normal rats for 7 days as well as mild diabetic (blood glucose levels >175 mg/dl induced by alloxan 35 mg/kg IP) and moderate diabetic rats (blood glucose levels >250 mg/dl induced by STZ 60 mg/kg IP) for 5 weeks showed varying hypoglycemic and anti-hyperglycemic effect. In normal rats, reduction in blood glucose was almost negligible (approximately 4% with 10 and 15% diet). (Yadav et al., 2002).
