Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

An annual 30-90/1.2 m cm. high with pendulous spikelets. They thrive on a wide range of soils of ample, but not excessive, fertility Loam soils are best, especially silt and clay loams. . Well-drained neutral soils in regions where annual rainfall is 7.7 dm or more are best, and temperatures of 5 to 26°C.

Distribution : This species is not a homogenous one and is considered to be derived mostly from A. fatua, and a few of its races from A. sterilis Linn. and A. barbata. Cultivated in N. India, from Bengal to the Indus in the Himalaya up to 12000 ft. Its cultivation is largely confined to Uttar Pradesh, Punjab and Haryana. To a smaller extent, it is also grown in some parts of Maharashtra, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Bihar, Orissa and West Bengal.

VARIETIES.

Kent- an introduction from Australia is the outstanding dual-purpose variety for plains and hills of northern India. It is a stiff-strawed, bold-seeded variety, flowering in about 112 days and is capable of giving a grain yield of 35 q per ha, and a yield of green fodder to the extent of 500 quintals per ha. the grains are highly suitable for milling by the breakfast-food industry.

NP101- which is early-maturing and dwarf-growing, is recommended for grain production only.

Haryana Javi 114 (HFO 114)- is recommended for cultivation in Haryana. This is a quick-growing variety suitable for the multicut programme. It yields about 600 quintals of green fodder and about 130 quintals of dry matter per hectare. It is a bold-seeded variety and produces about 25 quintals of grain per hectare. Two good cuttings can be taken with a minimum of three irrigations after sowing. The variety is capable of giving 25 quintals of grain per hectare after taking one cutting for fodder after about 65-70 days, provided it is sown in October.

For fodder- varieties, such as 'N.P> Hyb. 3', 'Kent', 'Weston 11', 'Bunker 10', Algerian', 'FOS1/29'

Phenology :

Erect tufted annual grass, to 1.2 m tall. Leaves are abundant, and bluish green in colour. Culms smooth or scabrous beneath the panicle. Grains are hairy, grooved lengthwise on the face.

Cultivation:

Land preparation:

The land should be ploughed well or worked with a blade-harrow three or four times. There should be a gap of about a month between the harvesting of the previous crop and the sowing of oats to allow the stubble to decay well. However, if the oat crop is to be grown immediately after harvesting the previous crop, it would be desirable to remove the stubble to ensure a good germination of the seed.

Sowing:

The sowing season extends from October to December. For multicuts, the crop should be grown in October. The seed is sown broadcast in the case of the fodder crop, and in rows 23-30 cm apart, if the crop is meant for grain. The seed-rate varies from 65-85 kg per ha, a larger quantity being used for sowings on poor soils. For fodder, a higher seed-rate is needed to ensure a dense stand of tall, thin-stalked plants.

Irrigation:

The oat crop requires three to four irrigations. For maximum yields, it should be irrigated at 60 mm cumulative evaporation, as measured with the open-pan evaporimeter. The tillering stage is critical under moisture stress. Usually, no interculture is done.

Nutrition:

When grown as a grain crop, the manure is generally applied to the previous crop in the rotation to prevent lodging. For the fodder crop, a fairly heavy basal application of organic manure supplemented by top-dressing with a nitrogenous fertilizer is practiced to encourage the growth of the plant early. The oat crop may advantageously be manured directly with a combination of nitrogenous and phosphatic fertilizers. The application of 40-100 kg of N per ha and 20-40 kg of P2O5/ha has been found to be very beneficial, depending on the crop in the preceding season, the length of the growing season, manuring, irrigation and the number of cuttings to be taken. The nitrogenous requirement of oats is more, following crops, such as maize, pearl-millet, sorghum, than after a fallow or legumes. Phosphatic fertilizers should be applied as a basal dose at the sowing time. Nitrogen may be applied in two equal doses at the sowing time and with the first irrigation or after the first cutting for fodder.

Crop rotation:

It is grown in rotation with leguminous or non-leguminous fodder and grain crops. The crops in the proceeding kharif are maize, pearl-millet, sorghum, cowpea, cluster-bean, etc.

Inter cropping:

The oat crop is usually grown pure but in northern Gujarat, rape is sown to form a subordinate mixture. The legumes, such as common vetch, senji, lucerne and berseem, can be associated with oats with advantage.

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used and Uses

In some areas it is grown for grain. It is also grown in pastures, cut for hay or green fodder and is also used for making silage. Its fodder and grain are highly nutritious and are especially preferred for milch cattle and draft animals, including horses. A very small portion of the oat grain is processed into food in the form of 'rolled oats' and 'oatmeal' for human consumption.

Oat flour contains an antioxidant which is used to preserve quality by delaying rancidity. Oat flour may also be mixed with wheat flour for multigrain baked products.


Less importantly, oats hulls are also used in the manufacture of construction boards, cellulose pulp, broiler litter, and as a filter in breweries.

 

Oats have several other potential applications: protein concentrate foods, texturized vegetable proteins, vegetable gums, infant and geriatric foods, sweeteners, Oat oil, cosmetic preparations, cariostats, pharmaceuticals, adhesives, and abrasives.

Harvesting Practices

The crop matures in about four-and-a-half to five months. For fodder, two to three cuttings are taken from January to March and the crop is allowed in some cases to set seed. For one cutting for fodder, the plants are harvested at 50 per cent flowering. For two cuttings, the first is taken at 65-70 days after sowing, and the second at flowering. After the first cutting the plants may be allowed to produce grains.

However, this practice result in lower yields of grain, straw and dry matter than in those obtained from the first cut. Harvest the plants when the grains are mature, but the straw is still somewhat green; otherwise there is a loss of grain due to shedding. Threshing is done in the same manner as in the case of wheat and barley.


Produce and Products

Currently, the major end products of oats are:

  1. rolled or flaked oats,

  2. furfural, and furans a chemical intermediate in the production of many industrial products (e.g. nylon, lubricating oils, butadiene, phenolic resin glues, and rubber tread compositions). and

  3. antioxidants.

Additional products are oat flour, feeding oatmeal, hulls and groats.

Processing Techniques

The cereal starches, such as maize, wheat, rice and sorghum, are recovered by several processes, of which the wet-milling is by far the most important. Other processes commercially employed in the manufacture of unmodified starches are the alka1i.

In all the above processes, starch is recovered in five stages: (i) softening or steeping of the grains, after thorough washing in water, (ii) glinding and sieving of the steeped grains, (iii) tabling or centrifugation of the ground mass, (iv) dewatering and drying of the final products, and (v) the recovery of important byproducts.

 

Wet-milling process-This process is universally adopted for the manufacture of all types of cereal starches. It makes possible a complete separation of the major components of the cereal kernels.

 

The raw material comes to the wet-milling plants in the form of shelled grains. The grains are screened to remove all large and small pieces of grains, chaff, sand and other foreign matter.


The screened grains are steeped in steeping conical staves in which water and sulphur dioxide are sent in. Sulphur dioxide helps in the prevention of the growth of putrefactive organisms, dispersion of proteins and in maximizing the recovery of starch. During steeping, washwater from a previous operation, concentrated to attain a soluble level of 0.10-0.20 per cent, is sent counter-currently through the steeping staves. The steep water coming out of the staves contains 40-80 g/l of total solubles and is sent to concentration plants for preparing animal feeds, mixed feeds, or fermentation nutrients.


After steeping, the grains are crushed in attrition mills and the pulverized grains are sent through a set of hydroclones under pressure. The heavier endosperm and fiber particles pass out of the bottom of the hydroclones, while the lighter germ is drawn off the top of the vortex.


The underflow from the hydroclones is sized in screens and ground in Buhrstone or Entroleter impact mills. The pulverized material is passed through screen pumps where fibers are removed as continuous mats which are washed with water, slurried, squeezed and dried. The de-fibered pulp, known as mill starch, is concentrated in centrifuges where protein is removed as a overflow and starch is obtained from the underflow. The starch stream is passed through a series of hydroclones to reduce the protein content to the minimum level of 0.3 per cent. In the last hydroclone, the starch is washed with softened fresh water. The starch milk from the cyclones is dewatered on moving belt filters or in basket centrifuges, and dried in flash driers.


Alkali process- In this process, the proteins in the cereal grains are dispersed by an aqueous solution of caustic soda or ammonium hydroxide. All cereal starches can be manufactured by this process. The cereal grains are crushed and sieved, the flour is slurred with an aqueous solution of ammonium hydroxide, or the grains themselves are directly soaked in a dilute aqueous solution of caustic soda in large vats. The alkaline slurry is screened in vibrating screens, the underflow is fed into a series of centrifuges where the separation of starch from the proteins occurs. The starch milk from the centrifuges is washed with water, filtered in vacuum filters and tunnel-dried. The protein solution from the centrifuges is dried in spray driers.


MODIFIED STARCHES

Natural starches are the basic source material for the manufacture of all types of modified starches. The modified starches may be classified into innumerable groups, such as thick boiling starches and thin boiling starches; or acetylated, esterified, etherified and hydrolyzed starches; or textile, laundry and paper starches and so on. The modified starches are categorized here into (i) dextrins, (ii) acid-modified starches, (iii) oxidized starches, (iv) pregelatinized starches, and (v) starch derivatives.


Dextrins

Known popularly as starch gum, white dextrins, canary dextrins, or the British gum, the dextrin is an intermediate colloidal substance formed during the hydrolysis of starch. It is marketed as a yellow or a ; white powder, or as granulated particles, or as thick brown coloured liquids, or as a white, viscous paste. Dextrins are prepared from unmodified starches and are named after the starch from which they are derived, e.g. Barley dextrin.

The dextrins are commercially prepared by two methods, viz. dry process and wet process. In the dry process, raw starch is directly heated to 200-250°C In steam, oil, or fuel heated roasters. During dextrinization, the colour of the original starch changes to yellow and brown and finally the entire mass is charred. At 150-80°C, a series of low viscosity canary dextrins are obtained, and at above 195°C a series of British gums are produced. In some manufacturing plants, small quantities of hydrochloric acid are added to the raw starch for bringing down the temperatures of dextrinization. In the wet process, dextrins are produced by acid hydrolysis, enzymic action, or by the action of Bacillus macerans on the raw starch. In actual operation, an aqueous suspension or a paste of starch is heated with a trace of acid or an enzyme and after sufficient degree of conversion is accomplished the excess acid is neutralized, the mixture filtered and the product dried. Only hydrochloric acid, nitric acid and formic acid are commercially used in this process. The roasted or the dried product is milled, sieved and packed.


Acid-modified starches

These starches are prepared by treating an aqueous suspension of starch with a dilute solution of an acid at subgelatinizing temperatures (50-55°C). The acids used for the hydrolysis of starch are hydrochloric, nitric, sulphuric, formic, acetic, propionic, and phosphoric acids. In modern industrial practices, it is usual to employ a mixture of hydrochloric and sulphuric acids. The raw Starch is treated with the acid until the desired viscosity change is obtained. The resulting slurry is then neutralized with sodium carbonate, and the acid modified starch is recovered by filtration, washing and drying.


Oxidized starches

These starches are produced by treating raw starch with oxidizing agents such as sodium hypochlorite, periodate, nitrogen dioxide, chlorine, potassium permanganate, potassium dichromate, and ozone. Commercial oxidized starches have been produced mostly from sodium hypochlorite-starch reaction, or to some extent by periodate or nitrogen dioxide-starch reaction. The effect of the other oxidizing agents on starch has been studied only at the laboratory levels.


Hypochlorite oxidized starches

A slurry of refined starch at a concentration of 20-24° Be is introduced into a reaction tank equipped with impellers for mixing the starch with chemicals. A dilute solution of sodium hypochlorite is let into the tank until the reacting solution has a pH of 8-10. As the reaction proceeds, the value of pH goes down and more quantities of the hypochlorite solution are added to maintain the original level of pH. The temperature of reaction is maintained in the range of 21-38°C. The reaction tanks are provided with a mechanical cooling device since the reaction is exothermic. When the desired amount of oxidation has been achieved, the oxidized starch is neutralized or made slightly acidic (pH 6-6.5). A solution of sodium bi-sulphite or sulphur dioxide gas is added to the treated starch to remove free chlorine present in the reaction system. The oxidized starch is finally washed in continuous vacuum filters and dried in hot air driers.


Periodate oxidized starches

These starches, known commercially as dialdehyde starches, are produced by the oxidation of the reducing and non-reducing endgroups of the starch by periodic acid. Periodic acid is manufactured by electrolysing an aqueous solution of iodine in caustic soda in electrolytic cells containing iron cathodes and lead dioxide coated lead anodes. The periodic acid is reacted with starch in polyethylene, or PVC, or glass-lined reactors. After the desired level of oxidation is achieved, the oxidized starch is washed with water, centrifuged and dried. The spent oxidants from the centrifuges are again recycled into the electrolytic cells, generating the periodic acid.


Nitrogen dioxide oxidized starches

These are prepared by reacting raw starches with liquid nitrogen dioxide in carbon tetrachloride at room temperature, or with the vapours of nitrogen dioxide, or with a mixture of sodium nitrite and nitric acid. Low temperature oxidation with a high concentration of nitric acid promotes carbonyl group formation, while the exactly opposite conditions give rise to non-uronyl carboxyl group formation.


Pregelatinized starches

This group of modified starches includes a variety of starches obtained by precooking natural, modified or derivated starches on hot rolls with or without the addition of chemical reagents. The starches have the common property of marked swelling on contact with cold water, and form colloidal dispersions reflecting the characteristics of the raw materials from which they are

derived.


Starch derivatives .

The term starch derivatives is applied to compounds evolved by the reaction of starch with other chemicals. The derivatives of starch outlined in this section include acetates, phosphates, carbamates and sulphonates of starch, hydroxyethyl starch, other types of esters and ethers, cationic starches, and cross-linked starches.

Other Uses

The seeds are a nerve tonic, stimulant, and antispasmodic. Avena forms an important restorative in nervous prostration and exhaustion after all febrile diseases, and as a tonic in spermatorrhoea and insomnia. It seems to exert a very beneficial action upon the heart muscles and on the urinary organs, speedily relieving spasmodic conditions of bladder and ureter.

The seeds are folk remedies for tumors. The seed contain the antitumor compound b-sitosterol. Tincture of the plant has been used as a nerve stimulant and to treat opium addiction. Various folk cultures consider the oat as antispasmodic, antitumor, cyanogenetic, demulcent, diuretic, nervine, neurotonic, stimulant, tonic and vulnerary. Oats have been suggested in folk remedies for cancer, dyspepsia, egilops, gastroenteritis, gout, hepatosis, insomnia, neurosis, paralysis, parotitis, parturition, psoriasis, rheumatism, spasms, tumors, but other oat species may be involved. In an article riddled with errors, the Globe (February 28, 1984) reports that oat straw, usually taken as a tea, is a sexual nerve tonic.
