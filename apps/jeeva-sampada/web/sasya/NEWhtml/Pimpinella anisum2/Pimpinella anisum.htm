Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT                                                                                                                                    

An annual aromatic herb. Aniseed, often referred to as anise, is the dried ripe fruit of an annual herb of the parsley family (1). The early Arabic name was "Anysum" from which was derived the Greek "Anison" or "Anneson" and the Latin "anisum." The Medieval name "Pimpinella," derived from the Latin name "dipinella," meaning twice-pinnate or bi-pinnate in allusion to the form of the leaves (5).

Climate and soil
P. anisum requires a warm and long frost-free growing season of 120 days. In temperate areas it is grown as summer crop and in subtropical areas grown as winter crop in North Indian plains. A warm weather during sowing with temperature of 20-30oC is desirable. Low temperature and high atmospheric humidity are conducive for vegetative growth while the crop requires warm summery weather during seed formation and development stage. Frost in early stages is deterious for growing seedlings. Crop is grown in wide range of soils ranging from sandy loam to clay loam having pH of 6.0 to 8.5. However moderate to heavy loam soils, which are well drained and moderately fertile are ideal. However higher seed yields have been reported over heavier, moderately rich black cotton soils. Poor drainage of the land as well as water stagnation is detrimental for the crop stand and reduction in yield.

Availability :

Native of East Mediterranean region. Indigenous to Greece, Egypt and Asia Minor. Now grown in Europe, Mexico, Russia, Turkey and India. It is known to have been used by the ancient Egyptians about in 1500 BC; the Greeks and Romans also used it. Latter on it was brought to India via Persia. Anise is cultivated nowadays in a wide range of countries, especially in southern Europe (in particularly in Spain and Italy), the former Soviet Union, Bulgaria, Romania, Turkey, Iran, northern Africa, India, China, Japan, Chili, Argentina and Mexico, and is often naturalized. Anise fruits are used in South-East Asia, but the crop does not grow satisfactorily in topical lowlands. Only very occasionally is anise growing in South-East Asia (2).

Conservation/ Threat Status : Cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES: Aromatic, erect, annual herb 15-50 cm tall, with stem and finely patently pubescent. Leaves alternate, entire to pinnately compound; petiole 4-10 cm long in lower leaves, gradually shorter to absent in the upper most leaves orbicular-reniform, dantetely lobed; blade of middle leaves pinnate or 3-foliolate with incised leaflets; blade of uppermost leaves 3-partite, subsessile. Inflorescence a terminal and leaf opposed, compound umbel; penduncle 2.5-7 cm long; involucre of bracts absent or short, 1-2-foliolate; primary rays 4-15, 0.5-2.5 cm long, hairy; secondary rays (pedicels) 7-13, 1-5 mm long (3). Root is thin and spindle-shaped.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Flowers bisexual; calyx indistinct; corolla with 5 obovate-cordate petals, inflexed; stamens 5, filaments longer than petals, inflexed at apex; pistil with inferior, bilocular, 2-carpelled ovary, styles 2, each with swollen stylopodium at base and globular stigma at the top. In anise the time from sowing to seed ripening is 4-5 months (3).

Fruit
The fruit is an ovoid pear-shaped schizocarp, somewhat compressed at the side,3-5 mm long, mericarp 5-ribbed, with numerous oil ducts, containing a single seed; fruit wall connate with seed testa (3). The two-part fruits separate heavily. The carpophore is almost two piece up to the base. Commercially available aniseed usually contains the whole fruits and occasionally parts of the fruit stalk. The fruits with the style-foot are 3-5 mm long, 1.5-2.5 mm wide and 2-4 mm thick. Vittae (oil ducts) are almost always present embedded in the fruit wall on the dorsal surface, sometimes in or directly beneath the ridges. The fruit are downy. Their colour is grayish-green to grayish-brown (4).


Breeding system and varietal improvement: Anise is a cross-pollinating species and is genetically heterozygous. 

Phenology:
Flowering normally starts 65-75 days after germination, whereas the duration of flowering and seed development is 20-25 days (3).

Pollinators: Flowers pollinated by little bees (Aphid florae).

Floral and seed biology: Seed in ovoid in shape about 5 mm long with short stalk laterally compressed and 5 longitude ridges on the pericarp. Seed maturity occurs in one month after pollination, when the oil content in the dried fruits is about 2.5%.
CULTIVATION ASPECT:
Seed germination:
Fruits should have a minimum purity of 90% and a minimum germination of 70%. Ripe-fruit’s seeds germinate quickly. Germination time is 14 days. Optimum soil temperature for germination is 18-21˚C. Only seeds from the previous year’s harvest germinate well. Long storage of seeds will reduce the seed vigour. Seeds stored for 5 years or more will no longer germinate (4).

Seedling development;
Initial growth is slow: 35-40 days from germination to stem emergence. The ripening of seed is uneven.

Local cultivars :
The genus Pimpinella L. comprises about 150 herbaceous species, most of them native in Eurasia and northern Africa.
Chromosome number
2n = 18,20

Selection of planting material:
Seeds are used for propagation. With respect to in vitro propagation, multiple shoot formation has been achieved from callus cultures derived from shoot apices, root and stem explants, and seeds. Somatic embryogenesis has been observed in callus cultures derived from shoot apices, root explants and seeds. Plantlets have also been directly regenerated from shoot apices.

Planting:
Anise is propagated by seed, which is drilled in rows or broadcast. Direct sowing is recommended, because seedlings are harmed by transplanting. Germination is facilitated by soaking the seed overnight in water before sowing. Seeds remain viable for 1-3 years. Prior to sowing, the soil is often ploughed and harrowed to obtain a fine seedbed. When anise is growing in rows, seeds are sown 1-3 cm deep, 15-90 cm between rows and 2,5-15 cm apart in the row. In experiments in Pakistan, rows 40 cm apart gave higher seed yields per ha then rows 20 or 30 cm apart or than broadcasting. The higher yield was attributed to more vigorous vegetables growth and umbels. At 30 days from sowing or when seedlings are 5-8 cm tall, they are thinned to 10-30 cm between plants. Seed rates ranges from 6-15 kg/ha. The crop is sown in October-November in northern India, and in March-April in Europe.

Seed are small and needs through land preparation for realizing good germination and high seedling starch. So field should be ploughed and borrowed 2-3 times to bring to fine tilth. It is immediately leveled to conserve soil moisture. Seeds are sown during October to early November in North Indian Plains and March- April in temperate areas in hills. Line spray 45-x 20 cm can also be drilled directly in rows at a distance of 45 cm at one cm depth. Seed rate: 8-10 kg seeds/ha depends up on the soil moisture and quality of the seed. Soak the seeds for 8-10 ha in water before sowing followed by irrigation to improve seed germination. 

Management after planting:
One light irrigation is given after sowing and optimum moisture is maintained till germination. On an average, 4-6 irrigations are required up to March depending upon the soil condition and climate. Irrigation is given at flowering and grain-filling stage that needs copious application of water.
Manuring: 15-20 t of well rotten FYM is applied before sowing at the time of land preparation. A total of 80 kg N 60 kg Phosphorus, 20 kg of Potash applied as basal. The balance nitrogen of 40 kg given in two splits at an interval of 25-30 days sowing at vegetative stage. Micronutrients, magnesium, iron, zinc are found useful in increasing the yield and quality. 
Weeding: Field should be kept weed free during the initial period of growth. 2-3 weedings are done at 20-30 days interval after sowing.
Crop rotation: With sacred basil and senna has been realized better in Karnataka.

MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
Aphids: Aphis craccivora and Myzus persicae attack to crop at the time of flowering.
Symptoms: The nymphs suck the sap from the plant causing shrivelling and drying of seeds.
Control: Spray 0.30% Dimethoate (30 EC) or Phosphomidon (85 WSC) or 0.1% Malathion (50 EC)

Semilooper: Thyranopluria orichalcia is a defoliating insect causing severe damage to the crop and feed on leaves.
Control: Spray 0.2% Monocrotophos or Endosulphur at 10-15 days interval.

Diseases:
Alternaria leaf blight: Caused by fungus.
Symptoms: Blight symptoms in leaves.
Control: Spray 0.25% of Mancozeb twice at 10 days interval.

Powdery mildew: Caused by Eryziphe polygonii
Symptoms: Appear at flowering stage in cloudy weather during February-March.
Control: Dusting sulphur 20-25 kg/ha or spraying of wettable sulphur 0.2% or Karathane, 0.1% or Calaxin 0.05% at an interval of 10-15 days.
Anise is sensitive to bacterial attack. In India, fruits-bearing anise may be severely infected with Trichothecium roseum, a fungus producing toxins, which if ingested over a prolonged period, may cause neurological disorders in humans (3).

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Aniseed is flavourant, culinary, household, cosmetic and medicinal. While the entire plant is fragrant, it the fruit of anise, commercially called aniseed. It one of the oldest spices used widely for flavouring curries, bread, soups, baked goods such as German springerle, Italian biscotti, sweets (licorice candies, especially aniseed balls), dried figs, desserts, cream cheese, pickles, coleslaw, egg dishes and non-alcoholic beverage. It is a favourite flavouring for alcoholic drinks in Mediterranean region, such as French Pastis, Pernod, Anisette, and Ricard, Greek Ouzo, Turkish Raki and Arabian Arak, and also South American Aguardiente, Russian Alasch, Puerto Rican, Tres Castillos. Ansieed oil is a component in German Boonekamp, Benediktener, Goldwasser and Spanish Pacharan and Ojen. Anisette combines ansie, coriander and fennel seeds in sweet vodka. Anise and anise oils are used in Italian sausage, pepperoni, pizza topping and other processed meat items. Anise is an essential component of Italian Anise cake and cookies. All parts of the plant can be used in the kitchen. The flowers and seed can be added to the fruit salads. Freshly chopped leaves also enhance dips, cheese spreads, vegetables or green salads. The stem and roots of anise are mixed with stews and soups to give a hint of licorice (Shuster 1992, Tainter and Grenis 1993, Brown 1998) (4). 

Anise fruits, either whole or ground, are used as a spice. They flavour curries, sweets, confectionery and baked goods, and are an ingredient of various alcoholic drinks or beverages, e.g. anisette of France, ‘raki’ of Turkey. ‘arguardiente’ of Latin America and ‘anisado’ of the Philippines. Leaves are used for flavouring and garnishing. In the Netherlands sugar-coated anise seeds are given to guests visiting a new-born baby. Fruits yield an essential oil, which is increasingly replacing fruits. Odour is not only attractive to humans, as is evidenced by its use as a lure for fox hounds and as bait for mice, rats and fish. Oil is also used in perfumery, soups and other toilet articles, as sensitizer for bleaching colours in photography and as an insecticide against small insects. The residue left after essential oil distillation may serve as cattle feed. 

Because of the similarity in seed aroma and flavour it is confused with fennel and is available in the market under the name ‘Lucknow Saunf’. Used to flavour food materials and is as well a constituent of traditional medicine. Aniseed is used for flavouring food, confectionery, bakery products, cake and cookies, soups, stews, breads, chewing gums and tobacco. Also used in flavouring alcoholic beverages. Aniseed oil is used for flavouring soap, in perfumery, and other toilet articles, in perfuming sachets, in dental preparation, mouthwashes and toothpastes. Fresh leaves of the plants are used as a garnish and flavoring salad. They are eaten as pot herb, which contains vitamin C (8.7 mg/g) in essential oil. Oil is used externally as an insecticide against small insects. Aniseed is widely used in food and confectionery. It is also used as a flavouring agent in alcoholic drinks (1). 

Highest average maximum use levels for anise oil are about 0.06% (570 ppm) in alcoholic beverages and 0.07% (681 ppm) in sweets (Luang and Foster, 1996). Suggested use rate of oleoresin anise is 7.5 to 9.0% (Heath, 1981). 

Constituents in the volatile oils are useful in pest control. The essential oils extracted from anise were found to be toxic to two green house pests, viz. the carmine spider mite (Tetranychus cinnabarinus) and cotton aphid (Aphis gossypii Glov. Sarac and Tunc indicated that the essential oil had a high residual toxicity to adults of Tribolium confusum and was the most repellent to Sitophilus oryzae adults (4).

Parts Used: Fruit 


Harvesting Practices: Anise fruits are ready for harvest when they turn grayish-green. Umbels come to maturity by March to mid April. Terminal umbels mature first and the laterals 15 to 20 days later. When 80-90% of fruit begin to turn greyish-green, the top of the plant including branches is cut and tied into bundles and stacked upto 2 m height. The uneven ripening of the fruits, which starts on the main stem and continues later on the side branches, leads to a dilemma: early harvesting will yield immature fruits of low quality, but late harvesting results in shattering of the fruits. Thus, two harvests may be desirable, though this is more expensive in terms of labour costs. In practice, anise is harvested when 80-90% of the fruits turn grayish-green or when the fruits in the main umbel are completely ripe. Harvesting is done by pulling the plants or cutting off the tops. Largely areas are combine harvested, but garden plots are harvested by hand.

Yield 
Average yields of anise fruits range from 0.7-1.5 t/ha. Yield of anise oil is about 15 kg/ha.

Post Harvest Handling
Harvested and bundled tops of anise are stacked up to 2 m high in the field with the fruiting heads toward the centre and left there for 4-5 days for further drying and fruit ripening. The moisture content of the fruits should have fallen to 10-20% before they are threshed, cleaned and stored in jute bags lined with polythene in a cool, dry place. Storage is difficult, because the essential-oil content decreases rapidly. It is therefore recommended to store fruits in airtight containers in a cool and dark place. For the extraction of anise oil, fruits should be crushed first and distilled immediately to reduce oil loss due to evaporation (3). 
Stability during storage, irradiation and heat processing
Anise has to be stored away from daylight and kept in a dry place in cool conditions (DAB 10 Eur, ÖAB 90, BP 88, PFX, Helv VII). The average loss of content of volatile oil is 1% of the original content per month. The content of trans-anethole decreases from 89% to 73% during a storage of six weeks with the influence of sunlight, the content of cis-anethole (more toxic isomer of trans-anethole) increases from 0.8% to 4.5% and the content of anisaldehyde increases from 0.8% to 7.0%. additional decomposition products are also formed. Because of the sensitivity to light and oxidation it is recommended that the volatile oil of anise is stored in well filled and well closed containers (glass or tin, not plastic) protected against daylight. Hence, a storage temperature of below 10˚C is recommended by PFX. During long-term storage there is an increase in anise ketone and anisic acid also. Moisture content of the seeds or humidity conditions of the storages atmosphere is the most important parameter to be considered for anise storage. At high moisture levels, the deteriorative reactions and off-flavours are inevitable in addition to the increased rate of loss of volatile oil by diffusion (Saklar and Esin, 1994) (4). 


Produce and Products: Essential oil: Anise oil is steam distilled from the crushed seeds of Pimpinella anisum. The essential oil floats on top of the water during the distillation, which is separated off. It is colourless to faintly yellow which solidifies upon cooling to about 15-19˚C due to the crystallization of anethole (4). Anise contains an essential oil, composed of anethole (main constituent of the aniseed oil), isoanethole and anisic aldehyde (1).

Oleoresin: Oleoresin anise is a yellow-green to orange-brown fluid. Volatile oil content of oleoresin is 15-18%. The presence of large quantities of fixed oil in this product results limits its shelf-life and the addition of permitted antioxidant is advised (Heath, 1981).

Chemistry:

Anise fruits contain per 100 g edible portion: water 9 g, protein 18 g, fat 16 g, carbohydrates 35 g, fibre 15 g,ash 7 g (Ca 646 mg, Fe 37 mg, Mg 170 mg, P 440 mg, Na 16 mg, Zn 5 mg). The energy value is about 1400kJ/100 g. the lipid fraction comprises about 10 g mono-unsaturated and 3 g poly-unsaturated fatty acids. The essential oil of anise, derived from the fruits, is a colourless or pale yellow, strongly refractive substance, liquid at temperatures above 20˚C, with the characteristic odour and sweet taste of the fruit. Anise oil is traditionally used with liquorice (Glycyrrhiza glabra L.), in liquorice candy. As a result the flavour of anise is often confused with that of liquorice and erroneously described as liquorice-like. Major constituents are:
 1-4% volatile oil.
 Coumarins: bergapten, umbelliprenine, umbelliferone, scopoletin.
 Ca. 8-16% lipids, including fatty acids: 50-70% petroselinic acid (C18:1), 22-28% oleic acid (C18:1), 5-9% linoleic acid (C18:2) and 5-10% saturated fatty acids mostly palmitic acid (C16:0).
 Β-amyrin and stigmasterol and its salts (palmitate and stearate)
 Flavonoid glycosides: quercetin-3-glucuronide, rutin, luteolin-7-glucoside, isoorientin, isovitexin, apigenin-7-glucoside (apigetrin).
 Myristicin
 Ca. 18% protein
 Ca. 50% carbohydrates and others

Fatty acids can be obtained by extraction in the remainders of oil extraction via. steam distillation. Lauric acid, which is most important to chemistry, is obtained from petroselinic acid which is found in high quantities (50-70%) in anise. The major constituent in volatile oil of aniseed is trans (E)-anethole (75-90%). Methyl chavicol (estragole) – 1.7-5.5%, anise ketone (para-methoxyphenylacetone) – 0.5-0.9% and β-caryophyllene are also present, but in lesser relative amounts. Other components in minor concentrations include anisaldehyde, anisic acid, linalool, limonene, α-pinene, acetaldehyde, p-cresol, cresol, hydroquinine, β-farnasene, γ-himachalene and ar-curcumene (Leung and Foster, 1996) (4).

The presence of the oxidation products may be and indication of oil deterioration. Anise is reported to contain coumarins, flavonoid glycosides, β-amyrin, sitosterol and myristicin.

Anise oil can cause skin irritation and induce nausea, seizures and pulmonary oedema. Anethole has been repirted as a cause of dermatitis. Long-term studies have shown that anethole is not carcinogenic. Methyl chavicol (estragole) is a hepatic carcinogen in mice. The reputed oestrogenic activity of anise, e.g. increasing milk secretion and promoting menstruation, is probably due to polymers of anethole, such as dianethole and photanetholes. Anise essential oil and anethole show antifungal activity. The essential oil also possesses antiviral activity against potato virus X (PVX), tobacco virus (TRSV). The spice and the essential oil have both antibacterial and antioxidant activity. Anethole, anisaldehyde myristicin exhibit mild insecticidal properties. The weight of 1000 seeds is 1-4 g. Oil from P. anisum is used interchangeably in the United States with that of star anise (Illicium verum Hook.f.), both under the name ‘anise oil’ (3).

The crop is grown for its seeds, which are rich in volatile oil containing about 84-87 percent anethole and 12-15% methyl chavicol. In other compounds include P-methoxy acetone, terpenes, isoanethole, anisic aldehyde and sulphur containing compounds (1). In the United States the regulatory status ‘generally recognized as safe’ has been accorded to anise (GRAS 2093) and anise oil (GRAS 2094) (3).

Anethole, a naturally occurring phenylpropanoid, is structurally related to catecholamines adrenaline, noradrenaline and dopamine (Newall et al. 1996).

Quality
According to ASTA, the standards for whole and ground anise are:
Moisture limit (10%) and ash and acid insoluble (not greater than 6.0 and 1.0%, respectively). Anise oil should contain 84-93% trans-anethole and 0.5-6.0% methyl-chavicol (estragole). Superior to star anise oil. Adulterants used are star anise oil, fennel oil and synthetic anethole. Pure anethole becomes fluid at +23˚C and solidifies at +21˚C.

Other uses:
Medicinal uses
Anise is considered as mild expectorant and added to cough medicines. It is also used to reduce the bitter taste of drugs (1). Anise fruits are considered stomachic, carminative, diaphoretic, diuretic, antispasmodic, antiseptic, stimulative galactagogue. In Malaysia, anise fruits are an ingredient of compound medicines against stomach-ache or colic, for instance together with leaves Blumea balsamifera (L.) DC. or Cajanus cajan (L.) Millsp. They are also used in Malaysia in a tonic taken after childbirth, and together with Phyllanthus leaves, they have been recommended against gonorrhoea. In folk medicine, crushed anise seeds mixed with oil of cloves into a paste and applied to the back of the neck, forehead and temples to relieve nervous headaches. The Aztecs chewed seeds to relieve flatulence. Infusion of seeds used for complaints ranging from infantile catarrh, tension and insomnia. Anise tea would be a pleasant remedy for sleeplessness than the old Eskimo cure – swallowing powdered animal bone washed down melted snow. Seeds relieve both hiccough and epilepsy; relieves indigestion when essence of aniseed is taken in hot water. Oil of aniseed is used in paregoric elixir, for its antiseptic properties in liquid dentifrice and as an insect repellent. Essential oil is applied as a carminative, stimulant, mild spasmolytic, weak antibacterial and expectorant, e.g. in cough mixtures and lozenges. The oil is also used in veterinary medicine against lice and mites (3). Anise oil and anethole are antibacterial, antifungal, antioxidant, stimulant, carminative and expectorant. Antibacterial activity is against Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pyogenes, Escherichia coli, Corynebacterium ovis, Salmonella enteriditis (Fyfe et al. 1998). Anethole showed antifugal activity against Candida albicans, Alternaria, Aspergillus, Cladosporium, Fusarium and Penicillium (Shukla and Tripathi 1987). Anethole, anisaldehyde and myristicin, along with d-carvone showed mild insecticidal properties (Leung and Foster 1996). Trans-anethole is inhibitory to food spoilage yeast Debaromyces hansenii (Curtis et al. 1996). Anise oil showed anitpyretic activity in rats and mice Afifi et al. 1994). Anethole is also structurally related to hallucinogenic compound myristicin. Anise oil is used internally for dyspeptic complaints and externally as an inhalant for congestion of the respiratory tract. The whole, crushed or ground crude drug can be used for infusion and other galenical preparations; e.g. several instant teas as powders containing aqueous extracts of aniseed, tea paste, some preparations with micro-encapsulated anise oil. It also serves as gastro-intestinal spasmolytic (Leung and Foster 1996, Bisset 1994). Olfactory masking with aniseed oil decreased aggression and prevented the decrease in milk production in dairy cattles. P-anisaldehyde has fungistatic activites, p-cresol is a disinfectant agent and cresols are used in veterinary practices as local antiseptics, parasiticides and disinfectants; hydroquinone has anti-bacterial, anti-tumor, anti-mitotic and hypertensive activities. It is cytotoxic to rat hepatoma cells. It can be used as a depigmentor and a photographic reducer and developer (Harborne et al.1999). 

In traditional medicine anise is used as aromatic carminative, stimulant, expectorant, oestrogenic agents to increase milk secretion, promote menstruation, facilitate birth, increase libido, and alleviate male climacteric (Leung and Foster 1996). The oil controls lice, used as a chest rub and ointment for scabies (Bown 1998). 

Excessive use of anise oil may induce nausea, vomiting, skin irritations, pulmonary odema and dermatitis in some people. Aniseed may cause allergic reaction. Anethole and estragole which are structurally related to safrole, a known heptotoxin and carcinogen is not reported to cause any such danger in man, but is hepatoxic to rodents (4).

Miscellaneous Information

  • A monograph on the physiological properties of anise oil has been published by the Research Institute for Fragrance Materials (RIFM).
  • Anise fruits are adulterated with exhausted fruits (after distillation), dust and other similar looking fruits and seeds. Ground anise is adulterated with ground funnel (Foeniculum vulgare Miller) (3).
  • Anise was so popular in medieval England as a spice, medicine, and perfume that in 1305 King Edward I placed a special tax on it to raise money to repair London Bridge. 
  • Anise oil is said to prove as tantalizing bait for mice, if smeared on traps. It is also used as the scent on the artificial rabbit in greyhound races. It is poisonous to pigeons (5).
