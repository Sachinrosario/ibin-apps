Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
It is found throughout the western Himalayas from Afghanistan to Garhwal, at elevations ranging from 1,200 m to 3,000 m above the sea level and is most common from 1,800 m to 2,600 m.  In India deodar forests are common from Kashmir to Garhwal and form the bulk of the vegetation in these regions.

The plant prefers light (sandy), medium (loamy) and heavy (clay) soils and requires well-drained soil. The plant prefers acid, neutral and basic (alkaline) soils and can grow in very alkaline soil. It cannot grow in the shade. It requires dry or moist soil and can tolerate drought. The plant can tolerates strong winds but not maritime exposure.  It cannot tolerate atmospheric pollution.  

Cedars can be grown in temperate climates. The most tender kind is C. Deodara, from the Himalayas, which can't be grown in very severe climates. They will thrive in almost any good, well-drained soil from acid sand to clay and limestone. The best soil is one that is light, well-drained, loam and peat.

Availability :  
A very large tall tree found in the North-West Himalaya extending east to the Dauli river tributary of the Alaknanda below Niti Pass and in the mountains of Afghanistan and in North Baluchistan.

It is distributed in North-West Himalayas between 1200-3000 m; from Afghanistan to UP, (Kumaon), often cultivated.  It is an ornamental tree & there are few trees in the world that can compare with it. It is also found in U.P., Himachal Pradesh, Jammu & Kashmir and Tamil Nadu.

Status : 

It is an ornamental tree. 

This was an important tree species in north India for domestic and also for commercial appliances.  Its wood is as good as Sal and Teak.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A large handsome evergreen conifer  tree with spreading branches and dark green (rarely silvery-blue) foliage, growing to a great size and to a great age. On an average mature forest trees form 8 to 12 ft in girth predominate, though for larger  trees, occasionally reaching up to 250 ft in height and 45 ft in girth are found. Rough, black, furrowed bark and spreading branching shoot, dimorphic;  Leaves needle-like triquetrous, sharp pointed; male cones solitary, cylindric at the ends of branchlets, female cones solitary at the ends of  branchlets, composed of imbricating thin woody plancental scales, seeds pale brown, wings longer than the seeds. The heartwood of deodar is light yellowish brown turning brown on exposure. It is oily, fragrant and strong.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

 

 


Breeding System :

The scented flowers are monoecious (individual flowers are either male or female, but both sexes can be found on the same plant)

Phenology : 

Flower from October to November and the seeds ripen from October to December.

Pollinators:
Pollinated by wind.

Back to Top

 

Seed Biology :

Seedling Germination :

Cones should be collected in winter and kept in warm room until they open. They should be sown immediately in a cold frame. One report says that a short cold stratification of one month improves germination rates. Seed pot should be kept moist, but one should be careful because the young seedlings are very prone to damp off, keep them well ventilated. Planting out of the seedlings into individual pots should be done, when they are large enough to handle.  Plants should be grown in a cold frame for their first winter and should planted into their permanent positions in late spring or early summer.  Protection should be given against winter cold for their first winter or two outdoors.

Seeds should be sown in light, sandy soil in a propagating house or frame. When they are large enough to handle, the seedlings are planted in a nursery border outside about 3 inches apart in rows 12 feet apart. They are transplanted every two years while they are young. Varieties that won't come true from seed should be grafted in the spring on stocks of their respective types. The stocks must be established in pots and the grafted plants placed in a closed propagating frame until the union is complete.

Cultivation:
Succeeds in warm dry areas with less than 40cm of rain a year, but also in areas with cool summers and up to 200cm of rain. Dislikes atmospheric pollution. Plants are fairly wind tolerant.

Small trees less than 50cm tall establish much more quickly and better than taller trees, those that are more than 2 m tall are difficult to establish. Larger trees will check badly and hardly put on any growth for several years. This also badly affects root development and wind resistance.

Plants are said to live for up to 600 years in the wild.

New growth takes place from May to the end of September and can exceed 1 m per year, slowing down as the tree gets larger and virtually ceasing by the time the tree is 20 m tall.

Small male cones are formed on the lower branches of trees, whilst the larger female cones are formed on higher branches. These female cones persist on the tree for 2 - 3 years before breaking up. The whole plant is aromatic.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES


Pests:

On Deodar about 60 insect species cause various degrees of damage during different stages of growth amongst which, Ectropis deodarae is considered a most injurious pest and control measures suggested.

Seedlings, both natural and nursery raised are subject  to attack by cockchafer grub which destroys the roots and cut worms (Agrotis ypsilon) which bite through the stems and roots.

Diseases: 

Trees are notably susceptible to honey fungus.

Deodar trees are also infected by a root rot disease, caused by Phytopthora cinnamomi, which can be controlled by applying Trichoderma viridae - a biological control agent.
Fungal diseases: Parasitic fungus Fomes annosus Fries (Trametes radiciperda Hartig) attack the roots especially of the young trees. Peridermium cedri Barcl. attacks young needles, resulting in the thinning of  foliage and dying of branches.

Processing Techniques

An earthen ghara, or vessel with a wide mouth and capable of containing about 4 seers, is sunk into the ground.  A large ghara of about 12 seers capacity is taken and three small holes are drilled in its under-side; it is then filled with scraps of the pine wood and over its mouth another smaller jar is placed and kept there by a coating of clay very carefully applied; and then both the jars are smeared over with a coating of clay.  These two jars thus stuck together are next set on the mouth of the receiver or ghara sunk into the ground and the joint or seat is made tight by a luting of stiff clay.  Light firewood is now heaped around the apparatus and ignited and kept burning from four to eight hours; the rationale of the process being that the heat causes the tar contained in the chips enclosed in the large ghara to exude and it falls through the three holes drilled in the bottom and into the receiver sunk into the ground.  When the fire is out the ashes are raked away the jars very carefully separated so that pieces of dirt may not fall into the receiver and the latter is then exhumed and the contents poured out.  It is only necessary to replace the receiver with the jars over it as before, duly charged with chips and lute the joints up carefully and the process can be carried on as before.  With care the same jars may be made to do over and over again without cracking.  One seer of wood yields about 2.6 chitaks of tar and 4.3 chitaks of charcoal.

Other Uses
  1. Timber - Used in railway sleepers, construction works, beams, poles, door and window-frames, shingles, bridge construction, carriage and wagon building, mast,  furniture, packing cases, brush backs, electric poles, carvings and several other purposes. In MP it is employed for boxes & locally by hill tribes for building purposes.
  2. Oil - Cedar wood oil is used for soap perfumery, antiseptic, diaphoretic, depurative and diuretic and is useful in leprosy, syphilis, skin diseases, wounds, ulcers, fever and stranguly.
  3. The heartwood is bitter, acrid, thermogenic, emollient, anodyne , anthelmintic, digestive, carminative, cardiotonic, galacto-purifier,anti-inflammatory, diuretic, expectorant, diaphoretic, antiseptic, laxative and febrifuge and is useful in inflammations, dyspepsia, epilepsy, fever, cardiac discolors, leucoderma, skin diseases.  It is used in the treatment of fevers, flatulence, pulmonary and urinary disorders, rheumatism, piles, kidney stones, insomnia, diabetes etc. It has been used as an antidote to snake bites.
  4. Bark - Astringent useful for fever, diarrohea, dysentery.
  5. Leaves are bitter, acrid and thermogenic and are useful in inflammations and tubercular glands.    In Ayurvedic medicine the leaves are used in the treatment of tuberculosis.
  6. The plant yields a medicinal essential oil by distillation of the wood, it is used in the treatment of phthisis, bronchitis, blennorrhagia and skin eruptions.
  7. Shelter and Wood Shelter-- A fairly wind-tolerant tree, it can be used in shelterbelt plantings.
