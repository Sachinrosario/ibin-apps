Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

A tall, evergreen tree found in temperate climate. Its altitudinal range higher than any other Himalayan conifer. Occurs naturally throughout the temperate zone of central and western Himalayas, mainly between 2000-3000 m though sometimes extending beyond these altitudinal limits. Rarely seen in Sikkim. It also occurs in Balipara tract of Assam. Owing to its capacity for springing up in dense even-aged crop, it may form extensive pure stands though at lower elevations, it is associated with chir pine, and at higher, with deodar, spruce, fir and broad-leaved species.

Found in variety of geological formations. A strong light-demander, though it may persist in shade for number of years. The species suffers from snow. Very sensitive to fire as it does not have thick, protective bark.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

A tall, evergreen tree with spreading or drooping branches. Bark smooth and resinous in young stems, turning grey and corky with shallow fissures on mature trees. Leaves needle like in clusters of five, abnormal fascicles containing 4, 6 or 7 needles are occasionally found.

Two types of branches are present: long shoot with unlimited growth; dwarf shoots with limited growth. Long shoots occur on the main stem as lateral buds in the axils of scale leaves, and terminate in an apical bud. Dwarf shoots are enclosed by a number of bud scales closely surrounded by a thick mat of hairs. Needles 10-20 cm long, bluish green, glaucous, persisting partly for 2-3 years. Five leaved pine.Plant is monoecious. Flowers arranged in a form of cones. Male and female cones borne on different branches of the same tree. Male cones are modified dwarf shoots and appear in clusters on the lower branches of the tree. Number of cones is 15. Each cone arises in the axil of a scale leaf, replaces a dwarf shoot and is surrounded by several bracts. Female cones replace the terminal buds of the long shoot. Male flowers catkin-like, arranged in cluster. Yellow to light brown, often pink towards the apex. Mature female cones lateral, solitary or in groups of 2 or 3, cylindrical, ripening after 18 months of their appearance. Light brown, very resinous and easily distinguishable from other pines. Seeds ovoid, 6-10 mm long (without wings); wings membranous and about as long as the seed.

Floral and seed biology:

Seed Germination & Propagation: Under favourable conditions, given abundance of light and protection from fire and grazing, the blue pine regenerates profusely. Regeneration fails to establish in the areas which are annually or periodically burnt. All the same, thorough burning of an area produces a favourable bed where regeneration is profuse. Artificial regeneration through direct sowing of seeds is preferred to transplanting nursery-raised stock. Food stored in the seed breaks down during germination and is the only source of energy for the germinating embryo. Seed germinates 20-25 days after sowing. The radicle emerges and penetrates into the soil. Hypocotyl elongates, straightens and carries above the ground the remnant of the seed alongwith the cotyledons. Cotyledons persist for a long time and are shed after the juvenile leaves and the long shoot have grown considerably.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Fuel: Wood is a good fuel and yields excellent charcoal. 

Used for internal fittings of residential houses. The wood after treatment commonly used for making packing cases, camp furniture, drawing boards, fermentation vats and lorry bodies and shingles and railway sleepers. Also used to make cheap pencils, battery separators, violins and matchboxes. Stumps used for burning as torches.

Other uses:

Resin - Though its resin is superior to that of the chir pine, blue pine is not tapped for its resin.

Bark - Bark contains a fair amount of colouring matter which is used for dying silk and wool. Also employed for roofing huts.

Needles - Needles mixed with plaster, employed in house construction. In winter, needles exude sweet manna, which is edible and used for adulterating honey. Oil is extracted from the needles.

Oil - Roots yield oil, applied by the workers in the paddy fields to keep off water insects.

Miscellaneous Information

In pure chir belt, absolute maximum temperature varies from 32 to 380 (90 to 1000 F) whereas minimum absolute temperature falls below freezing point in most parts of the region.

Pines have tap root system. Primary root soon becomes arrested while the long roots continue to grow and bear clusters of dwarf roots. Some of the roots branch dichotomously, form coralloid masses, have an ectotrophic mycorrhiza and are called mycorrhizal roots. Majority of fungi forming this belong to families Boletaceae and Agaricaceae (P. Maheshwari and Konar 1971). These fungi occur loose in the soil and can infect directly. A seedling with mycorrhiza acts as an inoculum for the spread of infection tot he adjoining seedlings. Excessive mortality has been recorded when good-quality seedlings have been grown in soil other than forest soil.

Chir pine is characterised by twisted fibre in some localities, the twist may being either left-handed or right-handed. Twisted chir, even when the degree of twist may be small, is unsuitable for sawing into planks or sleepers for which the wood is principally used. Plant is a source of turpentine.


Plant shows mycorrhizal association. Mycorrhizal associates found are Amanita berkeleyi (Hook. F.) Bas, A. emilii Riel., A. gemmata (Fr.) Bert., A. vaginata (Bull. ex. fr.) Vitt., Lactarius sanguifluus (Paulet ex Fr.) Fr., L. laccata, Scleroderma verrucosum, S. arcolatum, S. dictysporum, Rhizopogon sp, Suillus spp., Astrcus hygrometricus, Amanita vaginata, Lepista nuda, Suillus sibricus, Boletus edulis, Cantharellus cibarius, Scleroderma texense, Rhizopogon rubescence, Thelephora terrestris.

Wood: Sapwood distinct from heartwood. Sapwood white to pale yellowish-white, heartwood light pinkish-red to light red with darker striations, resinous, straight and fairly even grained, medium fine textured, soft and moderately heavy, average weight 515 kg/m3 (32 lb/ft3) at 12 per cent moisture content. Specific gravity 0.5, 513 kg/ c m. wood lustrous when freshly exposed, becomes more or less dull with age.

The timber next to deodar in commercial importance.

A non-porous wood. Growth rings distinct on all the three surfaces. Transition from early wood to late wood is usually gradual. Resin canals of the vertical type scattered, large and distinct to the eye appearing prominent dark lines or resin streaks on longitudinal surfaces. Rays very fine, not visible to eye, but seen under the lens as fine, numerous, closely spaced lines.
