HABIT & HABITAT
A prostrate, glabrescent, polymorphic shrub or climber armed with divaricate light yellow thorns, occurring in dry rocky and stony soils of north western India, through Punjab, Rajasthan, to the Deccan Peninsula.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Leaves variable in texture, orbicular to elliptic, base rounded, apex mucronate; flowers white, solitary, axillary; berry ellipsoid, ovoid orobovoid, pericarp thin; seeds 3-4 mm in diam.

Seed Biology :

Seed Germination : 
Fresh caper seeds germinate readily - but only in low percentages. Dried seeds become dormant and are notably difficult to germinate and therefore require extra measures to grow. Dried seeds should be initially immersed in warm water (40°C or 105°F ) and then let soak for 1 day. Seeds should be wrapped in a moist cloth, placed in a sealed glass jar and kept in the refrigerator for 2 - 3 months. After refrigeration, soak the seeds again in warm water overnight. Plant the seeds about 1 cm deep in a loose well-drained soil media. Young caper plants can be grown in a greenhouse (preferable minimum temperature of 10°C or 50°F).
Cultivation:).
"Collect stem cuttings in February, March or April. Use stems from the basal portions, greater than 1 cm diameter and 8 cm in length with 6-10 buds. Use a loose well drained media with bottom heat. A dip in a IBA solution of 1.5 to 3.0 ppm is recommended (15 seconds). A 70% rooting percentage would be considered good.
"Transplanting is carried out during the wet winter and spring periods, and first-year plants are mulched with stones.
"In Italy, plants are spaced 2 to 2.5 meters apart (depending on the roughness of the topography; about 2,000 plants per hectare). A full yield is expected in 3 to 4 years. Plants are pruned back in winter to remove dead wood and waterspouts. Pruning is crucial to high production. Heavy branch pruning is necessary, as flower buds arise on one-year-old branches. Three-year-old plants will yield 1 to 3 kilograms of caper flower buds per plant.
"Grown from seed, in California caper bushes reportedly begin to flower in the fourth year, however Italian sources report some flowering from first year transplants.
"Caper plantings will last 20 to 30 years."

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Flowers, Leaves, roots, Root bark, and Whole plant is also used.

Uses

  • The dried flower-buds have an acrid, astringent taste and are useful in scurvy. The pickled flower-buds constitute the commercial European capers. They are used along with the fruits, principally for flavouring pickles, relishes and sauces.
  • In India, they are used in curries as a condiment. In Ladakh, young leaves are used as vegetable.
  • The leaves are eaten by sheep and goats. Additionally, ash from burned caper roots has been used as a source of salt.

Chemistry
A sample of leaves from Ladakh contained crude protein, 17.9; fat, 4.9; crude fibre, 6.8; and total carbohydrates, 47.7%; calcium, 550; phosphorus, 135;iron, 72; and sodium, 85 mg/100g. The leaves and fruits from USSR, contain respectively: ascorbic acid, 70.8,and 135.5; iodine, traces, and 68.8 mg/lOOg. The flower buds Contain rutin, rutic acid, quercetin-3-rutinoside,kaempferol-3-rutinoside, pectic acid, pentosans, anisothiocyanate glycoside, a volatile emetic constituent,and a saponin. A flavonol glycoside, quercetin-7-0-glucorhamnoside is reported from the aerial parts. The juice of the leaves and fruits possesses anticystic, fungicidal and bactericidal activities. The seeds yield a pale yellow oil (34- 36%; acid val, 7.1-44.1; and iod val,115-125). The fatty acid composition of the oil is as follows: palmitic and stearic, 7-9; oleic, 42-46; and linoleic, 45-51%. The presence of vitamin E (134mg/lOOg) in the oil is also reported. The seeds and leaves contain glucocapparin and glucocleomin. The cortex and leaves contain stachydrine. The rootbark contains stachydrine, rutic acid and a volatile substance with garlic odour. Stachydrine, when given to dogs, rabbitsand rats, quickens the coagulation of blood (Kaul et al,loc. cit.; Zechner in Wiesner, 82; Ilyina, 410; Kjaer & Thomsen, Phytochemistry, 1963, 2, 29; Chem Abstr,1981,95,129367; 1977,86,185906; 1978,89, 87180; 1969,70,112377; Biol Abstr, 1973, 56, 21676).

Other Uses

1.      The plant is credited with anti tubercular property.

2.      The rootbark is extensively used in Ayurvedic system of medicine. The bark is bitter, aperient, diuretic and expectorant.

3.      It is given in splenic, renal and hepatic complaints. The bruised leaves are applied as a poultice in gout. An extract of the plant is one of the constituents of the Ayurvedic preparation 'Liv 52' administered to treat preliminary cases of acute viral hepatitis and cirrhosis of liver, and has shown encouraging results against viral infection in man. Liv 52 is also reported to protect albino rats against toxic effects of beryllium compounds, and mice against Semliks Forest Virus (SFV). The plant extract is also a constituent of another drug 'Geriforte' useful in treating senile pruritis, itching and other ailments associated with old age and anxiety neurosis

4.      Capers are said to reduce flatulence and to be anti-rheumatic in effect. In ayurvedeic medicine capers (Capers=Himsra) are recorded as hepatic stimulants and protectors, improving liver function. Capers have reported uses for arteriosclerosis, as diuretics, kidney disinfectants, vermifuges and tonics. Infusions and decoctions from caper root bark have been traditionally used for dropsy, anemia, arthritis and gout. Capers contain considerable amounts of the anti-oxidant bioflavinoid rutin.

5.      Caper extracts and pulps have been used in cosmetics, but there has been reported contact dermatitis and sensitivity from their use
