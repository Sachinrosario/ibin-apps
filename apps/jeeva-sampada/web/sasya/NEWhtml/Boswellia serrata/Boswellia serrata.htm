Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
A moderate or large branching tree, commonly found in the dry forests in the foothills of the Western Himalayas, and from Rajasthan to Bihar Southwards through central Indian to Andhra Pradesh.  It is very common in the dry rocky hills of central  and western Indian in association with Sterculia urens, Odina Wodier and Cochlospermum gossypium. The tree is commonly gregarious and in some forests the tree forms almost pure stands.

Altitude: up to 1 150 m, Mean annual temperature: 0-45 deg. C, Mean annual rainfall: 500-2 000 mm

Soil type: Typically grows on rocky ridges; it does well on neutral soils above gneiss, schist, quartzite, limestone and sandstone. The species has the ability to thrive in the poorest and the shallowest soils where most of its associates remain stunted.

B. serrata is a species characteristic of the tropical dry deciduous forests and occurs in very dry teak forests or in dry mixed deciduous forests in association with species such as Terminalia spp., Anogeissus latifolia and Acacia leucophloea. It is characteristically found on the slopes and ridges of hills, as well as on flat terrain, attaining a larger size on fertile soils. It is resistant to drought and resists fire better than other species in its zone of occurrence. The tree is also frost hardy and serves as a nurse tree for other species.

Availability :  
The global distribution of this species is reported from Arabia to Indian subcontinent.  Within India, its occurrence is conspicuous in the dry hills in Central, Northwest and Peninsular India.  It is often found gregarious on the slopes and ridges of hills and attains large size on fertile soils.  In India, it is found in Bihar, M.P., West Bengal, Himachal Pradesh, Punjab, A.P, Karnataka, Kerala, T.N., Goa, Gujarath, Dadra & Nagar Haveli, Maharashtra and Rajasthan.

Native: India, Pakistan

According to an estimate salai forest cover an area of about 15,000 sq. km.  At present, it is tapped only in Shivpuri, Tilamgarh, and Guna forest division. It is a leafy forest tree of the Coromandel coasts and other parts of India. Though formerly thought to furnish olibanum, this tree is not the source of that drug, but yields a soft odorous resin which slowly hardens within a period of a year, and is used only by the natives as incense.


Status : 

It is valuable for afforestation, can be grown in semi-rocky soils.  It can be planted as wind breaks and shelter belts.

Ornamental: It is popular for avenue planting in India.  

Common in dry deciduous forests, on hill slopes.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A medium-size deciduous up to 19 m in height, 3-5’in girth. The bark is  ash coloured papery and  peels off in thin flakes. Young shoots pubescent. Leaves alternate, imparipinnate, crowded at the ends of branches. Each branch produces 7.2±2.3, recemose  inflorescence; each inflorescence produces 20-90 flowers. Flowers are pinkish white bracteate, pedicellate, bisexual, actinomorphic,  The sepals are reduced to five small clefts. There are 10 dithecous, adnate stamens arranged in two whorls of five each. Stamens of outer whorl are larger. Pollen grains are trizonocolporate with a faintly rugulose exine; they are shed at 3-celled condition and contain starch as the reserve material. Fresh pollen grains show over 85% viability. Under laboratory conditions they lose viability gradually reaching 0% in 7 days.  The stigma is capitate and is of the wet papillate type. Stigmatic exudate is copious. The style is hollow with three flattened stylar canals. The stylar canal is bordered by a layer of  well-defined canal cells. Ovary superior, trilocular with  a prominent bright-red annular nectary disc. Each locule bears a single ovule on axile  placentation. The lower half of the ovary is embedded in the disc.

The fruit is a simple, dry dehiscent septicidal capsule. Fruits split open alone the septa and release winged seeds. The seeds are heart-shaped, dark brown and have a single wing surrounding the seed. The seeds are non-endospermous at maturity and each cotyledon is deeply trilobed.

Two varieties  are usually distinguished:

a)      Var.serrata having pubescent leaves with crenate-serrate margins and

b)      Var  glabra  which has entire, glabrous leaves with entire margins.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY


Breeding System :

Obligate outbreeder and self-incompatible.

 

Phenology :
In India, the white flowers appear in stout racemes at the ends of branches from the end of January to March-April; sometimes flowers may appear before the fall of old leaves or after the appearance of new leaves. The drupes ripen in May-June. The leaves turn yellowish to light brown before they nearly all fall in December; the new leaves appear in May-June.

Fruit dehisce and disperse with winged seeds by the third or fourth week of May.

Pollinators:
Apis dorsata (the giant Asian honey bee)  and A. indica  are the active foragers, visit flowers most  frequently and are  effective pollinators. Oxycetonia versicolor and Mylabris sp. occasionally  visit the flowers but carried no pollen grains.

Seed Biology :

Seeds have poor viability; however, they can be stored dry in tins for not more than 6-9 months. There are 13,400-25,600 seeds/kg.

Split fruit open along the septa and release winged seeds. Seeds are heart-shaped, dark brown and have a single wing surrounding the seed. Seeds remain viable only for 5 months under laboratory conditions.

Seedling Germination :

Natural propagation is usually good, even in the poorest of locations. Some regeneration is due to coppice and suckers, but the species also reproduces fairly well from seed. Mature seeds may be collected off the trees to facilitate artificial propagation. The seeds should be immersed in water before they are sown, to separate out the sterile pyrenes, which float on the surface. Seed germination takes between 7 and 15 days. B. serrata has the remarkable ability to sprout from large branch or stem cuttings.

Ripe seeds are collected off the trees and should be soaked in water.  Only those which sink in water should be selected for sowing.  Seeds should be directly sown immediately after collection, at the beginning of monsoon, following overnight treatment in mild hot water (for 24 months).  Seeds germinate over a period of 7 to 15 days and about 50 percent seed germination is observed. Germination is epigeal. Untreated seeds show 45% germination. Seeds treated with gibberellic acid (100 ppm, for one h) showed 75% germination. The epicotyl and the cotyledons of freshly emerged seedling are pinkish-red. After 2-3 days of exposure to bright sunlight, their colour changes to dark green.

During monsoon a large number of seedlings emerge below the canopy of trees. Young seedling perennate by means of an underground tuber and the aerial shoot dries up with the onset of winter. The tuber produces new shoot during the subsequent monsoon. This cycle of  production of new shoot from perennating tuber during monsoon and shoot drying continues for three years. A permanent shoot emerges only during the forth year. Seedling establishment is very poor. Although large number of shoots come up below the canopy each year, there was hardly any recruitment of young trees in the forests.

Cultivation:

The mixed forests in which B. serrata occurs are worked under selection-cum-improvement or under one of the coppice systems such as the coppice with standards, with a rotation of 40 years, or the coppice with reserve system, with the same rotation age. It produces root suckers, coppices and pollards well. The power to produce these vegetative shoots depends upon the climate and edaphic factors.

Planting season for 8 to 10 week old seedlings is April- May.  This crop can be raised by planting in containers.  These establish readily provided they are protected against browsers.  Polythene bags raised seedlings of 10 to 12 weeks are planted out in the field.  It does not tolerate transplanting since the root system of this species is very delicate.  About 45 percent of the seedlings survive field planting.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests: 

The bark of the felled log has the property of maintaining its green and healthy condition for some months. During this period, there is little liability to insect attack; but when decay sets in, larvae of the beetle Atractocerus reversus bore the wood. The alternative is to debark the logs and free them from attack by Atractocerus, but the peeled logs are freely attacked by Platypus, and Xyleborus spp. while the surface is moist and by powder post beetle when the surface has dried slightly. 
Diseases:

 White spongy sap rot is known to attack trees in avenues and forests. The tree is also subject to attack by other fungi that cause spongy heart rot, mottled sap rot, spongy root and butt rot, and white fibrous rot.

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Wood, Bark, Gum-resin

Uses

Gum or resin: The tree yields a yellowish-green gum-oleoresin known as ‘salai guggal’ from wounds in the bark. This gum has an agreeable scent when burnt. A mature tree yields about 1-1.5 kg of gum a year. It is said to be a good substitute for imported Canada balsam. It is also tapped for resin called ‘lobal’, which is used as incense.

  • The commercially used gum resin called 'Salai guggal or Indian Olibanum' is the solidified oliogum-resin exuded from the tree bark after injury. A good quality resin  is of golden colour and is transparent. It is said to be a good substitute for imported Canada balsam. It is also tapped for resin called ‘lobal’, which is used as incense.  
  • The crude oleo-gum-resin possess excellent adhesive properties and is used in calico- printing and textile industry. 
  • The gum-resin is fragrant and burns with a pleasant odour and is used as incense in the manufacture of agarbattis and incense sticks. 
  • It is also used as a fumigant. 
  • In recent years it has found important use in pharmaceutical formulations for relieving pains and aches particularly  associated with arthritis. 
  • Many commercial formulations based on salai guggul extracts in the form of ointments and tablets (‘Care’ ointment from Gufic Ltd, ‘Nopane’ tablets from Daur Research Foundation) are available in the market. Also, Boswellia pain relieving cream and Boswellia capsules are advertised in the internet as anti-inflammatory and antiarthritic agent. 
  • The gum-resin is also credited with  expectorant, diuretic, diaphoretic and antiseptic properties. It is reported to be useful in ulcers, goiter, cystic fibrosis of the breast, diarrohea, dysentery, piles and skin diseases. Used in the preparation of an ointment for sores and with butter for treatment of syphilis. The non-phenolic fraction of the oleo-gum-resin possesses anti-tumour, sedative and analgesic activity. The gum-resin is also reported to possess marked cholesterol and triglyceride lowering activity,  and hyperlipidaemic and anti-artherosclerotic activity .
  • It is also used for lighting fires.


Harvesting Practices

The fresh exudation from the punctured resin ducts comes in 5-8 centimeter long tears.  It hardens in about four days retaining its golden brown colour and transparency. Tapping extends from November to June. An average tree yields about 1kg of salai gum every year. 

The tapping of Boswellia gum for industrial purposes has not met with much success, because of very variable yield.  Generally trees over 30" in girth and also those damaged by borers yield the gum in same quantity while some trees do not exude any.  Dwarfed and suppressed trees or very old trees give poor yield.  Tapping does no injury to the tree and the method of tapping influence the yield.  

One method, which is reported to have met with some success, consists of shaving off a thin band of bark(6" in width) 2-2.5' from the base of the tree, thus the reddish phloem in which the resin canal and ducts lie is exposed and freshening it every 4th or 5th day.  The gum exudes usually after the first freshening. The first collection is made after 1 or 2weeks.   Freshening is done from time to time and the original blaze in slowly widened.
Tapping period extends from Nov. to June or July.  But the overall yield from large areas of forest is too small to be economical.

It must however, be remembered that the general degradation of the natural forests has considerably influenced the natural regeneration of this tree.  Salai being localized to hill sides, dry slopes and other refractory sites is considered as an outcome of ecological residue and as such it is difficult to regenerate the species artificially.  Excessive exploitation of this species thus may not be desirable.

Resin canals are long tubular structures which ramify both vertically and horizontally.  When trees are tapped during the rainy season, most of exudate gets washed off and also its colour gets darker. No satisfactory method for tapping gum-resin has been developed. Blazing all round or leaving a little space is the common method. In Bihar, 15 cm wide shaving of the bark around the bole at a height of 16 cm above the ground level is practiced. Fresh exudation from punctured resin-ducts comes in 5-8 cm long tears, resembling Canada Balsam.

Produce and Products
Volatile oil, gum-resin called  salai guggal or ‘Indian olibanum’.

Chemistry
Gum contains arabinose, galactose, xylose, diastatic enzymes galacturonic acid, digitoxose, rhamnose, the volatile oil obtained from gum oleo-resin contains œ-pinene, œ-phellandrene, sesquiterpene alcohols, anisaidehyde phenols. Aqueous fraction gave poly-saccharides A and B. Three triperpene acids, œ, ß and ÿ-boswellic acids have been reported. The anti inflammatory and anti arthritic properties are attributed to the presence of ß-boswellic acid and related triterpene acids.

Olibanum is a translucent, brittle, whitish-yellow substance, in roundish, club-shaped, pear-shaped, or irregular tears, and usually covered by a whitish, farinaceous substance produced by the pieces rubbing against each other. It has a sub-acrid, terebinthinate, bitter taste, and a pleasant, resinous odor, and when burned, it produces a brilliant flame, and diffuses an agreeable aroma. It melts with difficulty, not without decomposition, becomes soft and adhesive by chewing, forms an incomplete, white emulsion when rubbed up with water, and is dissolved by alcohol to the amount of about 65 per cent. It has a specific gravity of 1.22

Olibanum consists chiefly of an acid resin (56 per cent), soluble in alcohol and having the formula C20H32O4 (Hlasiwetz, 1867); it yields no benzene derivatives when fused with caustic potash. When burned, it emits an agreeable odor. Water removes from it a bitter, viscid substance, little soluble in ether. Olibanum also contains gum (30 to 36 per cent), insoluble in alcohol, and resembles ordinary gum arabic. With 3 parts of water it forms a thick mucilage (Pharmacographia). Finally, olibanum contains about 3 per cent of ash, and from 4 to 7 per cent of a volatile oil. According to Kurbatow (1874), it consists chiefly of a terpene olibene (C10H16), boiling at 158° C. (316.4° F.). Flückiger found it laevo-rotatory, and Wallach (1889) identified it as l-pinene, and in addition found dipentene. Schimmel & Co. also report the occurrence of phellandrene in the oil. 

Indian Olibanum contains moisture 10-11%, volatile oil 8-9%, rosin 55-57%, Gum 20-23%, insoluble matter 4-5%.

Processing Techniques
Gum is refluxed with ethanol, cooled and centrifuged.  Centrifugate is concentrated and stirred with ethyl acetate. To this is added 5% NaOH solution and stirred. Lower aq. Layer is drained and cooled. Carefully neutralized with Hydrochloric acid. The ppt. Obtained is centrifuged, Dried, blended with Lactose, pulverized and packed.

Other Uses

 

  1. Among the Gonds of Uttar Pradesh, the stem bark is chewed to relieve toothache and to check pus formation in the mouth. A lukewarm paste of the bark is applied externally to relieve eye-inflammations.
  2. B. serrata is the only non-coniferous source of turpentine in India.  It  is found suitable for paints and varnish making.
  3. Fibre: B. serrata has recently come into prominence as a raw material for pulp paper and newsprint. Experiments show that writing and printing papers of suitable strength can be prepared when 25-40% long-fibred bamboo pulp is mixed in the finish. The bark can also be used for cordage.
  4. Fodder: It is not readily browsed by cattle, although in India, it is considered a substitute fodder for buffaloes.
  5. Fuel: The wood is a good fuel. Charcoal made from it is particularly favoured for iron smelting.
  6. In folk medicine, the powdered flowers are used to treat colds and fevers.
  7. Medicine: The salai guggal gum is used as a diaphoretic and astringent.
  8. Ornamental: It is popular for avenue planting in India.
  9. Other products: B. serrata has been recorded in West Bengal as a new lac host
  10. Reclamation: The tree is a suitable species for afforestation on poorer soils in areas with a mean annual rainfall of 500-1 250 mm. It is valuable for reclothing dry sandstone hills which have been denuded of vegetation, where soil is markedly ferruginous and where complete protection against biotic factors is not always feasible. Under such circumstances, its ability to withstand the adverse effects of forest fires, its immunity to being browsed or lopped for fodder, its power of resisting the effects of insulations and drought, and its capacity for reproduction by seed, coppice and root suckers are great assets.
  11. The bark is sweet, acrid, cooling and used in tonic. It is good for vitiated conditions of pitta  asthma, dysentery, ulcers, hemorrhoids and skin diseases.
  12. The wood is also used for paper pulp. The wood is a good fuel. Charcoal made from it is particularly favoured for iron smelting.
  13. Timber wood is used for second grade plywood. Used in agarbattis (Joss sticks) and for lighting coal stoves.  It is used in cheap furniture, ammunition boxes, mica boxes, packing cases, cement barrels, well construction, water pipes, matches, plywood and veneers.

Miscellaneous Information

The specific name, serrata, comes from serra (a saw) referring to the toothed leaf-margins.

Religious aspects;

Tribals particularly  from Madhya pradesh plant the tree near the temple and worship. Believed that evil spirit will be eliminated if grow near the temples and homes.

Olibanum is the frankincense of the ancients and was among the offerings of the Magi to the infant Savior. It constituted a large part of the incense so frequently alluded to in the Scriptures. It is collected in the Somali country by making deep incisions into the trunk of the tree from which the milky gum-resin exudes and soon concretes. The clear tears are first gathered, and the portion which has run down the side of the tree or has fallen to the ground, constitutes an inferior sort.
