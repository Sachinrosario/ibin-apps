DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

A perennial herbs; root stock bearing tubers; leaves mostly suffused purple beneath but usually green above, oblong, acute, usually more than twice longer than broad; inflorescence raceme loose, simple or forked, with large sheath at base; flowers mostly 2 together and the upper one with 2 small bractlets, flower bracts oblong or nearly orbicular, bright red, erect and rather narrow, petals 2, upper staminodia 3 oblanceolate entire or emarginate; fruits papillose, 3-angled, 3-valved, bearing persistent calyx.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Rokshi (a local drink) is prepared from roots. (Singh et al, 1999)

Sikkim has a wide spectrum of traditional and cultural diversity. Lepcha, Bhutia and Nepali are the three major ethnic groups in the state in which Lepchas (literally the “ravine folk”) are the original inhabitants of Sikkim (Chopra, 1979). The Lepchas are Mongoloid descent and predominantly Budhists but many of them are also Christians. They are mostly concentrated in the central part of Sikkim and prefer in dwelling on the steep hillsides.

Their main occupation is agriculture. The polyandry marriages are permitted amongst the Lepchas although this is now becoming very rare. For marriage (past times), the maternal uncle of the boy approaches the parents of the girl with some bottles of liquor like Rokshi, etc. and some money (Verma, 1990). This Rokshi is a traditional and indigenously prepared local herbal drink, and is favourite drink of all the family members except tender children.

Processing Techniques:

The two important plants used in the preparation of Rokshi are Canna edulis Ker-Gawl. (Vernacular name: Phutarul; Family Cannaceae) and Zea mays Linn. (Vernacular name Makai; Family – Poaceae). The starchy root of Canna edulis is washed thorougly with water and cut into thin slices (2/3 mm. thick). Then it is mixed with the corn (Zea mays) powder in the ratio 6 : 1 by weight. 5 gm. of yeast (Vernacular name : Marcha ) is added with every 1 kg. of the above mixture is kept airtight inside a polythene bag or wrapped with banana leaf or other suitable plant leaves, for 7 –12 days, according to the temperature (undergoing fermentation process).

The apparatus consists of a long cylindrical silver or earthen vessel having a bit narrow neck, locally called as bhara. A stand having three legs is kept inside the bhara enable to keep a vessel on it to collect the Rokshi. The above fermented mixture are kept inside the long vessel. Another vessel having rounded bottom is kept on the top of the long vessel by keeping a cloth washer, in between them to keep it air-tight. Three – fourth of the top vessel is filled up with cold water with the intention to liquify the Rokshi vapour by contacting the cold surface and also for weight to keep air tight in between the two vessels. Then the lower vessel (having the mixture) is heated till prolong boiling. The vapour liberated from the mixture condensed into droplets on the outer surface of the bottom of the upper vessel by contacting its cold surface. The droplets are gathering at the centre of the bottom of the upper vessel & falling as droplets due to gravity and it is collected in the vessel which is kept inside the long bottom vessels as shown in the diagram (Fig. 1). In course of time the Rokshi collected inside the vessel is taken out and transferred to another vessel or bottle for storage. This process is continued till the mixture retain the flavour of Rokshi.

Daily consumption in small quantity just before and after food is advisable as health tonic, but intoxicating at times when consumed in large quantity (Huidrom, 1996). A few spoons of it is given to children in cold and cough.
