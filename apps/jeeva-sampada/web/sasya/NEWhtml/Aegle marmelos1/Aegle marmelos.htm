Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

A moderate-sized, slender, aromatic tree, occurs in sub-tropical medium rainfall areas Indo-Gangetic Plains and the Peninsula (Moist Tropical) and arid areas. Deciduous, medium tree suitable for field edges and house compounds. Cultivated in many places and found wild occasionally in the Deccan. Growing wild throughout the deciduous forests of India, ascending to an altitude of c 1,200 m in the western Himalayas and also occurring in Andaman Islands.

In its wild state it occurs commonly in the sub-Himalayan tract and in the dry deciduous forests of central and southern India. Generally, it is associated with Acacia catechu, Anogeissus latifolia Wall., Bauhinia racemosa Lam., Bridelia retusa Spreng., Butea monosperma Kuntze and a host of other species. In the drier, deciduous forests of South Mewara (Rajasthan), it is found associated with Anogeissus latifolia, Cassia fistula Linn., Dlabergia latifolia Roxb., Diospyros melanoxylon Roxb. and Schrebera swietenioides Roxb.

The tree is generally found scattered; in Baharaich district (Uttar Pradesh), however it occurs gregariously in patches of varying extent. It can withstand various types of soils, climatic conditions and pH range of 5 to 10. It is drought hardy and is ordinarily found in dry localities unfavourable to majority of species. It is fairly frost resistant and can withstand as low a temperature as -70; although it grows in open forests, it is capable of standing a fair amount of shade. In its wild state, the tree occurs in regions where the absolute maximum shade temperature varies from 400 to 460, the absolute minimum temperature from 00 to 70, and annual rainfall from 570 to 2,000 mm.


Availability :

The tree is more common in the Himalayan foot-hills from Himachal Pradesh eastwards to Bihar. Fair supplies could be obtained from the forests of Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Orissa and Andhra Pradesh.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

A small tree with strong branches. Stem is stout with white greyish to pinkish furrowed, corky bark, exfoliating in irregular flakes. Branches are spiny and strong usually spreading outwardly. Leaves are many, green, alternate, trifoliate, occasionally digitately five-foliate, leaflets ovate or ovate-lanceolate, crenate, acuminate, lateral sessile, terminal long-petioled, glabrous. Petioles puberulous.

Flowers are bisexual, borne in clusters, greenish white and sweet smelling. Buds globose, pedicels pubescent. Calyx flat, pubescent, 4-lobed; lobes rounded, sometimes obscure, ciliolate. Petals 4, much imbricate, free, oblong, coriaceous. Stamens numerous, sometimes 50 or more; filaments subulate, glabrous, free or sometimes 2 or 3 of them more or less united or subfascicled; anthers linear-oblong, apiculate. Ovary 11-celled, glabrous, ovoid-oblong, faintly ribbed, seated on an elevated disk; cells many-ovuled; ovules biseriate.

Fruit is a round berry with a hard skin (3mm) greyish yellow when ripe, globose, thick stalked, pericarp is smooth but not shining, filled with soft, yellow to orange pulp which is fragrant and with pleasant flavour.

Floral and seed biology:

Seed Germination & Propagation:

Generally bael is propagated by seed, although it seldom produces a plant true to type. Natural reproduction from seeds, therefore, is not satisfactory; moreover, the seeds have short viability and are much liable to insect attack. The seeds should be obtained from fruits collected of the trees and not off the ground as seeds from the fallen fruits lose their viability. They should be washed to remove the pulp, dried for a few days and, if necessary, coated with red lead to keep off ants. Sowing is done in June or July in the nursery. The seeds are covered with earth and watered. The development of the seedlings is very slow; at the end of the first season, the stem is ordinarily 5-7 cm tall with three to five leaves, and tap root, 20-25 cm long. The seedlings are prone to die back in frosty localities but have a good power of recovery. They require at least a year in the nursery to be fit for transplanting. In view of the slow development of seedlings, they should not be transplanted until the rains of the second season; or even the third in case of the backward plants. Usually there are heavy casualties in lifting the seedlings at the time of transplanting.

Also propagated by root cuttings, layers and stem cuttings.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts Used:

Twigs are used as fuelwood.

Wood: Wood is light yellow, and strongly aromatic when first exposed, later fading to yellowish grey or greyish white. The heartwood is lacking. The wood is lustrous with a smooth feel, without characteristic taste, heavy (sp. gr. 0.89; wt. 912 kg/cu m), hard, straight-grained or occasionally curly-grained in the radial plane, even and fine-textured.

It is used for making pestles of oil and sugar-mills, for posts, shafts, axles and naves of carts.

It is priced for sacrificial and religious fires.

In Assam, it is used for carving and in Gujrat for furniture.

In Kerala, it is employed in making catamarans and fishing-boats but is susceptible to infestation by shipworms.

The timber is suitable for manufacture of railway keys, brake blocks, tool handles and helves and agricultural implements. It is also useful for making pulp for manufacture of wrapping paper.Timber is of good quality.

Other uses:

Fruits - Fruits are rich in minerals and vitamins. Pulp is mucilaginous and used for making squash, preserve, and they are also medicinally useful. Ripe fruits are eaten fresh. The pulp, diluted with water and added with requisite amount of sugar and tamarind, forms a delicious cooling drink. The tender green fruit is utilized for making a preserve which occupies an important place in the preserves industry in North India. Bael fruits are occasionally substitued by wood apple [Feronia limonia (Linn.) Swingle] and mangosteen (Garcinia mangostana Linn.) fruits.Fruit - Fruit pulp is used for drinks and in treatment of dysentry. Pulp also used as soap. Unripe fruit is a source of a yellow dye.
Fruit rind - It yields an essential oil, marmelle oil, which is a mycotoxic.

Flowers - Flowers are used for scenting water.


Bark - Bark is a source of gum.


Leaves - Leaves are used to cure diabetes.

Fodder - The young leaves and shoots are used as fodder for cattle, sheep and goats.

Medicinal - Leaves, root, bark are useful.

Gum is also received from the tree. The pulp is used for mixing cement to make it strong.

Fruits and leaves are used in worshipping. Leaves are also used as cattle fodder.

It is planted near Hindu temples for its leaves and wood, which are used for worship, and for its edible fruits which are valued in indigenous medicines. Trees provide shelter and shade.

Medium tree suitable for field edges and house compounds.

Miscellaneous Information

 
Bael tree can also be propagated by root cuttings and layers. Under natural conditions the plant produces root suckers copiously. It is possible to raise plants by gootee or inarching from a desirable tree. Trials on budding, during May-July, are successful on two-year old bael rootstock employing one-month-old budwood of a tree bearing fruits of excellent quality. This method is advantageous for turning old and uneconomic bael trees into vigorous and economic ones, bearing fruits of desirable qualities. The seedlings or budded plants are transplanted in the field at a spacing of 10-12 m. They require attention in the first year after transplanting, when they should be manured after the rains. Weeds should be removed and the plants protected from stray cattle, goats and worshippers. They should be irrigated when necessary. Very little attention is required in the subsequent years as the plants become hardy. Budded plants bearing at the age of four or five years, whereas seedling trees require seven to eight years. Hindu sacred tree.
The plants are micro propagated.
