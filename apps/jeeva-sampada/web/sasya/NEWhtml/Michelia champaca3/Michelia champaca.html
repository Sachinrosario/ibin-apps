Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Michelia champaca is a large evergreen or deciduous tree of the moist or evergreen forests, chiefly found in eastern Himalayas, north- east India, western ghats and the hilly regions. It is cultivated throughout India in gardens and near temples for its fragrant flowers and handsome foliage. The tree thrives best in a damp climate and requires deep moist soil. It is a moderate light demander and is sensitive to frost. In its natural habitat the tree is ordinarily found the regions where the obsolute maximum shade temperature varies from 950 to 1050 F, the obsolute minimum from 380 to 620 F and the normal rainfall from 90 to 200 inches or more (5, 6, 7).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Michelia champaca is a tall tree, usually upto 30 m. in height and 3.5 m. in girth, with a clean cylindrical bole upto 20 m. and ascending branches spreding from a close oblong crown. Rusty tomentose shoots with dark-grey bark. Leaves petiolate (2-4 cm long), oblong lanceolate, acute or acuminate, entire or wavy, glabrous above and more or less pubescent beneath; enclosed in convolute stipules in bud, main lateral nerves 7-14 pairs (2, 5, 6, 8, 9).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Flowers solitary, axillary enclosed in a bud by a greenish-yellow pubescent, spathaceous decidous bract. Pedicels 0.5 cm long stout, sepals and petals 15-21, deep yellow or orange, in many series, the outer oblong, acute, the inner linear. Stamens numerous, many seriate; anthers linear, adnate, introrse. Gynophore stipitate. Carpels many, persistant, 1.5 cm long dark with white specks, 2 valved, ovules 10-12, two seriate (2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9).

Seed Biology :
Fruit 5-10 cm. Long, ripe carpels ovoid or ellipsoid and woody. The seeds are dark brown, angular and are covered with pink fleshy aril. Seeds are oily and quickly loose their viability.

Seedling Germination :
Seeds are pretreated with Gibberlic acid for better germination and sown at 1.5 cm depth in nursery beds, germination commences after 38 days and completes in 70 days.

Cultivation:

Natural regeneration is usually plentiful around mother trees. The tree flowers in hot and rainy weather and seeds late in August, which is too late for direct sowing, and seeds have low viability. Artificial reproduction is, therefore, accomplished by sowing fresh seeds in the nursery and transplanting 12-15 months old seedlings. Transplanting is usually carried out in the rainy season, about July, but if the winter rains are favourable it can be can be carried out successfully in January. A spacing of 10ft. by 10ft is considered sufficient. Root and shoot cuttings may also be used; stump planting has been successful in some places. The tree coppices well. It can also be propagated by soft wood wedge grafting. In this method six- to eight-month-old seedlings were cut to a height of 25-30 cm above the ground and a slit was made in the rootstock. Defoliated scions were cut into a wedge shape and inserted into the slits and the junction wrapped with a 200-gauge polyethylene sheet. The percentage grafting success was 65-70% after 45 days (5, 6, 9, 14).

Economic Potential and Uses

The seeds contain 32.2 % of fatty oil. Oil is used for perfuming the hair, oil when rubbed on the abdomen is useful in relieving flatulence and is said to have medicinal properties.

M. champaca is cultivated for the production of Champa Oil or Champaca Oil, highly esteemed in perfumery. It is obtained from the flowers in the form of a concrete by extraction with petroleum ether. The leaves also possess aromatic properties and can be steam-distilled to yield an essential oil (5, 9, 10).

Uses

Fragrant flowers of this plant are used for sacred purposes (religious ceremonies). Seed and fruit of the tree also have various uses. The fruits are reported to be eaten. Fruit and seed are both used in a preparation for healing the cracks on the feet. uses The seeds are ingested for removing intestinal worms. Champac flowers are employed in India in the preparation of attars and perfumed hair oils. The wood is used for posts, boards, veneers, furniture, decorative fittings, carving and carriage and ship building. It is also used for bent-wood ribs, general joinery work, bobbins, drums, toys and beads. It is suitable for making pencils, particularly after impregnating the slats with paraffin wax. It is suitable for aircraft construction, battery separators and tea chest plywood. It is classed among good fuel woods (5, 9, 10, 11).

Harvesting Practices
There is no organised way of seed collection for most of the tree species. Manually fallen seeds are collected from the ground.

Produce and Products
Oil is obtained from the seeds, flowers and leaves (essential oil) which is known to have industrial as well as medicinal importance. Various medicinal preparations are derived from the tree. Wood is good and would be serviceable for planking and for furniture.

Processing Techniques

On a commercial scale oil is obtained from the seed by expression or by extraction by solvents the former being a common practice in the villages. There are three traditional methods of expressing oil from seed. (1) Boiling method (2) Chappa method and (3) Bullock Ghani method. In boiling method the depulped seed is boiled in water for some time and then dried. Thereafter it is roasted on a hot pan until the shell become crisp and friable. The whole seed is crushed into powder and it is boiled in water for some time after which the oil floating at the top water is separated by decantation. In the Chappa method the powdered kernel is steamed over a vessel of boiling water and pressed between the two limbs of chappa. In bullock ghani method the powdered kernel is sprinkled with little hot water and pressed in a Bullock-Ghani (5, 7).

Other Uses
The root and the bark are purgative and emmenagogue and are useful in treatment of abscesses, inflamation, constipation, amenorrhoea and dysmenorrhoea. The stem bark is astringent, febrifuge, diuretic, stimulant and expectorant and is useful in chronic gastritis, fever, strangury, cough, bronchitis and cardiac debility. Flowers, flower buds and fruits are bitter, astringent, acrid, refrigerant, haemostatic, digestive, camlinative, depurative, vulnerary, anthelmintic, diuretic, diaphoretic, expectorant, emmenagogue, cardiotonic, stimulant, antispasmodic, stomachic and antipyretic. They are useful in dyspepsia, nause, burning sensation, haemoptysis, pruritus, skin diseases, leprosy, wounds and ulcers, anorexia, colic, flatulence, helminthiasis, vertigo, cephalagia, ophthahnic disorders, nephropathy, headache, gout, cough, bronchitis, amenorrhoe, dysmenorrhoea, strangury, malarial fever, cardiac debility and general debility. Seeds and fruits are useful in treatment of psoriasis plantaris. Young leaves of the tree are crushed in water to make a cooling antiseptic lotion used as eyedrops. The juice of the leaves is used for treating colic (5, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13).

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

This species is grown in India for the production of essential oil and dyes, and also its leaves, flowers, seeds and fruits have medicinal, cosmetic and economic uses. Various medicinal preparations are derived from the bark of the tree. It is considered efficacious as a stimulant, antispasmodic, and febrifuge. In the form of a powder and also as a decoction the bark is useful in intermittent fevers and in mild cases of gastritis. The bark has slightly aromatic qualities and is used as an adulterant for cinnamon. It adds flavour to betel nut which is chewed for its digestive and stimulant properties. The retail market price of the fruit powder is Rs 100 per Kg and Rs 20 per Kg for bark (9, 10, 16).
