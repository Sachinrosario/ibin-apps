Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:

Kaivun is a sub-deciduous shrub or a small tree. It is distributed all over the slopes of the Western ghats of South India, in Central and Western India. The plant is widespread is over 30,000 acres of the forest area covering portions of Aryan Kavoo, Edapalayam, Ayappankovil side of nagarampara portions of Karikattoor, Alapura and Konni Reserve. It is also found as a thick undergrowth in certain areas of the Konni teak plantations.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Kaivun, a large perennial aborescent shrub has cylindrical branches. It grows generally to a height of 5-15 ft. with a stem diameter of 1-2 inches.

Leaves alternate, simple, ovate, obovate or obliquely cordate with serrated margin, pubescent or nearly glabrous beneath, 3-5 nerved, the reticulate venation prominent on the lower side.

Flowers red or orange-red, solitary in the axils of leaves or in spares fascicled clusters.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:
Flowers are one and one half to two inches long, one half to one inch across, zygomorphic. Calyx tubulous, fine toothed. Corolla parts five, equal or unequal, the two lower petals larger.

Staminal column adnate to the gynophore, 5-lobed, anthers ovate, 5-10 in groups at the top of the column between the lobes; anther-cells divergent sometimes confluent.

Ovary at the top of the column, 5-lobed, five-celled, ovules many in each cell. Styles live, more or less united, seeds very small, diamond shaped, albumen scanty, the leafy cotyledons folded round the radicle.

Floral and seed biology:

Seedling germination :

Dry seeds are collected and germinated in the nursery. A bed of rich humus soil was prepared for the reception of the seedlings. Transplanting took in the middle of May just as the monsoon started. The seedlings were planted 2-3 ft. from one another. In another place seedlings were set 20 ft. apart. From a study of the growth of the plant in the wild areas it was seen that those which were growing at smaller distances from one another produced branches from the base of the main stem and grew straight without further branching. Isolated bushes grew very large and were very profusely branched.

The seedlings grew very vigorously. As they completed one year of growth the main stem had a diameter of one half to three-fourth inch and attained 4-7 ft. in height.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
fiberS

It is used mostly for rough sacking of canvas and as cordage for sewing gunny bags and cattle harness. In Travancore it is used for container bags called Chelavu and used as pans in lever weighing system. It is mixed with jute upto 40% for manufacture of sack. Can be spun into good yarn, woven into canvas, suitable for manufacture of writing and printing paper.

MEDICINAL

Dried fruit useful in intestinal complaints. Barks prescribed for diarrhoea, dysentery and biliousness. Juice of root-in empyema and stomach affections, used in diabetes in Konkan. The root and stem barks are considered to be expectorant, demulcent, astringent and antigalactagogue (1).

Parts Used : Stem, leaves, fruit, root and stem bark, root juice, wood.

Harvesting Practices : The best type of fiber is obtained when the plants are 1 ½ years old; plants older than 2 years yield coarse and brittle fiber. Stalks can be harvested annually for fiber extraction from regenerated shoots.

Chemistry:
Chemical analysis of fiber shows; ash, 0.954; cellulose, 74.86; lignin, 23.08; fat and wax, 1.098; nitrogen, 0.291%.

Processing Techniques:
The old method of retting the fiber was by keeping the cut stems submerged under water in a stream or pond for 18-24 days.

Stems cut in 3 foot lengths, tied in bundles and submerged in water in a tank. The water was changed frequently to keep the fiber clean and white. It took 15-22 days for retting. The younger stems retted earlier than the older ones. When the retting was complete the bundles were taken out and the fiber was stripped by hand from the cylindrical stems and was clean and washed well in clear water before drying in the sun.


Other uses:

The wood is a good fuel. Local manufacturers engaged in preparing fire works consider charcoal from Kaivun stem to be superior for making gun powders.

Leaves and tender branches are used for fodder.
