HABIT & HABITAT

S. officinarum is a tall perennial grass, known only under cultivation, with thick culms of varying colors ranging from pale or dark green to dark yellow, red, violet and often striped. They have broad leaves. The inflorescence is big and showy. They are known for their high sucrose content and low non-sugars .The fiber content is low. The cane growth under favourable conditions is good with moderate tillering. They are termed 'noble', a term coined by the Dutch scientists to refer to their colourful, showy culms and the need for good treatment. The cultigen S. officinarum is essentially tropical. In India They are mainly grown in southern parts. They can not withstand the rigours of drought or occasional frost that occur in the sub-tropical climate of northern India, but are being cultivated with care for chewing purpose. Interspecific hybrids have been bred which are suited to their local environments. Most commercial sugarcane is grown between 350N and S of the equator (4).


Availability :

This species which is the type species of this genus, comprises the so called 'original' varieties, i.e. the cultivars which were in cultivation in the tropical parts of the world before the advent of hybrid varieties earlier in the century. All along the accepted idea has been that S. officinarum is endemic to New Guinea and the neighboring South Pacific islands, and has spread from this area, and that the wild cane, S. robustum, also endemic to this area, has been involved in the evolution of this species; The great amount of variation noticed in this area in the two species lends- support to this idea. Hundreds of varieties of S. officinarum in flamboyant colours are maintained in the native gardens as chewing canes. Evidence from Tamil and Buddhist literature seems to indicate that S. officinarum has been present in India from very early times and the introduction of the Mauritius canes by Sleeman in 1827 is to be considered as a reintroduction. Sugarcane is mentioned in the Tamil classics of the third Sangam period (200 AD), which would refer only to the thick canes. From a description by Abdul Fazi (1500 AD) there seems to be no doubt that paundra canes were meant for chewing and the thinner hard varieties were used for making sugar. (4,5) 


There appears to be enough evidence now to be convinced that S. officinarum was known in India and China in early centuries B.C. and the region Irian-Java-Papua-New Guinea is important in this connection. India may be considered the centre of origin of the species, with the center of diversity in the New Guinea area. Owing to the ravages of the red-rot disease, it is presumed that the species could not survive in the center of origin and migrated to the more favourable climate in New Guinea where it diversified. Thus the north-eastern region of India assumes importance from the point of origin of the genus Saccharum and its species S. officinarum; the exploration of this area would therefore yield results for future work on sugarcane origin and breeding . (4,5)

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

Sugarcane is classified botanically as a grass. It is gigantic in size. Though it has all the morphological features of a grass, it does not occur wild in nature. The main parts of the sugarcane plant are the stalk, leaf, and root system [6].

The stalk

The stalk consists of segments called joints. Each joint is made up of a node and an internode . The node is where the leaf attaches to the stalk and where the buds and root primordia are found. A leaf scar can be found at the node when the leaf drops off the plant. The length and diameter of the joints vary widely with different varieties and growing conditions. In general, however, the joints at the base are short and internodal length gradually increases.

The buds, located in the root band of the node, are embryonic shoots consisting of a miniature stalk with small leaves . The outer small leaves are in the form of scales. The outermost bud scale has the form of a hood. Normally, one bud is present on each node and they alternate between one side of the stalk to the other. Variations in size, shape and other characteristics of the bud provides a means of distinguishing between varieties. The root band also contains loosely defined rows of root primordia. Each primordium exhibit a dark center, which is a root cap, and a light colored "halo." When seed-cane is planted, each bud may form a primary shoot. From this shoot, secondary shoots called tillers may form from the underground buds on the primary shoot. In turn, additional tillers may form from the underground secondary shoot buds.

The colors of the stalk seen at the internodes depend on the cane variety and environmental conditions. For example, exposure of the internodes to the sun may result in a complete change of color. The same variety grown in different climates may exhibit different colors. All colors of the stalk derive from two basic pigments: the red color of anthocyanin and the green of chlorophyll. The ratio of the concentration of these two pigments produce colors from green to purple-red to red to almost black. Yellow stalks indicate a relative lack of these pigments.

The surface of the internode, with the exception of the growth ring, is more or less covered by wax. The amount of wax is variety dependent. Where the internode is exposed to the elements, a black mold usually develops on the waxy surface.

The top of the stalk is relatively low in sucrose and therefore is of little value to the mill early in the harvest season. Later in the harvest season, the top 1/3 of the stalk may have higher sucrose than the middle and/or bottom 1/3 of the stalk. The top 1/3 contains, however, many buds and a good supply of nutrients which makes it valuable as seed cane for planting.

A cross section of an internode shows, from the outside to the center, the following tissues: epidermis, cortex or rind, and ground tissue with embedded vascular bundles. The epidermis is a single superficial layer of cells that exhibit different patterns which are variety dependent. Generally, the patterns are formed by two cell types, the so called long cells and short cells, that alternate with one another.

The cortex or rind consists of several layers of cells just inside the epidermis. The cells of the rind are thick walled and lignified. These cells help strengthen the stalk. More toward the center, the ground tissue contains the vascular bundles with the xylem and phloem. Xylem tissue conducts water and its dissolved minerals upward from the roots, and phloem conductive tissue transports plant- manufactured nutrients and products, for the most part, downward toward the roots. The vascular bundles are much smaller and more prevalent toward the periphery of the stalk.


Two types of cracks are sometimes found on the surface of the stalk. Harmless, small corky cracks which are restricted to the epidermis, and growth cracks which may be deep and run the whole length of the internode. Growth cracks are harmful since they allow increased water loss and expose the stalk to disease organisms and insects. Growth cracks are dependent on variety and growing conditions.

The leaf

The leaf of the sugarcane plant is divided into two parts, sheath and blade, separated by a blade joint. The sheath, as its name implies, completely sheaths the stalk, extending over at least one complete internode. The leaves are usually attached alternately to the nodes, thus forming two ranks on opposite sides. The mature sugarcane plant has an average total upper leaf surface of about 0.5 square meter and the number of green leaves per stalk is around ten, depending on variety and growing conditions.

A cross section through the leaf blade would show three principal tissues: (a) epidermis, (b) mesophyll, and (c) veins or fibrovascular bundles. The epidermal cells protect the underlying tissues from injury and drying. The epidermis contains stomata for controlling the exchange of gases. The mesophyll, or middle leaf tissue, contains the cells that perform most of the photosynthesis. The fibrovascular bundles contain the xylem and phloem elements which conduct water and nutrients to and from the leaves. In addition to the above, there can be found other fibrous tissue for aiding in shaping and mechanically strengthening the leaves.


The blade joint is where two wedge shaped areas called "dewlaps" are found .The color, size, and shape of the dewlaps on a mature plant are more or less characteristic of a variety. The "top visible dewlap" leaf is a diagnostic tissue that is frequently used in nutritional studies.


The leaf sheath is similar in structure and function to the leaf blade. It is slightly simpler, however, in that it lacks some of the more specialized cells of the leaf blade. The ligule is a membranous appendage inside of the sheath which separates the sheath from the leaf blade . It is a slightly asymmetric organ whose color, size, and shape are age and variety dependent. The auricles, as their name implies, are ear shaped appendages located at the upper part of the sheath margin. Not all sheath margins have auricles. Leaf pubescence, or the covering of the various leaf parts with short hairs, is also variety and age dependent. Pubescence is not found on the leaf blade of commercial varieties, but does exist in sugarcane germplasm. Sheath pubescence can be used to identify plants.

The root system

The root system of sugarcane is typical of any grass. The function of the root system is twofold: first, it enables the intake of water and nutrients from the soil; and second, it serves to anchor the plant. Two kinds of roots will develop from a planted seed piece. The set roots, which arise from the root band, are thin and highly branched; the shoot roots, originating from the lower root bands of the shoots, are thick, fleshy and less branched. The young root is white fleshy and succulent. At its tip is the root cap arid absorbing root hairs. Continued presence of white root and their number is indicative of conducive soil conditions for plant growth. With age and senescence, the white roots turn to brown and ultimately black ones. The black one mostly serve for anchorage and the brown ones are of intermediate " stage in functioning. The varietal and cultural aspects should aim at the periodical production of fresh white roots and also to maintain a high proportion of white and brown roots through the life span of the crop.

Before shoots form, the germinating seed piece must depend entirely on the set roots for water and nutrients. The set roots, however, are only temporary and their function will eventually be taken over by the shoot roots as they develop. The life of the shoot root is also limited. Each new tiller (shoot) will develop its own roots that eventually take over the function of the original shoot roots. This rejuvenation, governed by the periodicity of tillering, is important because it allows the plant to adjust to changing environmental conditions.

A longitudinal section of a root tip consists mainly of four parts: the root cap, the growing point, the region of elongation, and the region of root hairs. The root cap protects the tender tissues of the growing point as the root pushes through the soil. The growing point consists mainly of an apical meristem, where cell division takes place. In the region of elongation, the cells increase in size and diameter until they reach their ultimate size. Epidermal cells forming outgrowths (hairs) which dramatically increase the root-absorbing surface characterize the region of root hairs.

Tiller

Like any other plant, sugarcane branches out, but with this one difference that the branches, generally, are from underground portions of the stem. These branches are called 'tillers'. The portion of stem underground may not be more than a few inches, but in that short length is compressed a large number of internodes and buds. These buds sprout and emerge out as side shoots or tillers. These tillers again repeat the process of underground branching; thus. a large number of shoots arise from a single parent bud initially planted. These several shoots or tillers constitute the daughters, granddaughters and great granddaughters and so on, of the mother stalk.


The tillering habit from underground portions of stalk is an important economic character. The pure type of S. officinarum cane varieties are poor in this tillering. Therefore. the number of tillers per clump are fewer here than in others. After harvest of plant cane, these canes do not sprout up well to raise a second or third; crop of ratoons. The wild plants, particularly S. spontaneum, have well developed underground stem regions. In the new Co. canes, this character is well exhibited and this has been achieved by hybridisation with S.spontaneum. After the spread of Co. canes in India, ratoons have become popular. Ratooning is an economic necessity for the Indian farmer as he can raise a second crop of sugarcane with less initial investment. But this improvement through Co. canes, has led to neglect of ratoon crops which latter are taken as 'free crops' and ratoons are now the cause for fall in general average yield and also for increase of pests and diseases, fall in soil fertility or exhaustion of soil.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
The inflorescence is a compound panicle and is called tassel or arrow, as it resembles the arrow of a bow. It resembles in essential features, the panicle of any other grass or cereal crops like rice and jowar. It has a long stalk on which several branches of floral stalks arise and' florets' are attached to these fine stalks, There are pedicelled and sessile florets. The details of pedicelled and sessile florets are shown in the illustration; The main stem ceases to form leaves and start floral primordia and therefore, the main stalk ceases to grow but ends in a flower. The tassel is an open panicle, with the length of main axis, and degree of lateral branching being typical of varieties and species. The branching is profuse at the base of tassel and is gradually decreased at top and the whole inflorescence assumes a conical shape and that of head of an 'arrow' .

The florets or spikelets are in pairs and each pair has a ring at the base. Like any other grass panicle, the composition of  (1)  outer and inner glume,  (2)  a sterile lemma The fourth glume is not present in S.spontaneum but occurs in S. spontaneum. Opposed to palea aare two lodicules which function as mechanism for opening the spikelet for the emergence of anther or stigma. There are three stamens. The pistil has a branched style with two feathery stigmas. The ovary has a single ovule. The fruit is a caryopsis or seed fused with fruit coat.

When the flower begins to open, the callus hairs get widened, then the glumes covering the spikelet open. The anthers are thrown out and the plumose stigmas are exposed. After a short time, the anthers become pendulous and then they dehisce; the sutures in the anthers open and pollen grains are shed through the lobes. After a certain length of time, varying with the varieties, the spikelets close and flowers come to their normal position and they open the next day at about "the same time again. The freshly opened anthers are generally bright, yellow or light coloured and are turgid. The anthers that open the previous day are darker coloured pendulous and wrinkled. The panicle begins to open from the top and from the ends of spikes. Arrows generally vary in length between 2 to 3 feet. Bottom-most contains the maximum number of spikelets while the top-most contains minimum. The number of spikelets in an arrow varies between 16 to 30 thousands.


Breeding System :

Selection: The replacement of old established cultivars by new ,higher yielding cultivars is the feature of the sugarcane industry. Selection is done to obtain the vegetative mutants for the improvement of the crop.  (4,5,7)  

Hybridization:

Interspecific hybridization: The bulk of existing sugarcane hybrids has been derived through interspecific hybridization involving two or more species of Saccharum, with the predominance of S. officinarum and S. spontaneum. The objective here is to improve and impart hardiness to the ‘aristocratic’ and ‘rich’ domesticated species of S. officinarum through crosses with wild species, viz., S.robustum and S. spontaneum. The two cultivated species S. barberi and S. sinense because of their greater resemblance to the wild species, as far as characters for hardiness and disease resistance are concerned, have also been used as parents to hybridize S. oficinarum.

Nobilization: This process is essentially one of introgressive hybridization attempting to produce an end product with no impairment to the original noble genotype but with the addition of just the favourable complement of the wild cane. Back-crossing to the noble cane in the crosses between S. officinarum and S. spontaneum was adpopted by Jeswiet, with the intention of diluting the spontaneum influence in a process of back-crossing the ‘noble’ canes which the Java breeders termed as ‘nobilization’. The term although was intended for the crosses involving S. officinarum, the term has come to be loosely applied to crosses involving commercial hybrids with high percentage of ‘noble blood’. India followed a multi-species system of breeding after initiating the S. officinarum and S. spontaneum crosses. Co 205, Co 206 and M 2 were the products of first nobilizations, but these were crosed by venkataraman with POJ 213, one of the first generation hybrids of Black cheribon x Chunnee. From such tri-specific hybridizations, be he evolved Co 281 and co 221. The latter is a parent of versatile Co 290 which in turn gave C0 419, popular in peninsular India..

Poly-cross: This aims to compare cultivars by the performance in crosses with a large number of other cultivars. It is possible by this method to screen a large number of genetic stocks in a short period for general combining ability and production of large numbers of seedlings affords great scope for selection than in the case of ‘ bi-parental crosses’. The poly cross method actually involves the planting of many varieties, replicated or at random in an isolated crossing nursery in such a way as to facilitate the pollination of each by a mixture of pollen from the other varieties. Wide-Crossing: This has been a common system of breeding and continues to be characteristic in sugarcane breeding.The main purpose is to enhance the variability in the seedlings for enabling increased scope for selection. Use of varieties of increasing complex derivation having four different species of Saccharum in the ancestor as the parents is a common practice in this regard. The present day hybrids are either spontaneum derivatives, barberi derivatives or robustum derivatives with preponderance of officinarum genes. Commercial variety, 49-5 has as progenitors all the five species of Saccharum, including two different varients of S. spontaneum .. Presently, the breeding programmes at Coimbatore include broad-based crosses and distribution of hybrid fluff of these to the receiving stations.Inbreeding: this is nothing but breeding with ‘noble’ varieties for sugarcane improvement. In this process defects of the genotype are exposed and can be eliminated. From this method direct production of commercial varieties has resulted (e.g., B 3337, B 37172, CL 41-223). The advantage of inbreeding lies in the fact that even if the deficiencies occur in an otherwise suitable offspring, they can be eliminated by using it as a parent in hybridization. Inbreds have been used with success as parents of commercial varieties and also in the development of breeding stocks.


The number of seedlings from interspecific crosses of Saccharum now amount to many millions per annum. Only few of these will achieve commercial production. Nevertheless, significant and remarkable results have been achieved in greatly increased yields of cane sugar. Commercial production of modern cultivars is very short, as they are being continuously replaced with newer cultivars with better agronomic and factory qualities.


Phenology :

Sugarcane is a 14-18 month plant crop. The ratoon crop takes 12 months. The harvesting is limited to the 5-6 months when the cane reaches maturity and has the highest sucrose content. Growth of the stool does not proceed at a uniform rate. It is slow at first, gradually, increasing until the maximum growth, the boom stage is reached, which lasts from a week to several months, after which growth slows down as the cane approaches maturity. Sugarcane has four distinct phases in its lifecycle. (8)

  1. Germination – takes about 4-6 weeks depending upon the agro-climatic situations.

  2. Vegetative phase – this involves tillering and canopy development. This phase is accompanied by increased dry matter production.

  3. Grand growth phase – the phase at the end of which the growth has attained its maximum and further growth stops.

  4. Maturation phase - this phase includes flowering and ripening. The transformation from vegetative to reproductive phase is not distinct.

Where the cane is grown over a year, the growth curve is bimodal with a reduction in growth during the season with adverse growing conditions. In addition to age growth rate is influenced by temperature, amount of sunlight, moisture, fertilizer etc. Sugarcane is a short day plant. That is it flowers under the influence of short days.


Pollinators:


Natural pollination occurs through wind and insects. The spikelets take 5-15 days to open. The anthers dehisce about three hours later when the filaments have lengthened, the anthers hang down well clear of the floret and portion of the anther wall split. The stamens normally fall soon after flowering, but the stigmas are persistent. Anthers with little or no pollen are usually yellow; those with good pollen shed are brown or purple. Sugarcane pollen under normal environmental conditions remains viable for only a few hours. Male sterility occurs in sugarcane and this character is used for cane-breeding. Male sterility is common in interspecific hybrids. Artificial pollination under controlled conditions is followed to produce hybrids. (9) 

Seed Biology :

The seeds are extremely small and weigh approximately 250 per gram or 113,500 per pound. For commercial sugarcane production, inflorescence development is of little economic importance. Generally, a day length close to 12.5 hours and night temperatures between 68 and 77 degrees F will induce floral initiation. Temperatures that are too low and/or water stress inhibit inflorescence development.


That sugarcane bears viable seed was noted when an overseer in Highland plantation in Barbados recognised seedlings of sugarcane growing in field in 1858. But systematic study and discovery was by Saltwedel in Java in 1888 and by Harrison and Bovell in Barbados in 1889. First, hybridisation by emasculation of male was done by Lewton Brain in Barbados and Mitchell in Queensland in 1904.


The discovery of true seed in sugarcane has been responsible for enabling plant breeders to evolve new varieties. The rediscovery of Mendel's laws was helpful in planning hybridisation work. Even though sugarcane is genetically so complex that no economic character follows the simple laws of inheritance, the principles of genetics and cytology have enabled plant breeders to choose the right type of parents and assess heritability of characters and influence of environments on the same. The sugarcane plant is characterised by complex hybrid origin; this complexity had been preserved over centuries by vegetative multiplication under cultivation. (7,9)  


Seedling Germination :
Commercial sugarcane is propagated by cuttings of the stalk (seed cane) containing usually two or more nodes with buds. The bud, a miniature stalk with its growing point and root and leaf primordia, forms the new shoot. In addition, a seed piece contains root primordia within its root band, which develop into set roots which function until the young shoot develop its own roots. The transition from the dormant into the active stage constitutes a complex phenomenon characterized by changes in the activity of enzymes and growth regulating substances (hormones, auxins). Maximum germination and shoot vigor will result when both internal and external factors are optimal. Fortunately, these factors can be regulated to a considerable degree by cultural methods. (7,9)

Cultivation:

Sugarcane areas in India are mainly divided into two distinct belts, (1) Subtropical and (2) Tropical. The subtropical belt comprises of the states of Up, Bihar, punjab, West Bengal, Assam, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh and Orissa. The tropical belts covers Maharashtra, gujrath, Andhra Pradesh, Madras, Mysore and Kerala. The two belts are characterised by the marked difference in climate and agricultural conditions. More than 3/4th of the area under sugarcane in the country has long been in the subtropical belt. Sugarcane grows best on the medium heavy soils, but can also be raised on the lighter soils and heavy clay, provided there is adequate irrigation available in the former type of soils and drainage is good in the latter type of soils. In northern India, it is cultivated largely on the loams and clay loams of the gangetic and other alluviums, whereas in Peninsular India, it is grown on brown or reddish loams, laterites and black cotton soils.

Sugarcane is able to grow over a prolonged season. Under warm humid conditions, it can continue its growth, unless terminated by flowering. However, its height is strongly influenced by the age of the crop and seasons. Temperatures above 50o C arrest its growth; those below 20oC slow it down markedly and severe frost proves fatal. The crop does best in the tropical regions receiving a rainfall of 750 to 1,200 mm. It can also be grown in sub-tropical areas, but where the climate is subject to extremes. Sugarcane requires a very thorough and clean preparation of land. For preparatory tillage in Peninsular India, where sugarcane soils are mostly clays and clay loams, one or two deep ploughings and one cross-ploughing with a mould board plough are necessary. These operations should be followed by clod-crushing with a discharrow, or a junior cultivator or a beam clod-crusher or even with wooden mallets and stout sticks. In the alluvial loams of northern India, cultivators usually prepare their fields after winter rains.

Usually 90,000 to 1,00,000 eye buds per ha (30,000 three- or 50,000 two-eye bud setts) are planted. Normally a well-managed crop in peninsular India has 80% germination. The depth of planting should never exceed 5 cm. Moist soil of 3-6 cm depth should cover the setts to avoid drying. If heavy rains are expected, setts are placed on ridges covered with a thin layer of moist soil. The normal practice is to keep the setts in the middle of the ridge. If dry spells are anticipated, setts can be placed at the bottom of the furrow covered with a thin layer of moist soil. (8)   

Planting period

There is a notion among Indian farmers that cane can be planted at any time of the year. This is not true. Warm and moist soil is desirable for germination of buds. Soil moisture at 50-75% field capacity at 21-38 °C atmospheric temperature is optimum for satisfactory sprouting of buds. There are three distinct planting periods. (4,5)   

1) July-August (Adsali)

2) October-November (Savasali), pre-seasonal or autumn planting

3) January-March (Eksali) Suru crop

4) February-March (spring planting) in North India

Planting in July-August and October-November gives higher yield and bette quality than the planting in January-March. This is essentially due to the higher growth period in July-August and October-November plantings.The planting season and time different states are presented below

Planting methods

There are three methods of planting viz., flat- planting, furrow- planting and trench- planting. (5,12)   

(1) Flat- planting. In the case of this method, shallow furrows are opened with a desi plough, 80 to 100 cm apart; the setts are placed in them end to end and covered with 5 to 7 cm of soil, and the field is levelled with a heavy plank(sohaga).
(2) Furrow- planting. Furrows are made with a ridger, about 10 to 15 cm deep, in northern India and about 20 cm deep in Peninsular India. Setts are laid end to end or otherwise in the furrows and covered with 5 to 7 cm ofsoil, leaving the upper portion of the furrow unfilled. Immediately after planting and covering the setts, water is let into the furrow to soak its bed thoroughly and the cane setts are then dropped into the furrow and pressed into the mud by foot. In case of monsoon planting, as a precaution against the stagnant water in the furrow damaging the buds, the setts are placed inclined on the side of the furrow, instead of flat at the bottom of the furrow.
(3) Trench planting. Trenches, 20 to 25 cm deep and rectangular or trapezoidal in section, are made either by manual labour or with a tractor-drawn rigder; the bottom of the furrow is loosened by digging. Shallow furrows are made in the bed of the trench and the setts are placed end to end or otherwise in the furrows and covered, as in the case of flat- planting. Water is then let into the trenches.

The tractor-drawn sugarcane-planter designed and fabricated at the Indian Institute of Sugarcane Research, Lucknow, is a very suitable device for planting cane in trenches. Trench-making, setts planting applying fertilizers are done simultaneously with this tractor-drawm planter.

In most parts of northern India and Bihar, and in the Malnad tract of Karnataka, cane is planted on flatland. Furrow-planting is practiced in parts of eastern Uttar Pradesh and in Peninsular India, and trench- planting is done in some coastal areas where the crop grows very tall and the strong winds are liable to throw it down and damage it badly.

planting is done dry or wet. In the case of the dry method, irrigation is not applied at planting or immediately after that, and the planted setts germinate in the comparatively dry soil. In wet planting, on the other hand, the field is irrigated just before of after planting or at both times so that setts germinate in wet soil Whether the furrows are irrigated just before or just after planting makes little or no difference; indeed, in most cases the planted field gets additional irrigations to keep it wet throughout the period of germination.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

Sugarcane is vulnerable to several pests and diseases and this problem is more serious in the subtropical regions than in the tropical areas of the country. Sugarcane is ravaged by 212 insect pests and 76 non-insect pests. Yield loss could be 20% depending upon the level of infestation. (8)

Shoot borer (Chilo infescatellus Snellen)

This is commonly known as early shoot borer and is widely distributed. Its menace is during the early phases of growth. The attack of the pest is accentuated under late planted and moisture-stress conditions. The borer larva enters the cane killing the central leaf spindle and is easily diagnosed by the 'dead hearts'. The central leaf spindle can be easily pulled out and such plants have a tendency towards increased tillering. Hence Lakshmikanthan (1984) puts the threshold level at 30%. At the threshold level and above, the yield loss is approximated at 10-15 t/ha. However, if the infestation is severe the yield loss could be much higher. The control measures include cultural, mechanical, chemical, and biological methods. Light early earthing-up prevents the entry of the larvae. 'Dead hearts' are pulled out to kill the larvae. Late planting of cane is avoided and the crop is irrigated more frequently (7-10 days interval). Lindane (20% EC) is applied to the setts (Gamma HCH emulsion at 1.0 kg ai ha-l is applied over the sett at planting). Soil application of Sevidol at 12.5 kg ha-l around the base of the clump on the 30th and the 60th day after planting is advocated. This chemical can also be applied in whorls. Biological methods include spray application of the granulosis virus at 106-107 inclusion bodies per ml along with a surfactant like teepol or sandovit at 0.05% at the beginning of the pest infestation. If desired, the application may be repeated at fortnightly intervals.

Top borer (Scirpophaga excerptalis walker)

It attacks at all stages of growth and is of economic importance in subtropical India. The characteristic symptom is the 'bunchy top' due to the sprouting of the top buds. The larvae bores through the central core and 'dead hearts' are found which cannot be pulled out easily. Yield and quality loss could be to the extent of 20-30%. The control measures include collection and destruction of egg masses, and roguing of the affected cane tops. Autumn planting is to be advocated. Waterlogging should be avoided. Application of carbofuran 1.0 kg ai/ha or phorate 3 kg ai/ha during June-July for autumn planted cane controls the top borer. Varieties like Co J 67, Co 1007, Co 1158, and CoS 767 may be employed in the endemic areas. Biological control method includes release of 7richogramma japonicum in the subtropics and Isotima javensis in the tropics.

Internode borer (Chilo sacchariphagus indicus, Kapur)

This is widespread both in the tropics and the subtropics, and infects when the Ii internodes are formed. Lodged cane, water shoots, and canes in waterlogged conditions are severely affected. It normally attacks the top immature internodes. The larva tunnels upwards in a characteristic spiral fashion. Yield is reduced following the reduction in length and girth of the cane which are important yield components. The buds may not germinate with a consequent loss in yield and quality. J Subsequent to borer injury, secondary infections of wilt and pineapple disease may occur. The control measures include use of borer-free setts, detrashing at 5th, 7th, and 9th month of the crop stage, removal of water shoots, and avoiding heavy dose of N Spraying 0.1 % monocrotophos reduces the initial borer population at the grand growth period (4-6 months). Biological control with Trichogramma chilonis released inundatively at 3.5 cc/ ha at fortnightly intervals from the 4th month to harvest provides good protection against the internode borer.

Stalk borer (Chilo auricilius, Dudgeon)

This is a major pest in Uttar Pradesh, Haryana, Punjab, Bihar, and Orissa. At first, the larvae feed on the inner surface of sheaths, subsequently the sheath rots and the leaf dries out. The feeding of the leaf causes longitudinal orange-yellow streaks extending from the tip to the base. At a much later stage, the larvae bore into the internodes and the infected canes produce dead hearts, which is similar to what the early shoot borer does. The infected cane may dry up, and the damaged internodes may show reddening and emit rancid smell. Prolonged drought period, high N dose, frequent irrigation and waterlogged conditions favour the attack of the stalk borer. The cultural methods to control the stalk borer are providing drainage and removal of water shoots. Control of Johnson grass (collateral host) will reduce the pest attack. Detrashing, destruction of water shoots, trash burning, etc. keep the

stalk borer under check. Application of monocrotophos granules at 3 kg ai/ ha in July is effective in controlling the pest. Under severe infestation, the yield loss is to the extent of 16-33%, and the sugar loss by 2 units.

Gurudaspur borer (Acigona steniellus, Hampson)

This is the most serious pest of the subtropics. Its incidence was observed on an epic scale in Gurudaspur, Punjab-hence the name. Upon the entry into the internode, the larva feeds below the rind tissue in a spiral manner. It makes minute punctures on the rind from within. Externally the tunnel appears as a dark spiral ring made up of a series of punctures looking like beads in a rosary. When the larvae feed below the rind, the leaves wither; later on the entire whorl dries up. Cane growth is restricted and breaks if slightly pulled. Side shooting occurs due to bud sprouting. Yield loss could be 10-20% but depends on the infestation. The control measures include use of borer-free setts, destruction of infested cane, burning of trash and stubbles, and avoiding ratooning of the highly infested crop. Soaking the setts in 0.2% trichlorophon for 2 hours before planting kills larvae present within the setts. Resistant/tolerant varieties like Co 1148, Co] 46, Co] 64, Co 62175 should be used. Varieties with light sheath are less attacked. Inundative release of egg parasite Trichogramma chilonis at 1,25,000 parasites /ha checks the attack of this pest.

Root borer (Emmalocera depressella, Swinhoe)

This is the only borer which attacks the underground portion. The root borer is widespread in the tropical belt, Northern Gujarat, Maharashtra, Karnataka, and Andhra Pradesh. It attacks only the young shoots and causes 'dead hearts.' It rarely attacks well-grown sugarcane. External symptoms are rarely seen except yellowing of the leaves. Only on digging and on root exposure, the infestation can be seen. The dead hearts cannot be easily pulled out like in the case of early shoot borer. The loss in yield is about 10-15%. The infestation of root borer is high when the ambient temperatures are high with moderate humidity. The attack is severe under unirrigated conditions and in light textured soils (sandy to sandy loam). Ratoons are more prone to attack by root borer than the plant crop. The mechanical methods of control include deep stubble shaving of ratoons, removal and destruction of affected clumps, collection of moths by using light traps, and digging and destruction of stubbles after the harvest. The chemical method of control consists of application of chloropyriphos or Ekalux 5 G at 1-2 kg ai/ ha during May and August. But control is not that effective. Pink borer and green borer are minor pests.


White Grubs (Anomala sp., Holotrichia sp., Pentodon sp., Alissonotum sp., and Hetronychus sp.)

These grubs are 'C' shaped and fleshy with a white or grey body. The visible symptoms of attack include drying of leaves, drooping of inner spindles, and finally wilting of the plant. The loss in cane yield ranges from 10-12 t/ ha or even more if the infestation is high. The clump is easily pulled out since the roots are damaged. Integrated pest management is the only solution to control the pest. outing the first summer showers (tropical India), the beetles emerge in large numbers to mate and these can be collected and killed. Collection of large number of beetles (Holotrichia serrata, Fabticus), and up to 300, 000 in one day has been achieved in the Belgaum district of Karnataka. Fluorescent light traps can be used to attract the pest. An infested field may be ploughed and the grubs, pupae, and beetles picked by hand and destroyed. Wherever feasible, puddling the soil, and paddy if grown in rotation for 2-3 seasons is a sure method of control. Varieties like Co 953 and Bo 3 ate resistant to white grubs. Chemical methods of control include use of HCH dust (10%) at 10 kg ai/ ha or as an emulsion at 2.0 kg ai/ ha. Its efficiency can be further enhanced by the use of farmyard manure which attracts the young grubs. Quinalphos G at 2 to 2.5 kg ai/ ha reduces the grub population, increases the millable cane population and yield. Successful biological control has been achieved in Hawaii and Mauritius (Blackburn, 1984). The grubs are parasitised by Campsomeris marginella modesta (Smith) and Tiphia parallela (Smith). In Mauritius, scoliid wasps introduced in the past for control of white grub phyllophagt;l Smithi are still active. But gregarines have been found to infect a high percentage of larvae (Rajabalee et al., 1995).

Termites

Large termite mounds are seen in the sugarcane grown in Central and Eastern Africa. The yield loss is quite variable and may extend up to 60%. Important species of termites are: Coptotermes heimi (Wasmann), Odontotermes assmuthi (Holmgr), O. obesus (Rambur), and o. wallonensis (Wasmann). Termites feed on soft tissue and attack setts, stalks, and stubbles. The tunnel excavated by termites is filled with soil with the rind intact. There is severe germination loss and the affected cane dies. The attack of termites is more severe under drought conditions and in light textured soils (sandy to sandy loam). Termite attack can be controlled by soil application of HCS 5% dust at 1 kg ai/ ha or Gamma HCH (EC) at 1.0 kg ai/ha. As a post planting operation, either aldrin (EC) or gamma HCH (EC) drenching at 1.0 kg ai/ ha is advocated. Equally effective is chlorpyriphos at 1.0 kg ai/ ha. Digging out termite mounds to remove the queen and spreading, Vetox 85 would kill and destroy the pests.

Scale insect: Melanapsis glomerata (Green)

The white scale insect is a sucking pest. There are 35 species of scale attacking sugarcane but Melanapsis glomerata is of economic importance. It is prevalent on a wide scale in Andhra Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Haryana, and Punjab. With the formation of the internodes, scales appear on the cane. Heavy infestation of the scale insect starts from June and continues up to December. Scales are underneath the sheath, and are hence difficult to control. When the infestation is heavy, the entire stem is covered by scale insects and its encrustation gives a grayish black appearance. Canes are stunted and eventually die. The tips of the leaves show the signs of drying, and the leaves turn yellow. The infested cane becomes shrivelled with stunted growth and has a reduced internodal length, resulting in lowered yield and quality. Highly infested canes pose problems in milling. The problem of scale insect is more serious in lighter soils than in heavy soils. Ratoons are more prone to scale insect attack than plant crop. High temperature, humidity, and drought conditions favour the scale insect attack. It spreads through setts, ratoon stubbles, and trash. Field sanitation is of utmost importance. Ratooning of the infested fields should be avoided. Insect attacked seed material should never be used. Under severe infestation, trash should be burnt. The setts can be immersed in the insecticidal solution of malathion (0.1 %) or Dimethoate (0.08 to 0.15%), or phosphomidon (0.05 to 0.08%). When the crop is 5-6 month old and scale insect infestation is high, monocrotophos at 1.5 kg ai/ ha can be sprayed after detrashing. Soil application of carbofuran 2 kg ai/ha or Dimethoate 3 kg ai/ ha or Aldicarb at 2-3 kg ai/ ha or monocrotophos at 3.0 kg ai/ha is advocated. If the attack is severe, soil application can be done at 15-30 day intervals.

Pyrilla (Pyrilla purpusilla, Walker)

Pyrilla is a very destructive sucking pest. The adults and nymphs suck the sap from the undersurface of the lower leaves causing yellowish white spots to appear. High levels of manuring, irrigation, waterlogged conditions, and lodging enhance pyrilla build up. The hoppers exude a sweet fluid (honey dew) on which fungus grows (Capnodium sp.). As a result, the leaves are completely covered by a sooty mould. Due to sucking by a large number of sugarcane leaf hoppers, photosyn- thesis is affected and the top leaves dry up and the lateral buds germinate. The loss in yield due to pyrilla epidemics is approximately 28% and the loss in sugar is about 1.6 units. Heavy rainfall, high humidity (70-80%), and high temperature ((26-30 DC) favour pyrilla build up. Under heavy infestation, the trash which harbours the hopper is burnt. Detrashing reduces the pyrilla population. During the pre-monsoon period dusting with HCH (5-10%) at 20-30 kg/ha, or methyl parathion (2%) at 12.5 kg/ ha is recommended. Other chemicals used as foliar sprays are chloropyriphos. (0.3 kg ai/ha) and malathion (12.5 ai/ha).

White flies

There are three species of white flies namely, Aleurolobus barodensis Mask, Neomaskellia bergii Sign and N. andropogonis Corbett. Out of these, Aleurolobus barodensis is of economic importance and severely infects cane in Bihar, Gujarat, Haryana, Punjab, Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh. The nymphs of white flies suck the sap from the undersurface of the leaves which turn yellow, and pinkish in severe cases, and gradually dry up. The infested leaves are covered by sooty moulds caused by fungus Capnodium sp. which adversely affect photosynthesis. The white fly infestation retards cane growth and reduces the sugar content. To contain the spread of this pest, ratooning is discouraged, adequate phosphorus and potassium should be used, adequate drainage should be provided and the affected leaves should be clipped. Chemical control consists of a spray of monocrotophos (40 EC) or endosulphan (35 EC) at 0.2% after stripping the leaves bearing puparia.

Diseases:

Sugarcane is ravaged by nearly 130 diseases but only 30 are of economic importance. Among them red rot, smut, and wilt are of serious concern. A conservative estimate shows that th loss in yield ranges from `0-15% in endemic conditions. The red rot and wilt in severe form can cause total yield loss [8].


Different diseases of sugarcane.

Red-rot: (Colletotrichum falcatum,).

In perfect stage, the fungus is named as Glomerella tucumanensis. This disease occurs allover India in all states in. The disease expresses itself midrib of leaf and in the inside of the stalk. The midrib infection, though common, does not assume great economic importance. As the disease is located in the internal tissues of the stalk, it is not easily discernible in early stages. As the disease spreads to some extent in the stalk, the leaf margins first and ultimately the entire crown droops and wilts. By stages, all shoots of a clump are affected. In epiphytotic form, the whole field is destroyed. Since the organism grows and destroys the tissues, that usually serve for storage of sugar, and as sugar itself is broken down, in a seriously affected crop, there is no produce left for harvest and processing. When a diseased stalk is split open, a sour smell emanates and the tissues are reddened along the internode. Mid rib of cane leaves exhibit red rot infection. Streaks of red colour appear in mid ribs and extend in continuous or discontinuous streaks.

Control measures include growing resistant varieties like Co. 419, Co. 449 and Co. 521, good irrigation and drainage, avoiding ratoons, using disease free seedlings and keeping the crop free from insect injuries -borers, grubs and also rats and jackals is important.

Smut (Ustilago scitamineae)

This disease is also, called ‘carbon disease’. This disease occurs allover India. When the infection is complete in sprouting phase, particularly in ratoons, the new sprout are lean and lanky, profuse in number and the growing point projects out as a long black whip covered with black spores resembling soot. In older canes, the terminal growing point emerges as a whip as described above. Before the black spores burst out from the whip, they are covered by a thin white membrane. Each whip contains millions of spores, enough to infect the whole zone.

The control measures include growing resistant varieties like Co. 449, Co. 527, Removing smutted clumps or diseased clumps are to be wholly covered by fine muslin bag and up rooted and burnt away from the field. Using Healthy Seed and avoiding ratoons can be helpful.

Wilt (Cephalosporium sacchari)

This disease occurs in varying degrees in all states. Under certain adverse conditions it assumes serious proportions and losses are great. By itself this fungus appears to be weak and quite often it occurs as a disease complex with red rot. The disease does not visibly express itself in the young crops upto 5 or 6 months age. In early stages, the fungus grows along in the tissues and nodal infections are evident. With aging of the stalk, and change in climate from hot dry summer weather to cool moist monsoon, the symptoms of the disease develop fast. While red-rot express itself seriously under water logged heavy soils, wilt seems to be more dangerous under drought conditions. The symptoms are similar to those of red-rot with minor differences. When diseased canes are split open, the pith is reddened. Leaf margins and later the crowns wilt as if under drought. The stalks also dry and shrink. The reddening of pith tissue is more or less uniform and streaky. Transverse white patches which are the symptoms of red-rot are not seen. Canes affected by red-rot emit characteristic alcoholic smell while those affected by wilt emit earthy dirty smell. The shrinking of pith tissues is more rapid under wilt.

Control measures are more or less the same as for red-rot. Rotation of crops, selection of disease free seed and pre-treatment with hot air or hot water may be emphasized. Sett treatment with 0.1% Carbendazim has been recommended to avoid further spread of the disease.

Pineapple disease or sett rot (Ceratocystis paradoxa).

This is noticed in the setts planted in soil and rarely in the standing crop. This is a common disease in all states of India. It affects germination percentage of planted setts, which start rotting before the buds swell and sprout. The malady is greater under ill drained and heavy soil conditions than under well-drained conditions. The planted setts are invaded by the fungus, generally through cut ends. In early stages, the tissues of the setts are transluscent and later turn black. The vascular strands stand out as loose threads. The odor resembles that of pineapple and hence the name of the disease.

The disease can be largely prevented through ploughing and opening up the soil allowing good aeration and soil aggregate formation. Excess watering in early stages at sprouting phase is to be avoided. The most important step is to prevent the entry of the fungus into the planted setts through cut ends. For this purpose, the cut ends are dipped in fungicides of copper or mercury. There are many formulations in the market under trade names such as agallol, are tan perenox and bordeaux.

 

Rust (Puccinia kuenhii,).

This disease is not of general occurrence. It came to prominence when it appeared devastating on Co. 475 in Maharashtra and was the cause for withdrawal of this variety from general cultivation. Rust was noted Co. 876 in Madras State, on Co. 475 in Maharashtra, on Co. 421, Co. 467, Co. 475, Co. 603, Co. 658, Co. 678, Co. 732 and POJ 2878 in Telangana (Andhra), on Co. 992, Co. 993, Co. 986, Co. 987 and Co. 1019 in Andhra, on Co. 313 and Co. S. 510 in UP. Characteristic rust pustules or orange colour appear and later turn brownish, The spots appear on both sides of the leaf blade, They appear slightly raised on the lower side of the lamina due to rupture of sori and release of spores. The Co, Canes are generally resistant to rust, Variety Co 475 was given up in Maharashtra due to this disease, Chemicals may be sprayed to prevent the incidence of rust, Experiments at Lucknow institute indicated that thiram and thiocarbamates are the best, Ferbamnickel sulphate mixture spray, 5-6 times between November to March are recommende.

Leaf Spots (Helminthosporium sacchari)

Among the leaf spots, eye spot is more serious. The disease is also called as Helminthosporiose. Cooler nights, high humidity, low soil pH, low soil potash content, and high nitrogen levels favour incidence of the disease. Cloudy weather accentuates the disease but the incidence reduces with rise in temperature. Typical symptoms are round or oval elongated spots on the leaves, these spots will have reddish center and brown border. After a week, reddish brown streaks or ‘runners’ develop towards the leaf tip along the veins. The badly affected foliage looks reddish-brown when viewed from far. The severity of the disease is due to the toxin helminthosporioside, which reduces ferrous content of the leaf and impairs chlorophyll synthesis. 2-3 sprays of 0.2% copper-oxychloride fungicide, at 15-20 days interval just before the winter season controls the disease. Resistant varieties like Co 62175 is more tolerant than Co 419. Other leaf spots like Yellow spots caused by Cercospora koepkei are not serious and can be easily controlled by spray of 0.2% copper oxychloride.

Mosaic

This disease is caused by virus. They multiply in the host plant sap. The infection is systemic or by vectors usually the aphids in this case. This disease is noticed in all sugarcane countries, but in India it is not serious enough to cause huge losses -rather the losses are generally insignificant. When the green leaf is held against bright light, small elongated specks of pale yellow non-chlorophyll regions are seen. The intensity of elongated chlorotic specks varies with intensity of disease and varietal resistance. The intensity of specks also varies with the strain of the virus. In severe cases, the crop may appear a shade paler than normal dark green healthy crop.

Control measures include, rouging of diseased clumps, using disease free seed material in planting, Insecticides are to be sprayed to control the vectors (aphids), Pre-treatment of seed material with heat Cum chemicals, jowar, maize and some grasses that serve as alternate hosts should not be grown adjacent to or mixed with sugarcane and growing resistant varieties like Co, 419.

Grassy Shoot Disease (GSD)

This disease is reported to spread through vector -Longiunguis sacchari -the sugarcane aphid and also through harvesting implements.This disease is also called "bunchy" disease or 'leafy tuft,' The causal organism was first thought to be virus and now as mycoplasma. The disease has spread from Maharashtra to Telengana and then to the coastal districts of Andhra. This disease has appeared and assumed importance only in the last fifteen years from about 1955. As the name indicates, the clump of sugarcane -takes the appearance of bunchy grass. Normal stalks are not formed. A large number of lean and lanky pale to albino sprouts appear in the clump. If the disease makes its appearance late after stalk formation in the clump, the clump in stunted and grassy shoots appear at the base. The profuse proliferation into thin lanky pale shoots at ground level base of clump is characteristic of this disease. The disease appears manifold times in the ratoon crop. There are instances where 60-80 percent clumps of first ratoon end in grassy shoots with poor or no formation of millable stalks.

The control measures meant were under mosaic viz., roguing diseased clumps, planting disease free and pre- treated seed, controlling insects and avoiding alternate hosts in the neighbourhood apply to this disease. Avoiding ratooning is essential as the disease appears manifold times in ratoons. Growing resistant varieties will also help.

Ratoon Stunting Disease (R.S.D.) ( Clavibacter xyli)

The term 'ratoon' is misnomer as the disease occurs in plant crops as well. Generally, the crop is stunted in growth, the internodal length is reduced; tillering may be less; girth is not usually affected. Yield is considerably reduced in susceptible varieties. If the cane stalk is split open, particularly near top meristem, pinkish colour may appear in the centre. Usually, even in healthy canes, this colour may be present near rind, but not in centre. At lower nodes, the vascular bundles record orange-red discolouration. There are no visible diagnostic symptoms, but yield is much reduced at harvest. This happens by stunted growth of the stalk, and the reduction in population per clump. There are no detailed surveys in India to specify the extent of disease occurrence in different states, nor on susceptibility of varieties under cultivation.

Control measures include hot air treatment where the seed pieces are kept in hot air plant at a temperature of 540 C. After heat treatment, the seed pieces are to be cut by dipping in mercurial fungicide. Hot water treatment where the seed cane has to be dipped in hot water tanks. In the case of hot water treatment, waste steam from the mill can be utilized, reducing total costs. Fungicides and insecticides can be added to the water, to deal with diseases not cured by heat therapy. (5)  

Nematodes

There are about 50 species of nematodes associated with sugarcane [8]. There are ecto and endoparasitic nematodes. The lesion nematodes, Pratylenchous sp. are wide spread causing serious damage to sugarcane crop. The most important species is P. zea. Infestation is more serious in lighter soils. General chlorosis, stunting and wilting are the above ground symptoms while root galls, root lesions, stubby roots and necrotic root surfaces are the below ground symptoms.

The loss in yield and quality due to nematode infection depends upon the population and the variety. An average loss in cane yield is guesstimated at 16.5%. Threshold level of P. zea is estimated as 2 nematodes per gram of root.

Control:Fumigation of Chloropicrin telone and Methyl bromide,soil dressing with Furadon, Mocap and foliar application of oxamyl, Nemacin, Nemagon etc.

Uses

In India 80-90 per cent of the production of sugarcane is utilized for the manufacture of three products, widely used in foods, and beverages, for sweetening purposes, viz. gur or jaggery, vacuum pan sugar (also called plantation or direct-consumption white sugar) and open pan sugar or Khandsari. The rest of the production is used partly for chewing (i.e., as a fresh fruit) and the remaining quantity for planting purposes as seedcane. The largest quantity ranging annually between 50 and 60 per cent of the total cane production is used for production of gur while manufacture of white sugar accounts for some 25-30 per cent of the cane, chewing and planting 10 per cent and khandsari 5 per cent. The relative quantity used under each head is influenced by the open market price for sugar and gur in addition to other demand and supply factors. (2,3,10,13)   

Sucrose, the main constituent of cane juice and its products, besides being used widely for sweetening in foods, beverages and confectionery, can be utilized in the preparation of over 10,000 chemicals and chemical intermediates.


Harvesting Practices

Harvesting

The harvest includes cutting, cleaning, loading and transport and contitutes nearly 30-40% of the production cost[8]. Normally manual harvesting is done and contract labour is procured from nearby places Appropriate tools are necessary for efficient harvesting. Curve sickle is an effective implement to harvest cane and it can clean the cane by removing trash, adhering roots, etc. A hand axe shaped like 'Parasuram axe' is equally effective. The Indian Institute of Sugarcane Research (IISR) has developed a hand stripper, but it is not commonly used. But the stripper is very useful when certain cane varieties have spines. For better yield of cane and sugar, it is essential to harvest the cane at ground level Some varieties like Co 62175 have 5-6 immature top internodes, which contain more moisture and less of recoverable sugar. The tops should be removed and cleared from trash and other extraneous matter. The binding material and trash can reduce juice extraction with a drop in sugar by 0.3 to 0.5 units. However, the permissible trash and other extraneous matter under mechanised harvesting is 7% and manual harvesting is 2 to 3%. An efficient transport system is essential and trucks are normally hired by the factories. Large trucks ,tractors and bullock carts can be used. (4,5,8,16)

Produce and Products

Sugar, jaggery and Khandsari are the three main products of sugarcane. Apart from these there are other allied products like liquid jaggery, rab, bura,misri and shakkar. (4,5,7,8,12) 

Sugar: Is the white crystalline product obtained from sugarcane juice, by manufacturing vacuum pans.

Jaggery: A solid or semi-solid product obtained on concentrating sugarcane juice without prior purification. Are manufactured in open shallow iron vessels by continuous stirring.

Khandsari: brown sugar obtained from sugarcane juice by the open pan process. It is mostly in coarse powder and is chiefly used in making sweetmeats.

Liquid jaggery: An intermediate product obtained during jaggery making. This contain water, sugar and non-sugars. Fructose and glucose are in equal proportions, with proteins, organic acids and minerals.

Rab: Semi-liquid form of jaggery obtained by concentrating juice to lower brix. It is stored in earthen pitchers. On storage crystals will develop. These crystals are used in rural areas as sweeteners.

Bura: It is produced by recrystallization of any kind of sugar and khandsari and made into a fine, free flowing product. It is used as a sweetening agent.

Misri: it is conglomeration of sugar crystals of irregular size and shape obtained by recrystallization of sugar. It is chiefly used as a ‘prasada’.

Shakkar: this is a brownish coloured powdered gur obtained after concentrating sugarcane juice to higher brix. It is a sweetening agent and is easy to dry with easy packaging, handling and transport.


By-products

The major byproducts obtained during the manufacture of gur and sugar from sugarcane are molasses, bagasse or fibre, and pressmud (or filter-cake, filter-mud). In addition to these, cane trash and cane tops form together approximately 25 per cent of the weight of the cane and are used as a feed. The molasses obtained in sugar factories is mainly used by the distilleries for the manufacture of alcohol and potable spirits; the rest is used as manure and cattle feed and in curing of tobacco

Bagasse, the fibrous residue; when sugarcane is crushed. for extraction of juice, is almost entirely used as fuel in ; the manufacture of sugar and gur. Bagasse is also used in the manufacture of paper in place of bamboo and sabai grass normally used for the purpose .

Press-mud is produced as a byproduct to the extent of 3 per cent of the weight of cane in sulphitation factories and 7 per cent. in carbonation factories. The sulphitation press-mud is almost entirely used as manure while the other is used to fill up pits and sometimes in the production of lime for building purposes.

Sugarcane wax, obtained from the sulphitation pressmud, can be used as a substitute for imported carnauba wax which is used in the manufacture of carbon paper, shoe and other polishes, wax paper and such other products.

It is formed in the plant through photosynthetic activity. Glucose and fructose are known to as invert sugars in the sugarcane juice.(11)

Sucrose or the sugarcane sugar in over-ripe cane decomposes into glucose and levulose. This decomposition is due to inversion.‘Glucose’ is a normal constituent of both ripe and unripe cane. It occurs along with levulose in over-ripe canes, and is therefore found in all cane juice to a greater or lesser extent. In the sap of perfectly ripe sugar cane little or no ‘fructose’ seems to occur, but it may be found in unripe or over-ripe canes. In unripe cane it is principally present in all parts still growing, from which it disappears as the cane ripens. In dead canes, where the sucrose has already undergone partial inversion, just as in the juices circulating in the factory, in which also fructose arises by inversion to some extent.


The millable cane contains 50-60% of the total dry matter of the whole plant, the tops and trash 30-40%, and the roots and stubble about 10%. The chemical composition is affected by the cultivar, age portion of the plant, environment and cultural treatment. The leafy tops have a high percentage of reducing sugars and a low percentage of recoverable sucrose, except when ripened under ideal conditions. The amount of sucrose in the stem increases upto maturity and is usually highest near the base, but the changes occur with degree of ripeness.  Processing Techniques

Manufacturing of white sugar

At least two types of sugar are made in most modern factories: that for export, known as ‘raw sugar’, and that for local consumption known as ‘white sugar’ or ‘plantation white sugar’. The processes used in their manufacture differ only in the later stages. (8,13,16,17) 

Following are the main unit operations involved in manufacturing sugar from sugarcane (8):

1. Extracting of juice from sugarcane

2. Clarification of cane juice

3. Evaporation of water

4. Crystallization

5 Centrifuging

6 Sugar drying, grading and packing and storing

Extraction of juice from sugarcane

The sugar (sucrose) in cane is in the dissolved state and hence in the form of juice. Heavy duty preparatory devices, consisting of a number of rotating knives with hardened edges are used to prepare the cane. Generally two or three such sets of knives are used. These knives not only cut and disintegrate the cane into smaller and thinner pieces, but also rupture and open the cells holding the juices to facilitate easy and better extraction of juice.

Milling

The cane prepared as above is passed through a series of mills for extraction of juice. The prepared cane is passed through the rollers. A heavy hydraulic load is applied on the top roller and the prepared cane is pressed while passing through twice between the top and the two bottom rollers. The juice extracted flows down the two bottom rollers through the grooves and is collected in the gutter placed below the mills.

Clarification process

The objective of clarification is qualitative rather than quantitative. The clarification should result in a juice which is neutral (pH 7.0), clear and transparent, with a light colour. During the process, all the suspended impurities are eliminated, most of the colouring matter is removed, some of the non-sugar solids are precipitated out and removed by settling, while the sugar (sucrose) and the reducing sugars (glucose and fructose) are kept intact. Hence the functions are many. A number of physical and chemical reactions are involved in the clarification process. The process followed depends upon the quality of the final product. Generally two types of sugar are manufactured in a sugar factory: (1) raw sugar (2) white sugar (direct consumption white sugar).Clarification process for manufacturing raw sugar

Defecation process

This process is used especially for the production of raw sugar. It is very simple; the juice is heated to about 70 0C, and calcium hydroxide in the form of milk of lime is added to the heated juice to raise the pH of the juice from about 5.5 to 7.6-7.8. After a few minutes retention period, the treated juice is again heated to 1000C+ and pumped into a continuous clarifier. A retention time of about 2.5 to 3 hours is given in the clarifier during which time the precipitated flocks settle down. The clear transparent juice is decanted for further processing. The sediment from the bottom is pumped through a rotary vacuum filter. The filtrates are recirculated back into the process and the filtered mud is thrown out. The filter mud is used as organic manure in the fields. The filtrates are generally recirculated by mixing them either with mixed juice or treated juice. The clear juice goes to evaporators for concentration.


During heating and addition of calcium hydroxide, and again heating, a number of calcium salts are precipitated, the predominant salt being calcium phosphate. If the cane juice is deficit in phosphate content, that is made up by additions of phosphate, either in the form of phosphoric acid or super phosphate solution. Phosphate in the mixed juice ensures formation of calcium phosphate flocs, which absorb and adsorb colloidal particles and small precipitates, resulting in better and faster sedimentation and clearer juice. Certain polymeric compounds are used in small doses as settling aids being added to the juice just before it enters the clarifier. These chemicals aid quicker settling and also help in sedimentation of the colloidal particles.

Clarification process for manufacturing white/plantation white sugar

White sugar demands a better degree of clarification. More impurities, particularly colouring matter, need to be eliminated in the clarification process. Besides, the juice needs to be treated with some bleaching agents which remove colour partially, making the resultant clarified juice light coloured and transparent.

The agents used for clarification are heat, milk of lime (calcium hydroxide), sulphur dioxide gas, phosphoric acid, etc. Use of carbon dioxide gas is also in vogue in some old sugar mills as bleaching agent in place of or in addition to sulphur dioxide gas. However most, factories which are using carbon dioxide are slowly abandoning the process and changing over to sulphur dioxide. Hence the use of carbon dioxide is not dealt with here.


Double sulphitation process

This is the standard process followed in all most all sugar factories manufacturing direct consumption white sugar. the juice is guided to the bottom of the tank through a fairly large diameter pipe during which the milk of lime is added. At the bottom of the tank there is a network of pipes through which SO2 gas is bubbled. Thus the juice is first limed and immediately after, it is sulphited. Increase the pH first to about 8.0 and then sulphite to about 7.2 pH, leaving a time gap of a few seconds between liming and sulphitation.

The treated juice after treatment with milk of lime and sulphur dioxide gas, is heated again up to 100 °C+, and sent to specially designed clarifiers where a retention period of about 2.5 h is allowed. The precipitates settle down and the sediment is collected at the bottom of each tray of the multi-tray clarifier.

Evaporation of water

The clear decanted juice from the clarifier which is generally called as clarified water is to be evaporated before the sugar is crystallized out. The evaporation is done in two stages. In the first stage, the evaporation is carried out until the solid content is about 60 to 65%. In the second stage, further evaporation is carried out in a controlled manner when crystallization takes place.

Crystallization in vacuum pans (pan boiling)

The evaporation of water is done in two stages, the first being in multiple effect evaporators. The second stage of evaporation involves crystallization of sugar in terms of crystal formation and crystal growth. Until syrup sulphitation (from the milling) the process is continuous. Once the syrup comes to the pan floor it is stored in storage tanks and further processing becomes batchwise. Even with con- tinuous pans, the pan boiling process is not totally continuous as certain operations like 'graining' and 'footing' are done batch by batch. The evaporation rate varies from stage to stage.

Centrifuging

The massecuite after cooling is centrifuged in centrifugals to separate the mother liquor (molasses) from the crystals. There are two types of centrifugals used for the purpose. ‘Batch type’ centrifugals are used to cure A-massecuite and ‘continuous centrifugals’ are used for curing B-& C-massecuites. Often part of the B- massecuite is cured in batch type centrifugals to use the cured sugar as the seed for footing for A-massecuite. The sugar coming out of the continuous centrifugal is generally melted and the melt liquor is used for boiling in A-massecuite. The C- massecuite is very thick and viscous. Hence centrifuging this massecuite offers resistance. To reduce the viscosity and ease curing, the massecuite is reheated immediately before it enters the centrifugal. In addition, a little water or dilute final molasses (the latter is preferred) is used at the entry of the massecuite to the centrifugal.


A-curing in the earlier days was done in two stages; today it is common to cure in a single stage, washing with only superheated water instead of steam and water. The modern high speed and high gravity factor centrifugals perform very efficiently making double curing redundant. Single curing is giving excellent results in terms of sugar quality. Double curing results in enormous re-circulation of sugar beside increasing the power consumption, initial investment, maintenance cost, etc.In the modern centrifugals, the drives with stepped speed multi-pole motor have been replaced by thyristor controlled AC drives with step-less speed variations. These centrifugals with flat bottoms are fully automatic and can recycle as high as 16 cycles/hour. These machines are not only energy efficient, but also highly productive as there is no time loss during the change of speeds, both up- wards and downwards.


Sugar drying, grading, packing and storing

Sugar drying

In most places in case of white sugar manufacturing, hopper drying is practiced. The sugar is made to hop on multi-deck hopper conveyors while the hot air, followed by cold air is blown, which will dry and cool the sugar. The lumps are separated out.

Sugar grading

Grader: The sugar is carried through elevators from the hopper and made to fall on a multi-deck vibrating grader consisting of 3 or 4 screens arranged one above the other. Some lumps, joint crystals, etc. are separated out on the top screen whereas the dust is passed through the lowest screen and taken back into the process. The middle decks are fitted with suitable screens to separate and collect the sugar in two or three grades sizewise. The sugar collected from the graders are separately stored gradewise in bins from where it is filled in bags, weighed automatically, and packed.

Warehouse

The sugar industry is a seasonal industry and hence the production takes place only in some part of the year whereas the consumption of sugar takes place all through the year. So in case of white sugar manufacturing, the production cannot be shipped out as and when produced. A substantial quantity of sugar is to be stored in properly constructed warehouses. Sugar being hygroscopic, the construction of the warehouse needs special attention. The flooring has to be waterproof. The roof also has to be protected. In areas of heavy rains, even the roof is covered with a thin layer of waterproof material on the roof sheets. Care should be taken to see that the humidity inside the godown is not too high or not too low. The ventilation is to be controlled accordingly. Generally sugar is bagged in 100 kg bags in India. Elsewhere it is bagged in 50 kg bags.


Manufacture of gur


Sugarcane is the major raw material for the manufacture of' gur, though the product is also prepared in small quantities from palmyra, date and coconut palms. Even today the harvest of cane for gur manufacture is done in a large measure, taking into account the festival days-Deothan Ekadasi, some time in the middle of November in the north when the cane is believed to have ripened, and Pongal in the south in January.(4) 


Three main operations are involved in the manufacture of gur

1. Extraction of juice from cane,

2. Purification of the Juice and

3. Concentration into gur.


Extraction is done with a crusher or series of crushers. The use of wooden rollers fitted close to one another vertically or horizontally have now been replaced by iron rollers, the 3-roller ones being in general use. These are either drawn by bullocks or run by power. The juice extraction is about 55 to 60%. Many crushers of improved design, run by power, are being adopted by progressive and well-to-do farmers. In spite of these improvements, the loss of sugar from cane in gur manufacture remains significantly high. Crushing should be done in the cool hours of the morning and the extracted juice should be boiled without delay.


Purification or clarification-The juice is boiled for concentration and removal of dissolved foreign material in a circular pan, made of iron or zinc sheets, over a furnace. The suspended impurities and the gummy colloidal constituents in the juice are removed by coagulation during the first heating, lasting about 30 to 45 min. Then the juice is clarified to remove all impurities, which would favour inversion of sugar.

 

Clarification is done to prevent non-sugar being formed again in the juice and next to remove as far as possible all objectionable matter which may be present in the juice. A further requirement is to prevent charring.or over-heating during the concentration of juice. The aim therefore is to make it light in colour.

Concentration of juice -The striking-point temperature generally ranges between 118 °c and 123 °C. The juice of bad quality or immature cane needs a higher striking point. The correctness of the striking point is judged by any one of the of the following methods:

  • When the boiling has progressed sufficiently, a small quantity is taken out of the pan and thrown into a bucket of cold water. If the mass forms into a ball with a metallic sound when thrown against the side of the pan, the boiling is taken as complete.

  • At the striking point, the boiling mass when stirred will leave the bottom of the pan clear instead of sticking to it

  • At the striking point, if the wooden stirrer is taken rout of the pan and held in the air, the sticking mass will form long silky threads flowing in the air and will not fall in drops.


Manufacture of Khandsari


The word khandsari or khandsali is derived from the word khandsala, meaning sugar house. This was the term used for a sugar factory in ancient India. Khandsari sugar is yellow to whitish in colour, opaque and is said to have easy digestibility and better nutritive qualities. (4) 


Indigenous manufacture

For centuries khandsari manufacture had been carried on in a crude and inefficient manner, using mostly manual labour. The boiled juice, called the rab, is taken out and filled into bags generally made of wool. These bags are placed one over the other and pressure is applied through manual labour for draining out the molasses. When the flow of molasses stops, the bag is emptied into special pits with brick flooring. The half-treated massecuite is put into the pit and covered with a thick layer of the seaweed. This moistened seaweed generates heat, creating chemical reaction. In 3 days all the mass of molasses is drained and it becomes white. Every alternate day the upper layer of white sugar is removed and the process continued until the entire material is treated. The sugar is sun dried and then it becomes ready for the market. In addition to the low extraction of juice in the crusher, indigenous process is wasteful, crude and unhygienic, Much of the sugar goes into the molasses and there is irregulated method be of crystallization. The recovery is hardly 5 %. An improvement over the indigenous process was the introduction of the centrifugal machine to separate the molasses as completely as possible. The colour of the product is sought to be improved by washing with hot decoction of soapnut fruit and mixing sodium bicarbonate.


Improved process

An improvement was effected in 1948 at the National Sugar Institute, Kanpur, by utilizing the open-pan sulphitation process. Introduction of lime sulphitation technique in the khandsari sugar industry improved the recovery and quality of sugar onto white crystalline lustrous product resembling white sugar from vacuum-pan factory. The recovery reported was as high as 7.5%. The improved process has the following advantages over the indigenous process: the recovery is 2% higher, the sugar produced has crystalline lustre, resembling mill sugar and thus commands a ready market, and the price level is governed by the price structure of mill sugar. The disadvantages over the indigenous process are the capital required is more, the process is more complicated and needs technical skill and good management, and the molasses is not so good for edible purpose.

In India nearly 35 million sugarcane growers and about 3.6 lakh workers have been closely associated with the sugar industry and depend on their livelihood on this single crop , and therefore, in the present socio-economic set-up it has become imperative that maximum benefit accrues to all the sectors of sugar industry from sugarcane. Apart from the self-sufficiency of sugar requirement a part of it is exported to other countries to earn foreign exchange so essential to build up other industries. About thousands and above crores of Rupees is locked up in this industry which provides employment to technicians, scientists, farming community and a large number of village labour.  (2,19,20)


With the manufacture of the sugar machinery in India a new era has dawned in the country which saves the drain on country’s resource in building of the industry further. The industry has helped the cause of agriculture. It is estimated that about 25-30 million people are directly interested in the industry, major part of which forms the growing of sugarcane in the field and making it available to the industry connected with gur and sugar making.

The problems connected with the working of sugar industry by the farming community provides a good ground for preparing the business and other leadership in the village which is so essential to commercialize agriculture, if it is to be paying and attractive proposition on the years to come.

Besides the above there are several other ways the sugar industry is likely to be a great boon for socio-economic development of agriculture in the rural area. The districts in which more than 5 factories have been established, have undergone a phenomenal change in the surrounding areas from the point of agricultural development, educational awakening, cultural and economic progress in various ways.

Export Potential

Sugar was second only to coffee as an individual source of export income for developing countries and a larger earner than all cereals, livestock products, forestry and fishery products. This highlights the dependence of developing countries on cane sugar exports and the pressures which they must be under because of the drastic decline in sugar revenues. To further emphasize the relative weightings and importance of sugar to developing countries it might be added that 70 percent of world sugar output is produced in developing countries and 85 percent of cane sugar. Similarly, developing countries account for 65 percent of total world sugar exports by volume, including 80 percent of cane sugar exports.


It is a known fact that Indian sugar has been selling in the world market at lower values for a host of reasons, the most notable being the limited demand for plantation white sugar with the added disadvantage that countries which import such white sugar are generally construed to suffer from limited buying capacity. Indian plantation white sugar has to face tough competition from Brazilian crystals which generally are sold at hefty discount. As any further export beyond the current level would expose Indian sugar to much heavier discount, it is felt that incremental exports should be made in terms of raw sugar. India had some hands-on experience in the export of raw sugar to meet the European Union quota obligation in the past at the behest of the Indian Sugar Exim Corporation (ISEC).


The quality of raw sugar produced in India is wholesome and is likely to command a premium mainly because Indian raw sugar is free from dextron which normally retards processing and contributes to reduced refinery yield. In fact sugar industry is of the view that incremental exports beyond the level of 1 to 1.2 million tonnes might be in the form of raw sugar rather than white sugar. Raw sugar export could be made in much larger quantities at Lower Ocean freight as it would be in bulk form rather than in bagged form. Production of raw sugar would also entail cost saving benefits to the factories by way of enhanced capacity utilisation, lower use of chemicals and high recovery percentage. (14,16,19,20)
