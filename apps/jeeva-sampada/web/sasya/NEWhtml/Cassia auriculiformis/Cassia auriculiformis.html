HABIT & HABITAT
 

Erect bushy shrub, present throughout Central and South India, in most districts on dry stony hills and black cotton soil. 



Availability :  
In India, the species grows wild in the Deccan plateau and South India.  It thrives best on dry stony hills and black soils.  Its bark is mostly used as a tanning material in South India.  It also occurs in Andhra Pradesh, Maharashtra, Karnataka and Rajasthan.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

Shrub 2-4 m tall, bark grayish, branchlets finely pubescent; leaves pinnately compound, 5-9 cm long, stipules large, 0.8-1.0 cm foliaceous, reniform, persistent, cordate at base with one end produced into a tail, leaflets 6-12 pairs, oblong-obovate, 1-2 x 0.5-1.0 cm, thin coriaceous, glabrous above, sparingly puberulous below, base cunneate-truncate, margin entire, apex obtuse-retuse, mucronate, petiole to 1.5 cm, eglandular rachis with linear, erect, stipulate glands opposite to all leaflets; stipules lunate, foliaceous 0.7-1.0 cm, persistent, petiolate up to 4 mm. Recemes corymbose or subterminal up to 10 cm, peduncle up to 2 cm, bract stipular, bracteolate linear, 1 mm Flower yellow with orange veins, 3.5 cm across, calyx lobes 5, ovate, outer ones smaller than the inner; Petals  5, golden yellow; stamens 10, upper 3 staminodes (with filaments 3 mm and empty anther 1.2 cm),  7 lower stamens, 2 small with filaments 1-2 mm and curved anther 4 mm and 4 long with filaments 1-2 mm;  ovary stipitate, falcate up to 1.7 cm, pubescent, style is 1 cm. Pods flattened, linear, seeds 6-12.

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Roots, leaves, flowers, seeds, bark

Uses

  • Avaram bark is the principal indigenous tan bark used in south Indian tanneries, although since world war I, but wattle bark and extract have affected the extent of its use.
  • Avaram tannin penetrates the hide quickly and produces a light coloured leather with elastic grain and good tensile strength.  The leather however develops an objectionable brick red  colour on exposure to light, but this can be obviated by giving the avaram tanned leather a final bath in myrobalan extract. The tan liquor obtained from this species is stable and has low tannin loss on standing.  An important tanning feature of this bark is that it is easy to use and yields uniformly successful results.  In past, the half tanned leather was exported, where its tannage and  preparation for a variety of uses were carried out.  It was used in the production of famous East Indian leather.  It is also used in medicines and as a green manure.


Harvesting Practices
The tannin content increases with age, although after the third year the increase is not considerable.  The size of the twigs from which the bark is taken is of more importance than the age.  Twigs thicker than a lead pencil size which have not developed a corky kind of bark appear to be the best.

Plants grown on lime-rich soils are rich in tannin than those grown on red loam and gravelly soils.  The product usually employed in factories contain 18% tannin and 10% soluble non-tans.  The tannin content of the bark varies with the age of the plant, in older plants the tannin content may be up to 23%, while in the young plants it is only 12% or sometimes even less.

The method of collection of bark consists of cutting of shoots from the base and stripping off the bark from shoots.  The bark obtained from the shoots is dried and transported to tanneries.

Processing Techniques
Extract prepared by leaching avaram bark at 95-100°C are too dark for use.  Leaching at 50°C adding a small quantity of sulphur dioxide, and concentrating to 66% total solids, is stated to give an improved extract.  A light coloured extract is obtained by extracting the bark in a closed vessel under pressure or partial vacuum.  It has been observed that the leather tanned by unstripped twigs is as good as that obtained by using stripped bark, and the farmer can be safely employed for tanning in places close to the source of supply.

Other Uses
  1. The roots are astringent, cooling, depurative and alexeteric and are useful in skin diseases, leprosy, tumours, asthma and urethrorrhoea.
  2. The bark is astringent, alternative and a decoction of  bark is used as enemas and in gargles.
  3. Leaves are used in diabetes, urethrorrhoea, nocturnal eission and pharyngopathy.
  4. Seeds are astringent, sour, cooling, constipatin, depurative, aphrodisiac, anthelmintic, stomachic, alexeteric and are useful in diabetes, chyluria, ophthalmia, dysentery, diarrhea, swellings, abdominal disorders, leprosy, skin diseases, worm infestations and chronic purulent ad conjunctivitis.
