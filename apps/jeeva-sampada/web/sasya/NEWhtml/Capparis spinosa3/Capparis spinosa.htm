Taxonomic Details

Name : Capparis spinosa L. 
Family : Capparidaceae
Classification :
Class : Magnoliopsida 
Subclass : Dillenidae
Order : Capparales 

Synonyms : Capparis cordifolia Lamk (1785), C. mariana Jacq. (1797), C. sandwichiana DC. (1824)

Common Names: 
English : Caper, Caper bush

Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Tall shrub, mostly xerophytic.

Climate and soil
Thrives under semi-arid or seasonal conditions. occurs in drier parts of South-East Asia in the lowlands, often along seashores, up to 350 m altitude. Grows well in temperature over 40˚C and annual rainfall of 350 mm. Found on dry, well-drained sandy soils derived from lava or limestone (1).  

Availability :

Distributed from Mediterranean through the East to India.

Conservation/ Threat Status : 

Back to Top

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES: Shrub up to 2 m tall, branches prostrate, twigs terete, unarmed, with short, floccose, greyish indumentum which soon disappears. Leaves alternate, simple, glaucous; petiole 0.7-1.3 cm long; blade subfleshy when fresh, ovate-elliptical to suborbicular, 1.5-7.5 cm x 2.5 5.5 cm, base truncate to rounded, apex rounded to obtuse, veins in 5-7 pairs (1).

Back to Top

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR: Flowers solitary, axillary, showy and fragrant; pedicel 4.5-7.5 cm long, glabrescent; calyx zygomorphic, with sepals in 2 pairs, posterior sepal deeply saccate, 2.5-4 cm x 1.7-2.5 cm, other sepals subovate, 2-2.5 cm x 0.7-2.5 cm; petals 4, in 2 pairs, white, turning red-purplish, upper pair rhombic, 3-5.5 cm x 2-4 cm, with a thick fleshy base, lower pair 3-3.5 cm x 1.5-3.3 cm; stamens 100-190, 4.5-6 cm long, white to reddish; pistil on a 6-7 cm long gynophore which is hairy near the base (1). 

Fruit: Fruit a berry with a thin, leathery to corky pericarp, ellipsoidal, 2.5-5 cm x 1.3-1.5 cm, olive-green with distinct ribs (1). 

Breeding system and varietal improvement: Propagated by seeds or cuttings.

Phenology:
Flowers are noctiflorous. Flowering starts in one year after sowing and produces flowers continuously year after year (1).

Pollinators: Insects

Floral and seed biology: Seeds numerous, subglobose, 4 mm in diameter, embedded in a yellow fruit pulp.


CUTLIVATION ASPECT:
Seed germination:
Fresh seeds germinate readily, while dried ones are erratic due to seed-coat-induced dormancy (1).

Related taxa:
The genus Capparis L. is a large pantropic genus of about 250 species, predominantly distributed in the sunny, warm and dry habitats having seasonal climates. In India and western Himalayas, var. himalayensis (Jafri) Jacobs occurs, having twigs late glabrescent, petiole 2-7 mm long, gynophore 4.5-7 cm long). Typical var. spinosa occurs in Mediterranean. var. mariana occurs in South-East Asia. Other varieties are var. nummularia (DC.) F.M. Bailey, species are: C. buwaldae Jacobs, C. pyrifolia Lamk, and C. zeylanica L. medicinally used species are: C. micracantha DC., C. pyrifolia Lamk, C. sepiaria L., C. zeylanica L. (1). 

Chromosome number:
2n=24, 38.
Selection of planting material:
Seeds are the planting material.

Planting:
Seeds are chemically scarified in sulphuric acid. Sown a few cms deep in seedbed. Plants from stem cuttings are better, for which, cuttings of 1.5 cm diam. is planted (1). 

Management after planting:
Mulching is done in first year to ensure optimal rooting. Pruning is done in every winter to remove dead wood. Rooted seedlings are transplanted to field at 2-2.5 cm x 2-2.5 cm spacing, resulting in densities of 1600-2500 plants/ha (1).

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Commercial capers are the pickled flower buds and used as a condiment. Used in salads, mayonnaise and other cold sauces. They keep their spicy taste during cooking, and combine well with grilled or stewed meat, and even better with fish. Approved for food use by Food and Drug Administration (FDA) of US (1). 

Parts Used: Unopened flower buds


Harvesting Practices: Young flower buds are harvested at regular intervals of 8-12 days. Very labour-intensive. Yield is 1-3 kg per plant annually (1).

Post Harvest Handling
Fresh buds are bitter, dried ones are pleasantly spicy but lose their aroma quickly. Harvested buds are first kept in dark room for 3-4 hours and subsequently put into vinegar or salted vinegar. After 8 days they are removed from vinegar, pressed and transferred to fresh vinegar, which is repeated for another 8 days. Then graded according to size using sieves, smallest being the best quality (called ‘nonpareil’ capers) (1).

Produce and Products: ‘Nonpareil’ capers. 


Chemistry: Sharp taste is similar to those of mustards and horseradish. Have a similar effect as a digestive stimulant. The attractive flavour is due to capric acid. 

Per 100 g edible portion contains approx.: water – 79 g, protein – 5.8 g, fat 1.6 g, carbohydrates – 6.5 g, fibre – 5.4 g, ash – 1.6 g. Caper contains rutin and a number of kaempferol- and quercetin-derived flavonoids. Yellow rutin crystals are present in small groups in flower buds (1).

Processing Techniques:

Other uses: Used in traditional medicines, but not in South-East Asia. Flower buds have beneficial effect on lesions of the vascular system. Infusions or decoctions obtained from root bark are used for ailments like dropsy, anaemia, general atony and arthritis. In India root bark is considered a purgative, tonic, diuretic, anthelmintic, emmenagogue and analgesic, and extracts are a common ingredient of hepatoprotective herbal drugs and traditional medicines such as Liv-52 (1).
