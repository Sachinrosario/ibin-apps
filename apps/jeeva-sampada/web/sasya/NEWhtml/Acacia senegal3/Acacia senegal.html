 Other Biological Details
HABIT & HABITAT
A small thorny, deciduous tree. Native of Africa, it has become successfully naturalized in several parts of India particularly  in Rajasthan, Gujarat, Harayana, and Delhi (the Aravalis).

It is most common in the areas with 300-400 mm rain/year but can grow in areas with as little as 100mm, and a dry period of 8-11 months.  Altitude range of 100-1700m.  Very drought resistant and tolerant to high daily temperatures but sensitive to frost.  Prefers well drained, sandy soils but can grow on slightly loamy sands.

Thrives on dry rocky hills, in low-lying dry savannas, and areas where annual rainfall is 25–36 cm. This hardy species survives many adverse conditions, and seems to be favored by low rainfall and absence of frost. Ranging from Warm Temperate Thorn through Tropical Thorn to Tropical Dry Forest Life Zones, gum arabic is reported to tolerate annual precipitation of 3.8–22.8 dm, annual mean temperature of 16.2–27.8°C, and pH of 5.0–7.7.


Availability :  

The Acacia Senegal inhabits Africa from Senegal eastward to Egypt. It is called Hashab, and yields the most valuable gum, that coming from Dejara, in Kordofan, being the best product. It is known as Hashabi gum. The gums yielded by this species are known as the Kordofan and Senegal gums. It constitutes large forests north of the River Senegal.

Widespread in tropical Africa from Mozambique, Zambia to Somalia, Sudan, Ethiopia, Kenya, and Tanzania. Cultivated in India, Nigeria, and Pakistan. Drought-tolerant, A. senegal is the characteristic species in the drier parts of Anglo-Egyptian Sudan and the northern Sahara and is to be found throughout the vast area from Senegal to the Red Sea and to eastern India. 

It has become successfully naturalized in several parts of India, particularly in dry rocky hills of Sind, Rajasthan, northern Aravalli hills, Gujarat, Punjab, Haryana and Delhi. Found in Tamil Nadu also.

It extends southwards to northern Nigeria, Uganda, Kenya, Tanzania and southern Africa. In India it is found chiefly in Sindh and Ajmir. In Sudan, the tree exists both in the wild and cultivated - mainly on sandy hills, but it also grows well in cotton soil.

A. senegal is associated with a wide range of vegetation types, from semi-desert grasslands to Anogeissus woodlands. It prefers clay plains and rocky hill slopes. It can easily become a serious weed. South Africa and Australia have policies against further introduction of this noxious weed.

Native: Angola, Botswana, Burkina Faso, Eritrea, Ethiopia, Gambia, Kenya, Mali, Mozambique, Namibia, Niger, Nigeria, Senegal, Sudan, Tanzania, Uganda, Zambia, Zimbabwe

Exotic: Australia, Egypt, India, Pakistan, Puerto Rico, South Africa, Virgin Islands (US)

Status :Not threatened.

Rare in open places.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

A tree with yellowish white bark and spreading feathery crown. Trees are characterized by the presence of three spines at each node, the central one is hooked downwards and the two laterals are straight and directed upward. Leaves are bipinnate with stipular spines; pinnae 6-10, rachis not above 2.5cm, downy, pinnules glaucous green. Flowers off-white to cream, fragrant and bisexual in axillary  5-10cm long spikes. Flowers are bisexual. There are over 100 stamens in each flower. Anthers are dorsi-fixed and terminate in a stalked gland. Each anther is 8-loculed and each locule bears one polyads comprising 16 pollen grains. Pollen grains are shed at 2-celled condition and rich in lipids. Average number of pollen grains per flower ranges from 13,000 to 15,000.

Stigma is cup-shaped, wet and non-papillate. The receptive region is confined to the concave surface of the cup-shaped stigma. The length of style ranges from 7 to 10 mm in different populations. The style is solid with a core of transmitting tissue. The ovary is unilocular, superior and bears about 10 ovules.

Pods straight, strap-shaped, ca 7.5 cm long with 2-8 seeds

Chromosome no:(2n=26)

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Breeding System :
The species is an outbreeder and  self-incompatible, although a small  proportion of  fruits result from geitonogamous self-pollination.  Self-incompatibility is of the late acting type. Pollen tubes - even in self-pollinated flowers enter the embryo sac. Stigma becomes receptive 2h prior to anther dehisce and continues to remain so for a period of 8-12h.

Reproductive biology of only a few Australian and African species of Acacia has been investigated.  The investigated species exhibit protogynous dichogamy.  Many acacia species are self-incompatible and the site of incompatibility is reported to reside in the nucellar region.  Except for a report on some aspect of floral biology, pollination biology and fruit set of A. senegal in Senegal, there are no studies on reproductive biology of the species. 

Anthesis occurs between 0730 and 0930h and the anthers start dehiscing from 1030h onwards.  The stigma becomes receptive 2 h prior to anther dehiscence and continues to remain so for a period of 8-12h.  On the day of anthesis the pollen grains in a polyad showed 71% viability.  After 24h the viability was reduced to 46%.  Temporal studies of pollen germination and pollen tube growth in manually pollinated pistils showed pollen germination within 2h after pollination.  Pollen tubes grew for 18-24h in the style before reaching the ovules (Rajesh Tandon, K. R. Shivanna and H. Y. Mohan Ram, 2001).

None of the emasculated and bagged flowers set fruit, ruling out the possibility of apomixis.  Similarly, there was no fruit set from any of the non-emasculated and bagged flowers (n=120); none of these flowers showed the presence of polyad on the stigma, indicating the absence of spontaneous autogamy (Rajesh Tandon, K. R. Shivanna and H. Y. Mohan Ram, 2001).

 

Phenology :
Trees remain partially/completely leafless for about 4 months (March-mid June). Leaves appear on the branches by the end of June followed by the emergence of new shoots. Flowering is initiated in July with the onset of monsoon. The inflorescences arise only from the axils of leaves of the new shoots and thus occupy a peripheral position on the canopy. Fruits are initiated during the peak of blooming and continue until the end of September. The fruits mature by the end of January. Leaf shedding is initiated by from January. The pods remain on trees even after they mature and dry. Fruits dehisce during March but their seeds remain attached to their pods. Fruits are dispersed by wind and rain during June and August (Rajesh Tandon, K. R. Shivanna and K. R. Shivanna, 2001).

Trees in a particular population showed synchronization of phenoevents.  Trees remained partially or completely leafless for over 4 months from march until mid-June.  Leaves appeared on the branches of the previous year by the end of June followed by the emergence of new shoots.  Flowering was initiated in July with the onset of monsoon.  The inflorescences arose only from the axils of leaves of newly developed shoots and thus occupied a peripheral position on the canopy.  The summit of the canopy was the first to come to bloom and the flowering subsequently extended to lower branches (Rajesh Tandon, K. R. Shivanna and H. Y. Mohan Ram, 2001).

Fruit development was initiated during the peak of blooming and continued until the end of September.  Fruits attained their full size in 20-25 days, and matured by January.  Leaf shedding started from January, and by February and March the trees became leafless, while their pods remained attached.  Fruit dehiscence occurred during March but seeds remained attached to the pod walls.  Fruits are dispersed by wind and rain water during June-August (Rajesh Tandon, K. R. Shivanna and H. Y. Mohan Ram, 2001).

Pollinators:

A. senegal is presumably insect pollinated.
Mass effect will be created by the tiny flowers crowded into spikes and fragrance act as insect attractants. A small amount of nectar is present in the nectar cup. Individual flowers are rather conspicuous.  Nevertheless, being crowded into a spike they present a visual impact on visiting insects,  Flowers are visited by a variety of insects such as bees, butterflies, beetles, thrips and wasps. Giant Asia honey bee Apis dorsata  is the most effective pollinator  The nectar is accessible to all the insects. Pollen and nectar are the rewards for floral visitors. One-day-old flowers appear faded when compared to freshly opened  flowers; faded flowers are not visited by insects. (Rajesh Tandon K. R. Shivanna, H. Y. Mohan ram 2001)..

Fruit set: Fruit set is rather low. Average fruit set through open pollination is only 0.86%. Flower : fruit ratio is 250-400 flowers :1. Fruit set resulting from manual pollinations was as high as about 30% indicating that insufficient pollination is the main cause for low fruit set. 

Seed Biology :
Seeds are bronze coloured with a persistent sigmoid funiculus. Each seed has an  horse-shoe shaped marking called a pieurogram. Seeds exhibit dormancy due to impermeable seed coat.  Seeds that escape predators remain dormant in the soil.  In nature seeds germinate during monsoon when water is available. Seed should be harvested before pods have dried for easy collection and to avoid insect attack. Seed is easily extracted by hand. Freshly extracted seed should immediately be dusted with an insecticide. There are 10,000-30,000 seeds/kg. Seed storage behaviour is orthodox; 5% germination following 51 years open storage at room temperature; viability can be maintained for 6 years with air-dried seeds stored at room temperature; viability can be maintained for several years in hermetic storage at 10 deg. C with 4.5-9% mc.

Unlike other acacias, the seed coat of A. senegal is not impermeable to water even after storage, and scarification is normally not necessary.  Germination is improved if the seeds are soaked in cold water for 12-24 hours before sowing.

The number of seeds developing in a fruit varied considerably.  One to eight seeds developed out of 9 to 13 ovules present in the ovary.  Three and 4 seeded fruits were more common than 1, 2 or 5 to 8 seeded fruits. 

The pattern of seed abortion in the pod is also non-random.  The order of both seed development and seed abortion was positively skewed.  Nearly 80% of the fruits were showed seed development at the first position (stylar end.  Seed abortion at the first position was around 30% (Rajesh Tandon K. R. Shivanna, H. Y. Mohan ram 2001).

Seedling Germination :


It is easy to propagate from seeds and germination is fast and uniform. The seeds germinate in clumps in 4 days after sowing and the total germination period is 8 days. Overnight soaking and removal of a portion of testa considerably enhances germination.

The seedlings attain an average height of 11cm. in the first growing season in gravel soil and 20cm in sandy soils.  Natural recruitment of fresh seedlings occur along  the seasonal drains when rain water drags the seeds on the ground.  

Propagation is made either from direct seeding of treated seeds or via nursery-grown seedlings in various kinds of containers ; naturally the farmer is much cheaper and used to be a part of the traditional management of the Acacia bush-fallow production system of Kordofan.

Fresh seed requires no pretreatment if sown immediately after harvest. Seed collected in previous seasons, however, requires pretreatment to break seed dormancy. Soaking seed in water for 12-24 hours gives good results and is simple to apply. Seeds can also be nicked. More mature seeds can be immersed in concentrated sulphuric acid for 3-15 minutes or dropped in boiling water for 5 seconds. Artificial regeneration by way of shoot cuttings is possible. With vegetative propagation, seedlings grow best when they are watered every 7th day. Some grass cover or mulch seems necessary for optimal seedling establishment.

A. senegal is usually raised in the nursery in polyethylene pots, 2-4 seeds per pot, thinned to one seedling after 4-6 weeks. Direct seeding (5-8 seeds in 30 x 30 x 30 cm pits or larger) can also be used.

Strict protection from fire, livestock grazing and efficient control of weed competition during at least the first two years is important to seedling survival. Minimum spacing for block planting is 4 x 4 m. At 10 x 10 m spacing, agricultural intercropping is possible, for example, inter planting with millet, beans, or groundnuts.


Cultivation:
For intercropping, 10x10m spacing is suitable.  For gum production plants can be raised in the nursery or in direct seeded plantations spaced at about 4x4m.

In Sudan, trees are cultivated over a very large area. Best propagated from seeds  in special "gum gardens." Elsewhere, it is collected from wild trees. In Pakistan, the best period for afforestation is the early monsoon season (Apr.–Jun.). Surface sowing is recommended in mildly alkaline sandy soils. Plants can also be reproduced by shoot cuttings. Trees coppice well.

Occurs between the 100 and 800 mm of mean annual rainfall, mainly between 200 and 600 mm. It is extremely drought resistant as it occurs close to the very border of the Sahara and West Asian Deserts.

Soil: A. senegal is sensitive to water logging. In the  areas of its distribution it tends to be restricted to sandy habitats and dry river beds, but to fine textured soils under the higher rainfalls of the South Sahelian and North Sudanian ecozones., it may also occur on shallow soils and duripan lithosols. The tolerance to pH is quite broad : 5-8 .

Weeding should be immediate in the season following planting and for the following 2 growing seasons. Protection of young trees from livestock browsing is essential. Seedlings are rather slow growing with initial annual increments of 30-40 cm. For firewood production, trees can be cut on a rotation of 20 years using coppice with standards, lopping up to 50% of the lower crown in rainy season.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

Seed is very susceptible to insect attacks.  The buffalo treehopper (Stictocephala bubalus) may destroy seed crops.  Larval stage of Coleoptera (bruchids), Lepidoptera and Hymenoptera damage the seed. Spiders (Cyclops sp.) may completely cover the young growing apex. Seedlings are often grazed by gazelles, goats, and pigs. Locusts (Acridium melanorhodon) can defoliate vast areas overnight.
Diseases: 

Fungi reported on this crop are Cladosporium herbarum, Fusarum sp., Ravenelia acaciae-senegalae and R. acaciocola.

Uses

Gum arabic is the dried exudation obtained from the stems and branches of Acacia senegal (L) Wildenow or closely related species. Its uses fall into three main areas: food, pharmaceutical and technical. The concrete juices of other species are also included under the commercial name Gum Acacia.

Gum arabic exudes from the duct of the inner bark; it is tapped in the hot season (May-June) when the trees are stressed. Tapping begins when trees are 4-5 years old. It commences after leaf fall and ceases during the colder months of the dry season. Gum nodules form in 3-8 weeks, exuding from the former broken abscission scars. Care should be taken to keep the gum clean. Annual yields stand at 188-2856 g for young trees and 379-6754 g for older trees (7-15 years). Gum production is excellent on poor soils and higher in stressed trees.

  • The gum is used extensively in confectionary especially in the preparation of sweetmeats, as an adhesive, stabilizing agent in soft drinks, In confections, gum arabic inhibits sugar crystallization and acts as an emulsifier to prevent fat from separating and forming a surface film. In beverages. it acts as a clouding agent and foam stabilizer. Flavor companies use gum acacia to emulsify flavor emulsions. By forming a thin film around the flavor particle, it functions as a fixative for spray-dried flavors. In candy products it is also used as a glaze.
  • It finds wide application as a means of encapsulating flavours and is also used in a range of dairy and bakery products as a glaze for topping the surfaces (candy, snack foods, alcoholic and nonalcoholic beverages, baked goods, frozen dairy desserts, gelatins, and puddings, imitation dairy products, breakfast cereals, and fats and oils).  It is used in soft and alcoholic drinks, either as a vehicle for flavouring or as a stabilizer or clouding agent.
  • Pharmaceutically, it is used in the manufacture of cough syrups and tablets. Gum arabic's use is much less than it once was, and it has been displaced by modified starches and celluloses.  However it still finds some use in tablet manufacture, where it functions as a binding agent or as a coating agent prior to sugar coating, and it is also used as a suspending and emulsifying agent, sometimes in combination with other gums; as demulcent for inflammations of the throat or stomach and as masking agent for acrid tasting substances such as capsicum; also as a film-forming agent in peel-off masks. 
  • The demulcent, emollient gum is used internally in inflammation of intestinal mucosa, and externally to cover inflamed surfaces, as burns, sore nipples and nodular leprosy. Also said to be used for antitussive, astringent, catarrh, colds, coughs, diarrhea, dysentery, expectorant, gonorrhea, hemorrhage, sore throat, typhoid, urinary tract  
  • Industrially the gum is used an adhesive, protective colloid and as a safeguarding agent in printing inks, where it is used to treat offset lithographic plates; as a protective coating to prevent oxidation.
  • It is also used as component of solutions to increase hydrophilicity and impart ink repellency to the plates; and as a base for photosensitive chemicals.
  • Other miscellaneous uses include ceramics, where gum arabic helps to strengthen the clay, certain types of inks, and pyrotechnics.  use in textiles, paints and adhesives (including traditional office glue and postage stamps) has decreased to very low levels in recent years, at least in Western markets.
  • Used for dressing fabrics, giving lustre to silk and crepe; for thickening colors and mordants in calico-printing; in confections and sweetmeats. 
  • Causing partial destruction of many alkaloids including atropine, hyoscyamine, scopolamine, homatropine, morphine, apomorphine, cocaine, and physostigmine, gum arabic might be viewed as a possible antidote. 

Harvesting Practices

The gum flows naturally from the bark of the trees, in the form of a thick and rather frothy liquid, and speedily concretes in the sun into tears; sometimes the discharge is promoted by wounding the trunk and branches. The secretion is most abundant in dry, hot seasons, and among old stunted trees, especially after a rainy season has softened their bark, and rendered it apt to split during the succeeding hot weather. The more sickly the tree appears the more gum it yields; and the hotter the weather the more prolific it is."

Tapping begins when the trees are just starting to shed their leaves.  After five weeks the first collection of gum is made, with further collections from the same tree at approximately 15 day intervals until the end, making five or six collections in total.

The older methods of making small incisions into the tree with an axe have largely been replaced by one which utilizes a specially designed tool, a "sunki".  The pointed end of the head is pushed tangentially into the stem or branch so as to penetrate just below the bark, and then pulled up so as to strip a small length of bark longitudinally from the wood.  Damage to the wood should be minimal.  Several branches are treated in a similar manner at one tapping.  In following years, other branches or the reverse side of the previously treated branch are tapped.

After this superficial injury, tears of gum form on the exposed surfaces comes out, and  are left to dry and harden.  As far as possible, the tears are picked by hand from the stems and branches where they have formed, and not by knocking to the ground where they can pick up dirt.  They are placed in an open basket carried by the collector; the use of plastic sacks has been found to increase the risk of moisture retention and mould formation.

For trees which are planted from seed, tapping starts at age 4-5years; for those planted as seedlings, tapping start in the third year.

Gum is then graded according to the colour and purity. White gum fetches more price in the market.

Yields of gum is very variable, a figure of 250 gms of gum per tree per season is often cited as an average yield.  Yields of several kg or more have been reported from individual trees.

More information:

Gum is normally collected soon after the tear drops become dry, graded according to the colour and purity. White gum fetches more price in the market.

Gum exudes from cracks in bark of wild trees, mostly in the dry season, with little or none in the rainy season when flowers are out. In some areas, a long strip of bark is torn off and the gum allowed to exude. In Africa, it is regularly tapped from trees which are about 6 years old by making narrow transverse incisions in bark in February and March. In about a month, tears of gum form on surface and are gathered. Trees begin to bear between 4–18 years of age and are said to yield only when they are in unhealthy state due to poor soil, lack of moisture or or when they are damaged. Attempts to improve conditions tend to reduce yield. Gum from wild trees is variable and somewhat darker colored than that from cultivated plants. Collected gum is carefully freed of extraneous matter, sorted and sometimes ripened in sun before export. Gum arabic is odourless with a bland taste, yellowish and some tears are vermiform in shape. Ripened or bleached gum occurs in rounded or ovoid tears over 2.5 cm in diameter, and in broken fragments. Tears are nearly white or pale yellow and break readily with a glassy fracture. Gum is almost completely soluble in an equal volume of water and gives a translucent, viscous, slightly acid solution, but is insoluble in 90% alcohol. Kordofan (Sudan) Gum is yellow or pinkish, has fewer cracks and is more transparent

Strangely, Gum Acacia is only produced by trees that are in an unhealthy condition. Gum yields are improved by natural factors that lessen the vitality of the trees – hot weather, poor soil, lack of moisture, etc. It is important to remember that a damaged tree will give a larger yield of gum. Thus, the natives will cut and strip the bark from a tree and return later to remove the tears of gum that form in the wounds or scars. Within 3 - 8 weeks, the gum will start to collect in the wound, but this depends on the weather conditions. Gum droplets are about 0.75 - 3 inches in diameter, and they gradually dry and harden on exposure to the atmosphere. A young tree will yield 400 - 7000g annually.

Commercial high tonnage gum arabic production requires severe physiological stress from water depletion and heat. Gum formation has been regarded as a natural response of trees under dehydration stress to store a strongly hydrophilic form of reserve carbohydrate. Gum is known to be formed at leaf-fall and translocated to roots even though the tree has not been "tapped."

The dry season lasts from October to June and the gum is collected every 10 days. During the rainy season no gum is formed since the trees are in full bloom. After collection, the gum is brought from the farms and stands to villages. From there it’s transported to market. In the Sudan, the gum is auctioned (under government supervision) and this usually sets the world price.

Chemistry

Gum arabic, a highly water-soluble gum of relatively low viscosity. It is the only acacia gum toxicologically tested to establish its safety as a food additive. 

Gum acacia contains neutral sugars (rhamnose, arabinose, and galactose), acids (glucuronic acid and 4-methoxyglucuronic acid), calcium, magnesium, potassium, and sodium. Its complex structure is still not completely known. Its backbone chain consists of D-galactose units, and its side chains are composed of D-glucuronic acid units with l-rhamnose or l-arabinose as end units. The molecular weight has been reported to be between 200,000 to 300,000 and as high as 600,000.

Gum arabic from A. senegal is a pale to orange-brown coloured solid, which breaks with a glassy fracture. The best grades are in the form of whole, round tears, orange-brown in colour and with a matt surface texture; in the broken, kibbled state the pieces are much paler and have a glassy appearance.  Inferior grades, and gum from species other than A. senegal, may not have the characteristic tear shape and are often darker in colour. Gum from A. seyal (gum talha) is more friable than the hard tears produced by A. senegal and is rarely found as whole lumps in export consignments.

Gum arabic is a complex, slightly acidic polysaccharide. The precise chemical and molecular structure differs according to the botanical origin of the gum, and these differences are reflected in some of the analytical properties of the gum. As a result, the functional properties and uses to which gum arabic can be put (and its commercial value) are also very dependent on its origin.

Gum Arabic is extensively used as a food additive, but there is no regulatory or scientific consensus about its calorific value. It is a complex polysaccharide, primarily indigestible to both humans and animals, not degraded in the intestine, but fermented in the colon under the influence of microorganisms. Despite a range of animal studies, there are no usable data for humans which can quantify the utilizable energy of Gum Arabic. Estimates in the literature from animal experiments vary from 0 to 4 kcal/g. After certain allowances are made for the energy losses from volatile and gaseous fermentation products, an upper level of 2 kcal/g for rats has been set. The situation in man is demonstrably different, with greatly reduced amounts of such products, and the need to adapt for varying periods before Gum Arabic is attacked by colon bacteria. In the absence of an agreed scientific assignment, the FDA in the USA insist upon 4 kcal/g in nutritional labelling, whereas in Europe, no value has been assigned to soluble dietary fibre, such as Gum Arabic. This review argues that based on present scientific knowledge only an arbitrary value can be used for regulatory purposes.

It dissolves readily in either hot or cold water to give clear, low-viscosity solutions at concentrations as high as 50 to 55%. Gum arabic is most stable at a pH from 4 to 10

Processing Techniques

When the gum is auctioned, there is a basic cleaning process including sieving, hand selection and grading. The main purpose of this cleaning process is the removal of sand, bark, extraneous material and any adulteration with other gums

The same principles of cleaning and sorting are followed in most other countries and the trader or trading organization then usually exports the graded gum. In Sudan, however, the cleaning process is repeated when the Gum Arabic Company receives consignments of gum from the regional centres at its export warehouses in Port Sudan. Since 1991 the cleaning operation has been mechanized using a system of conveyor belts and shaking and sieving machines. Final inspection of the cleaned gum and removal of any remaining foreign matter or dark coloured pieces is made manually as it moves on a belt to be bagged.

When imported into the consumer countries most gum arabic is further processed into kibbled and powdered forms. Kibbling entails passing whole or large lumps of gum through a hammer mill and then screening it to produce smaller granules of more uniform size. These pieces are more easily dissolved in water, and under more reproducible conditions, than the raw gum and so are preferred by the end-user.

Powdered gum may be produced from kibbled gum but it may also be produced by a process known as spray drying. This furnishes a high-quality, free-flowing powder with even better solubility characteristics than kibbled gum. The gum is dissolved in water, filtered and/or centrifuged to remove impurities and the solution, after pasteurization to remove microbial contamination, is sprayed into a stream of hot air to promote evaporation of  water. By altering atomizing conditions, powder can be produced with varying particle sizes and bulk densities, according to the end-user's requirements. Spray drying is an energy-intensive process and this, together with the requirements for large quantities of pure water, makes it something that most gum arabic producers could not consider. 

The U.S. P. thus describes Acacia: "In roundish tears of various sizes, or broken into angular fragments, with a glass-like, sometimes iridescent fracture, opaque from numerous fissures, but transparent and nearly colorless in thin pieces; nearly inodorous; taste insipid, mucilaginous; insoluble in alcohol, but soluble in water, forming a thick, mucilaginous liquid. Acacia should be slowly but completely soluble in 2 parts of water. This solution shows an acid reaction with litmus paper, yields a gelatinous precipitate with basic lead acetate T.S., ferric chloride T.S., or concentrated solution of sodium borate,—and does not reduce alkaline cupric tartrate V.S. The powder is not colored blue (absence of starch) or red (absence of dextrin) by iodine T.S."—(U. S. P.).

The best quality of gum arabic—that known as Kordofan gum, Turkey gum, or White Sennaar gum-is perfectly colorless, of a shining, conchoidal, vitreous fracture, opaque in mass, but transparent in small fragments, hard but pulverable, inodorous, and of a faintly sweetish and viscous taste. It is generally in tears, round or angular, and seldom larger than a hazel nut. The very pale, yellowish-white, yellowish-red, or brownish tears belong to the second quality, and may be rendered colorless by the action of sunlight, or when treated with chlorine water. The specific gravity is from 1.33 to 1.52. It almost invariably forms a white powder.

Gum arabic does not deteriorate if kept dry, but its concentrated mucilage, after a long time, will become sour (acetic acid). Hot water is said to hasten this fermentation, if employed in making the mucilage. Dilute solutions of the gum become moldy. A few drops of sulphuric acid added to it-the solution being poured off from the resultant precipitate of calcium sulphate—is said to prevent this change.

Other Uses
  1. Wood : Heartwood is nearly black and is used for making weavers shuttle, cartwheels, sugarcane crushers, water-wheels and as a fuel wood.
  2. In many parts of Rajasthan the seeds are used in some traditional dishes and are eaten by rural poor and tribals. The foliage is an important for goats, cattle and camels.
  3. Young foliage makes good forage. 
  4. Plants useful for afforestation of arid tracts, soil reclamation, and windbreaks. 
  5. Erosion control: A. senegal has been used for desertification control, re-establishment of a vegetative cover in degraded areas, sand dune fixation and wind erosion control. The rostrata and leiocarpa varieties are useful for soil stabilization because of their weediness.
  6. Nitrogen fixation: The nitrogen-fixing ability of A. senegal is questionable.
  7. Soil improver: Improves soil fertility by nutrient cycling after leaf fall.
  8. Ornamental: It is grown as an ornamental tree in Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands.
  9. Boundary or barrier or support: Cut branches are useful for fencing in cattle because of their spines.
  10. Intercropping: It is a highly suitable tree for agroforestry systems, widely grown in combination with watermelon, millets and forage grasses. In the Sudan, A. senegal is grown in 'gum gardens' for gum as well as to restore soil fertility. A traditional bush-fallow system is followed with a 20-year rotation during which time A. senegal is grown for 15 years. Agricultural crops are grown for five years (millet, sesame, sorghum, groundnuts), followed by five years with young, unproductive A. senegal trees, which later produce gum during the last 10 years of the rotation. Corresponding to this rotation, 1/4 of the land is kept in agricultural crops, 1/4 in young unproductive trees and 1/2 in productive trees.

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

For some unknown reason, the trees in India do not produce gum in commercial quantities. As a result India imports gum Arabic from Sudan. However, there are reports on induction of gum production following ethephon treatment. This potential need to be explored systematically to produce gum from trees growing in India.

The European Community is by far the biggest regional market for gum arabic.  Imports averaged almost 28000tonnes/year over the six years, with a peak of over 32000tonnes in 1991.

Outside EC, the United states is the largest market for gum arabic.  They averaged 7500 tonnes annually but exceeded 10000 tonnes in 1994.

Japanese imports averaged 1900 tonnes during 1988-94.

Outside Africa, India produces small amounts of gum, similar in quality to gum talha, but a proportion of her exports of gum arabic consists either of re-exports of African gum or locally produced gum ghatti (from Anogeissus latifolia) misclassified as gum arabic.

India has imported around 11921 tonnes of Gum arabic valuing Rs. 2110 lakhs during 1995-96.

Gum arabic is often the principal source of revenue for semi-nomadic African people who gather it from wild, untended plants. Trees are grown commercially ("gum gardens") or in state forests in the Sudan from nursery seedlings or in direct seeded plantations spaced at about 4 x 4 m. Tapping, which consists of slitting the bark and collecting the walnut-sized gum globules three weeks later during the dry season, begins about 5 years after planting and continues for about 15 years. An average annual yield is 250 g/tree, although production may range from a few grams to 10 kg or 0.2 to 6.7 kg. Tree improvement programs and better management might increase quality and production. Gum arabic's export price in Sudan, which produces about 85% of the world's supply, was $3,450/ton (Oct. 1987), but the current trend is towards a lower price.

Annual yields from young trees may range from 188 to 2856 g (avg. 900 g), from older trees, 379 to 6754 g (avg. 2,000 g). Gum arabic is important export product for some areas in tropical Africa and Mauritania. From Africa some genuine gum is shipped to India then to Europe and America. Between 1940 and 1950, United States imports range from 3,179–8,989.  

A. senegal produces some 90 % of the marketed gum arabic sold in the world. Annual world commercial production varies from 20,000 to 60, 000 tons per annum, averaging 40,000.  80 % of the world trade used to originate in the Rep. of Sudan, particularly in the Kordofan province ; but this has decreased and other countries are developing their own production, as it is an open ended market. Production varies widely and wildly from 10 g to 10 kg per tree / annum. The average may be of the order of 100 g per tree and up to 250 g / tree / annum in the best managed orchards of Kordofan.

 Miscellaneous Information

Ingested orally, acacia is nontoxic. However, some people are allergic to its dust and develop skin lesions and severe asthmatic attacks when in contact with it. 

Sudan gum arabic has been an article of commerce for some 2000 years. Originally the gum was sent to Arabian ports, and then to Europe, hence the name ‘gum arabic’. In the Middle Ages, it often went first to Turkish ports and so acquired the name ‘Turkey gum’, which was once commonly used for it in the trade. Today, A. senegal is grown primarily for gum, but plays a secondary role in agricultural systems, restoring soil fertility and providing fuel and fodder.

The term 'Gum Senegal' is not, strictly speaking, synonymous with Gum Acacia, though it is commonly so used. Gum Acacia is the name originally pertaining to Sudan, Kordofan or Egyptian (hashabi) Gum, which possesses properties rendering it superior and always preferred to any other known to commerce. During the political and military disturbances in Egypt between 1880 and 1890, this gum became so nearly unobtainable that occasional packages only were seen in the market. Among the many substitutes then offered, the best was Gum Senegal, which was adopted as the official equivalent of Gum acacia. In this way, it came about that the names were regarded as synonymous. In 1890, the original Acacia again came into the market and eventually became as abundant as ever, but it is no longer possible to entirely separate the two names. Most of the characteristically distinct grades of Acacia Gum are now referred to particular species of the genus Acacia.
