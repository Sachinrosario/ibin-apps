Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
A moderately-sized, spiny almost evergreen tree, found throughout the drier parts of India. Frequent in wasteland. Apparently native to Egypt, the Arabian peninsula and India, cultivated and naturalized in many tropical and subtropical countries.  It is a native of India and widely occurs all over the country.  It is found in almost all states of the country viz., Rajasthan, Gujarat, Andhra Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Bihar, Orissa, West Bengal, Uttar Pradesh, Haryana, Punjab, etc.,

Altitude: 0-1 340 m, Mean annual temperature: 4-47 deg. C Mean annual rainfall: 200- 1 270 mm.

Soil type: Grows on a wide variety of soils, seemingly thriving on alluvial soils, black cotton soils, heavy clay soils, and can tolerate poorer soils.

It has a strong light requirement. Severe frost affects small seedlings as well as large trees. It is drought resistant and occurs in plain, flat or gently undulating ground and ravines. Trees grow best on alluvial soils in ravine areas subject to periodic inundation. It is considered a serious weed in South Africa. The tree is widespread in the northern savannah regions, and its range extends from Mali to Sudan and Egypt.

It is very common species in dry to moist inland habitats nearly throughout India from Punjab to West Bengal southwards, where it often forms pure stands or is dominant in mixed stands. It is usually confined to low elevation sites on flat or gently undulating terrain and ravines, though the species is occasionally found up to 900m elevation. Its common associates include Acacia leucophloe, Prosopis cineraria, Azadirachta indica, Ziziphus jujuba and Cassia auriculata.  

The tree is found to perform well in saline and alkaline soils.  Grows well in black cotton soils and clayey soils.  The annual rainfall requirements range from 590 to 1200mm.  This tree has high adaptability to dry seasons with tolerance of up to 50°C.  It also has a high drought hardiness.  It has a strong light requirement.

Grows in woodlands of various sorts, wooded grasslands, scrubs and thickets. Thrives in dry areas, but endures floods. Grows in 10–1,340 m altitude, in a wide range of conditions. Found on a wide variety of soils, seemingly thriving on alluvial soils, black cotton soils, heavy clay soils, as well as even poorer soils. Ranging from Subtropical Desert to Subtropical Dry through Tropical Desert to Tropical Dry Forest Life Zones. Babul is reported to tolerate annual precipitation of 3.8–22.8 dm, annual mean temperature of 18.7–27.8°C, and pH of 5.0–8.0.

Availability :  
Native from Egypt south to Mozambique and Natal; apparently introduced to Zanzibar, Pemba, and India, Arabia. Considered a serious weed in South Africa.

Throughout the greater part of India in forest areas, roadsides, farmlands and tank foreshores. It is found all over Tamil Nadu. It is also cultivated. It is either gregarious in patches of forests or in groups or single tree in fields.  The largest tracts are found in Sind. There are three varieties of this babul : Telia babul, Kauria babul and Ramkanta babul.

Indigenous to plains of A.P. and Mahrashtra and is cultivated or found naturally throughout the drier parts of India. It is essentially a tree of the plains, occurring on flat or undulating ground and ravines

Native: Botswana, Egypt, Eritrea, Ethiopia, India, Kenya, Mozambique, Namibia, Nigeria, Oman, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, Sudan, Swaziland, Tanzania, Uganda, Yemen, Republic of, Zambia, Zimbabwe

Status : Not threatened.  

It is highly recommended for various types of wastelands as it improves the porosity and productivity of the soil and water supply of wells in surrounding areas.  It is used as shelter belts, avenue trees.  It is an important nitrogen fixing species particularly in dry regions.

The tree is largely grown in homestead and field bund plantations in rural India.  The wood has several uses as a building material and firewood.  The bark is generally removed when the tree is felled for timber or fuel.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Small tree, 2.5–14 m tall, quite variable in many aspects; bark of twigs not flaking off, gray to brown; branches spreading, with flat or rounded crown; bark thin, rough, fissured, deep red-brown; branchlets purple-brown, shortly or densely gray-pubescent, with lenticels; spines gray-pubescent, slightly recurved, up to 3 cm long; leaves often with 1/2 petiolar glands and other glands between all or only the uppermost pinnae; plnnae 2–11 pairs; leaflets 7–25 (-30) pairs, 1.5–7 mm long, 0.5–1.5 mm wide, glabrous or pubescent, apex obtuse; peduncles clustered at nodes of leafy and leafless branchlets; flowers bright yellow, in axillary heads 6–15 mm in diam.; involucel from near the base to about half-way up the peduncle, rarely somewhat higher; calyx 1–2 mm long, sub glabrous to pubescent; corolla 2.5–3.5 mm long, glabrous or pubescent outside; pods especially variable, linear, indehiscent, 8–17 cm long, 1.3–2.2 cm broad, straight or curved, glabrous or gray-velvety, turgid, blackish, about 12-seeded; seeds deep blackish-brown, smooth, sub circular, compressed, areole 6–7 mm long, 4.5–5 mm wide. 
Chromosome no.: (2n=52.)


REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Breeding System :
A. nilotica is a hermaphrodite, 
Phenology :

Leaf fall in Babul occurs for a very short period in April-May.

Leaf renewal is from mid April to the end of May.

Flowering takes place in the period from June to September.

Pollinators:
Pollinated by a wide range of insects, including Coleoptera, Diptera, Hemiptera, Hymenoptera and Lepidoptera. These insects mainly forage on pollen.

Seed Biology :

Seed storage behaviour is orthodox. Viability can be maintained for several years in airtight, moisture-proof conditions at 10 deg. C. with 4.5-9% mc. There are 5000-10 000 seeds/kg.

Pods especially variable, linear, indehiscent, 8–17 cm long, 1.3–2.2 cm broad, straight or curved, glabrous or gray-velvety, turgid, blackish, about 12-seeded; seeds deep blackish-brown, smooth, sub circular, compressed, areole 6–7 mm long, 4.5–5 mm wide.  Seed viability is reported to be 2 to 3 years.  

Seedling Germination :

Natural reproduction is mainly through seed, to a limited extent by seedling coppice and artificial regeneration by direct-sowing.

Direct seeding is commonly used to propagate the tree, though potted seedlings may also be used. 

-----Pretreatment involves boiling seed in water followed by cooling or immersion in concentrated sulphuric acid for 1 hour. 

-----Immersion in cold water for 48 hrs;

-----Immersion of seeds in hot water(80°C) for 30 minutes after which the seeds are soaked in ordinary water for 24hrs prior to sowing; 

------10 to 30 minutes soaking in sulphuric acid (90%) followed by washing in water and drying prior to sowing and; 

------by passing through goats, germination rates of 75-95% can be realized in 1 week. Bare-root seedlings are seldom used because the high incidence of root injury causes poor survival rates.
Natural regeneration is also seen. Trees are propagated in forests by seeds. Stored seed may require scarification. Young seedlings are said to "require full sun and frequent weeding"

Seeds from goat and sheep pens are better for artificial sowing than those collected from pods.

Seeds are sown in raised beds in shade with a medium of soil and compost, in 2:1 ratio, by broad casting or by dibbling.  They are also raised in polythene bags.  The seeds are sown in polybags during February-March, about 1.5cm deep.    Germination commences in 7 to 30days after sowing.  Germination varies between 80 to 90 percent.  About 60 to 70percent plants establish.  The seedlings are maintained in the nursery for a duration of about 5 months.

Vegetative propagation can be practiced through cuttings and air layering.  Stem cuttings from 7 year old plants pretreated with IBA (100,200 or 500ppm) and planted in September in polytunnels.  40% of the cuttings were found to root.

Air layering consist of one year old shoots of three year old plants, pretreated with IBA 4000ppm using a medium of Sphagnum moss and excess water squeezed out to avoid stem decay.  Rooting was observed in 41 days.

Cultivation:

Young seedlings are said to require full sun and frequent weeding. A. nilotica ssp. nilotica coppices very weakly.
The best time of planting out kikar is June/July, since there is enough moisture in the soil in this season.  If irrigation facilities are available, the planting can be done in the month of Feb/March.  But rainy season planting is best and gives good results.

Pit size of 30cm cube should be dug up well in advance or at least one month before the commencement of the planting.  The stakes are marked in the field so that digging of pits may be easy.  Plants of 4 months old can be transplanted from nursery to fields.  By this time the plants may have attained a height of 30cm.  The pit should be filled up with a mixture of soil, sand, FYM in equal ratio.  For planting out in the field, following tree economic models can be followed, 1) Single row 2) Multiple rows 3) Close planting.  Winter is the best season for application of fertilizers.  FYM before planting in the field.  Weeding is carried out before  manuring.  Irrigation is necessary up to first two years after planting out.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests: 

In a survey for phytophagous insects on Acacia nilotica, 43 species were recorded in Pakistan, of these, 16 appeared stenophagous. The more promising for biological control of the tree were: Anarsia sp. cf. acaciae, Pseudosterrha paulula, Azanus ubaldus, and Ceutholopha isidis feeding on flowers; Bruchidius sahlbergi and Sulcobruchus sp. damaging seeds; Ascalenia callynella, Gisilia stereodoxa and an unidentified gracillariid boring shoots; and Cydia sp. making stem galls.

Wood borers may afflict the stems and bruchids may afflict the seeds: Oelosterna scabrator Fabr., Psiloptera fastuosa Fabr., P. coerulia Oliv. and Aeolesthes holosericees Fabr., C. scabrators is a root borer. Whenever the bark is stripped off the shoots and branches, A. holosericea destroys the wood by tunneling. Babul has been recorded as a new host of Hemaspidoprectus cemereus Green. Timber is attacked by Toreda, Martesia,  Sowerby and Limnoria.  

Diseases: 

Heavy seedling mortality is caused by damping off.  The heartwood of the stem and branches is damaged by fungi causing the wood to become brittle.  It also suffers from root-rot, spongy heart-rot by Fomes badius, white spongy rot by Ganoderma lucidum.

Following fungi have been reported on this plant: Fomes pappianus Bres. attack injured and old trees. Other fungi are Ctyospora acaciae, Diatryphe acaciae, Diplodia acaciae, Fomes badius, F. endotheius, F. fastuosus, F. rimosus, Fusicoccum indicum, Phyllactinia acaciae, Ravenelia acaciae-arabicae, Septogloeum acaciae, Septoria mortolensis, Sphaerostilbe acaciae. Trees are also parasitized by Dendrophthoe falcata and Loranthus globiferus var. verrucosus.

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Bark, Seeds, pods

Uses

Gum, Tannin

  • Gum exudes from intact/injured bark is Used in calico-printing,  as a sizing material for silk and cotton, manufacture of paper. 
  • Gum Fried in ghee and used  in the preparation of sweetmeat, ice-creams and  stabilizers. 
  • In pharmacy the gum is used as a substitute for true gum arabic. Also used in diarrhoea, dysentery, diabetes mellitus and  sore throat. Gum Acacia is a demulcent and serves by the viscidity of its solution to cover and sheathe inflamed surfaces.
  • Trees tapped for gum arabic. The gum arabic is still used in making candles, inks, matches, and paints.
  • Inferior varieties are employed in the manufacture of inks, distempers and certain types of paints, and mortars.
  • Inner bark contains 18–23% tannin, used for tanning and dyeing both locally and on a commercial scale. Bark tannin obtained is used for tanning skins.  It also dyes in a buff colour. The bark from older trees is richer in tannin which has a deeper colour and imparts a good colour to the leather and leather is rendered soft.   The tannin of this species is suited for heavier leather rather than for  kips and half tanned hides.  It gives a dark coloured, firm and durable leather. Young pods produce a very pale tint in leather, notably goat hides (Kano leather).  The tannin has a tendancy to ferment.  It is used in combination with other tannins. Bark is the cheapest source of tannin suitable for heavy leathers.  Better results could be obtained when this material is used in combination with myrobalans. 
  • The pods of A. nilotica produces tannin of good quality.  The tannin and the non-tannin content in the pods is 17.5 and 28.08% respectively.  The tannin content is about 18-27 percent after the seeds have been removed from the pods.  Large quantity of the pods of babul are used locally for tanning purposes in Maharashtra, M.P and U.P.  In tanning industries, pods are used not only for tanning but also to soften and impart a good colour to the leather.  An estimated yield of pods from an average tree is about 19kg pod cases.
  • Roasted seed kernels, sometimes used for flavoring and when crushed provide the dye for black strings worn by Nankani women.

Harvesting Practices
The gum harvest from the various species lasts about five weeks. About the middle of November, after the rainy season, it exudes spontaneously from the trunk and principal branches, but the flow is generally stimulated by incisions in the bark, a thin strip, 2 to 3 feet in length and 1 to 3 inches wide being torn off. The gum runs in the furrow and thickens in the furrow in about fifteen days, hardens on exposure to the air, usually in the form of round or oval tears, about the size of a pigeon's egg, but sometimes in vermicular forms, white or red, according to whether the species is a white or red gum tree.  Some trees yields about 1 kg of gum in a year but an average tree yields about 500gms per year.  The yield of the gum depends upon the age of the plant, the yield per tree lessens with increase in the age and some times very old trees do not yield any gum.  The flow can be increased by tapping, but it is not generally practiced.  The colour of the gum exuded varies from pale straw to dark reddish brown or almost black colour depending on the age of the tree and the conditions of collection.  

The tannin content of the bark varies with age.  The tannin content is comparatively higher in older trees than younger trees. 

An estimated yield of pods from an average tree is about 19kg pod cases.

Tannin, timber, gums, oil from seeds

Chemistry
Gum consists principally of Arabin, a compound of Arabic acid with calcium, varying amounts of the magnesium and potassium salts of the same acid being present. It is believed, also, that small amounts of other salts of these bases occur. (Arabic acid can be obtained by precipitating with alcohol from a solution of Acacia acidulated with hydrochloric acid.) The gum also contains 12 to 17 per cent of moisture and a trace of sugar, and yields 2.7 to 4 per cent of ash, consisting almost entirely of calcium, magnesium and potassium carbonates.

  Chemistry of plant:

The plant contains gallic acid, m-digallic acid,(+)-catechin, chlorogenic acid, gallolyated flavan-3,4-diol and robidandiol (7,3',4'5',-tetrahydroxyflavan-3,4-diol).

Babul has been reported to contain l-arabinose, catechol, galactan, galactoaraban, galactose, N-acetyldjenkolic acid, N-acetyldjenkolic acid, sulphoxides pentosan, saponin, tannin. Seeds contain crude protein 18.6%, ether extract 4.4%, fiber 10.1%, nitrogen-free extract 61.2%, ash 5.7%, and silica 0.44%. Phosphorus 0.29% and calcium 0.90% of DM. When bullocks were given the seeds and bran (2:1) with dry pasture grass daily DM intakes were 1.82, 0.91, and 5.35 kg respectively. Total DM intake/100 kg bodyweight was 1.40 kg. The animals retained 20.8 g N and 7.4 g Ca daily but the P balance was slightly negative. The CP content of the browse at 12.9%, the crude fiber at 15.2%

Other Uses

  1. Agroforestry: often used to demarcate fields. Trees used in Sudan for afforestation of inundated areas. 
  2. Apiculture: The fragrant flowers of A. nilotica ssp. nilotica are popular bee forage.
  3. Bark: Astringent, acrid, cooling, styptic, emollient, vulnerary, anthelmintic, constipating, depurative, aphrodiasiac, diuretic, expectorant, alexeteric, emetic and nutritive. It is used in vitiated conditions of kapha and pitta ,haemorrhages, wounds, ulcers, helminthiasis, ascites, chronic dysentery, diarrhoea, leprosy, leucoderma, skin diseases, burning sensation, strangury, cough, bronchitis, leucorrhoea, haemorrhoids, protoptosis, seminal weakness, pharyngodynia, uterovesical disorders, oral ulcers and odontopathy, demulcent. It also shows anti-viral properties. Decoction is used for gargling. Also used as a substitute for soap.
  4. Fodder: The crude protein content of the leaves is 14-20%, and 11-16 % for the highly palatable pods. Pods and shoots are used as forage for camels, sheep and goats, especially in Sudan, where it is said to improve milk from these animals. In India, it constitutes a chief diet for goats and sheep, and seeds are a valuable cattle food.
  5. Food: Tender pods and shoots are used as a vegetable, and roasted seed kernels are sometimes used in Sudan for food flavouring. Air-dried seeds contain crude protein and are eaten raw or roasted in India in time of acute food scarcity.
  6. Fuel: The calorific value of the sapwood is 4500 kcal/kg, while that of the heartwood is 4950 kcal/kg. This valuable source of firewood and charcoal has been used in locomotives, river steamers and small industries. Burning charcoal, however, emits sparks. In India and Pakistan riverine plantations are managed on a 15-20 year rotation for fuel wood and timber.
  7. Medicine: The Zulu of South Africa take the bark for cough. The Maasai of East Africa use a bark decoction as a nerve stimulant, and the root is said to cure impotence. An astringent from the bark is used for diarrhoea, dysentery and leprosy. Bruised leaves are poulticed onto ulcers. The gum or bark is used for cancers and tumours of the ear, eye or testicles and indurations of the liver and spleen, condylomas and excess flesh. Other ailments treated by these products include colds, congestion, fever, gallbladder, haemorrhage, haemorrhoids, leucorrhoea, ophthalmia, sclerosis and smallpox. Bark, gum, leaves and pods are used medicinally in West Africa. Sap or bark, leaves and young pods are strongly astringent because of the tannin the possess, and in Senegal are chewed as an antiscorbutic, and in Ethiopia as a lactogogue. A bark decoction is drunk for intestinal pains. Other preparations are used for, gargle, toothache, ophthalmia and syphilitic ulcers. In Tonga, the root is used to treat tuberculosis. In Lebanon, the resin is mixed with an orange-flower infusion for typhoid convalescence. In Somalia, the wood is used to treat smallpox. Egyptian Nubians believe that diabetics may eat unlimited carbohydrates without any consequences as long as they also consume the pods in powder form. Extracts are inhibitory to at least 4 species of pathogenic fungi.
  8. Pods/seeds : Food for goats and sheep. Seeds useful in treating stomach complaints and skin disorders. 
  9. Poison: The aqueous extract of the fruit, rich in tannin (18–23%), has shown algicidal activity against Chroccoccus, Closteruim, Coelastrum, Cosmarium, Cyclotella, Euglena, Microcystis, Oscillatoria, Pediastrum, Rivularia, Spirogyra and Spirulina.
  10. Reclamation: In India, this species is used on degraded saline and alkaline soils. It grows well when irrigated with tannery effluent and colonizes coal mine waste heaps. Over 50 % of the Chambal ravines in India have been re-vegetated with A. nilotica. ssp. nilotica.
  11. Seeds: Eaten roasted, oil is extracted with petroleum ether.
  12. Soil improver: Probably nitrogen fixing. Boundary or barrier or support: This subspecies makes an ideal windbreak surrounding fields; its narrow crown shades less than other windbreak species.
  13. Tender branches are fodder for goats and sheep. Leaves are good for treatment of diarrohea. 
  14. The aqueous extract of the fruit, rich in tannin (18–23%) has shown algicidal activity against Chroccoccus, Closteruim, Coelastrum, Cosmarium, Cyclotella, Euglena, Microcystis, Oscillatoria, Pediastrum, Rivularia, Spirogyra, and Spirulina.
  15. The exhausted tanned stuff consisting of used bark and possibly pods form cheap fuel,  also suitable for paper making.
  16. Wood: Hard, heavy and durable timber useful in house construction, for making agricultural implements, as firewood and for charcoal making. Sapwood is yellowish-white, heartwood reddish-brown, hard, heavy, durable, difficult to work, though taking a high polish. Because of its resins, it resists insects and water, and trees are harvested for the timber for boat making, posts, buildings, water-pipes, well-planking, plows, cabinet-work, wheels, mallets and other implements. It is also used for spokes, naves, and axle for bodies of carts and for shafts and yokes, agricultural implements, ploughs, harrows, crushers and Persian wheels, tent pegs, boat handles, bedsteads, railway wagon, tops, walking sticks, carving and turnery, hammer handles.  Wood yields excellent firewood and charcoal. 
  17. Young bark used as fiber, twigs esteemed for tooth brushes (chew sticks).

Miscellaneous Information

This tree is the "babbula" of Sanskrit writers, who mention the use of young leaves and pods as an astringent. The bark is used as a substitute for oak in government hospitals in India.

 

In Maharashtra State, areas of A. nilotica are closed by law for regeneration in five-year rotations. The removal of leaves from this species should be restricted to prevent damage to flower development and a consequent loss of pods.
