DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:
 Stem terete-obtuse angular, glabrous or thinly pubescent towards the apex. Leaves long petioled, deltoid-ovate to rhomboid-oblong, 2-7 x 1.5-5.0 cm, shortly mucronate at apex, cuneate to subtruncate at base, subentire or repand at margin, glabrous; petioles up to 10 cm long. Flowers in axillary or terminal, 2.0-11.5 x 0.2-0.5 cm, slender, often paniculate spikes; male and female flowers intermixed but female more numerous; bracts and bracteoles deltoid-ovate to lanceolate-ovate; bracteoles shorter than the tepals. Tepals or perianth 3, rarely 4, 1.2-1.7 mm long. Utricles subglobose, 1.2-1.5 mm in diam., shorter beaked, corrugated, indehiscent or rupturing irregularly. Seeds rounded, 1.0-1.2 mm in across, dark brown to black, reticulate.


REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:
Inflorescence: Small axillary clusters and in slender terminal and axillary paniculate spike-like racemes; Flower: Numerous, sessile, bracteolate, pale green, shortly pedicellate ; Male Flower: Perianth of 5  membranous, subequal segments, sepals 5, ovate, bristle pointed ; Androecium: Stamens 3, staminode 0, anthers 2-celled, filaments free ; Female flower:  Perianth-segments oblong, erect in fruit, sepals 5, oblong, obtuse, apiculate; Gynoecium: Ovary ovoid, compressed, 1-celled, ovule solitary, subsessile, erect, styles 2 or 3; Fruit:  Utricle, indehiscent, suborbicular, compressed, orbicular, rugose ; Seed: Lenticular, smooth, shining, black. 


Floral and seed biology:

Flowering & Fruiting:  September- November.

Mode of pollination and seed dispersal:

 Flowers are pollinated by insects. Seeds are dispersed by wind.

Genetic variability:
The plant has reported chromosome number 2n=34.

AGRO TECHNOLOGY:
Propagation: Plant is propagated through seeds.

Soil Type: Plant thrives well in sandy loams.

Irrigation: First irrigation is given just after the broadcasting the seeds. The next irrigation is given at the interval of 10-15 days till the crop becomes 2-3 months old.

Nutrient requirements: Well rotten farmyard manure and the mixture of NPK fertilizers are also recommended.

Harvesting: Grains are harvested they are fully mature, foliage are cut periodically when leaves are crisp and green.

Economic Potential and Uses
UTILIZATION:

Chemistry:
A sterol has been isolated from roots of A. viridis and assigned the structure 24-methylene-20-hydroxy cholest –5,7-en-3 b-ol. (Roy et al, 1982).

24-Ethyl-22-dehydrolathosterol (24-ethyl-5a-cholesta-7, trans-22-dien-3b-ol, spinesterol) is the predominant component of the 4-desmethylsterol fraction separated from the herb extract of A. viridis. The structure of this sterol was established from its 13C NMR spectrum. The other sterol tentatively identified in the fraction by GLC were three D7-sterols (24-methyllathosterol, 24-methyl-22-dehydrolathosterol and 24-e-thyllathosterol) and two D5-sterol and 24-ethyl-22-dehydrocholesterol). (Behari et, 1986).

Total fatty acid from leaves contain palmitic acid (77.8%). (Khan and Khan, 1989). C.A. 1991, 114, 3392n.

Chemical analysis of nutrient composition in A. viridis were analysed for water, energy, fat, protein, minerals, amino acids and carotenoids. Leaves of A. viridis were in particular rich in beta-carotene (3290micrograms/100g). (Nordeide et al, 1996).

The contents of oxalic acid and calcium in several wild edible plants including Amaranthus viridis were analyzed. Oxalic acid was determined by a spectrophotometric method based on the decrease of absorbance at 335nm due to the dissolution of the zirconium (IV)-chloranilate complex, consecutive to the addition of oxalic acid. Calcium was determined by at absorption spectroscopy. (Guil et al, 1996). C.A. 32165y, 1996, 125.

Nutritional (ascorbic acid, dehydroascorbic acid and carotenes); antinutritional and toxic components (oxalic acid, nitrate and erucic acid) were determined in sixteen popular species of wild edible plants including A. viridis. The carotenoid content is 15.4mg/ 100g and the nitrate content is 597mg/ 100g. (Guil et al, 1997).

The leaves of seven plant foods were analyzed including Amaranthus viridis. The fatty acid, vitamin E, carotenoid, selected mineral and amino acid contents of these plant foods were determined. In quantitative and qualitative terms, Amaranthus viridis was found to be an excellent source of protein. It also contained considerable amounts of the two fatty acids that are essential in humans (linoleic and alpha-linolenic) and a number of minerals including iron, magnesium, calcium and zinc. (Sena et al, 1998).

Retinol and b-carotene content of indigenous raw and home prepared foods in Northeast Thailand were studied. The criteria used to select vitamin- A rich foods Retinol and b-carotene contents were determined prior to and following cooking using HPLC methodol. (Pongtorin et al, 1999). C.A. 1999, 130, 152843t.

Leaves of seven plant species were analyzed for their mineral, amino acid and fatty acid contents including as one of them A. viridis. It has been found to contain and large quantity of iron and significant amount of selenium and phosphorus was there. (Freiberger et al, 1999). C.A. 49329x, 2000, 132.

Flavanoid glycoside of four Amaranthus species were investigated. Eight flavanoid glycoside were found based on aglycons Kaempferol, quercetin, isoharmnetin, 3,7,4-trihydroxy flavone afrormosin and diadzein. (Kawashty et al, 1999). C.A. 90714g, 2000, 132.


Pharmacology:
An antiviral protein purified from the leaves of Amaranthus viridis was named amaranthin. It shows an antiviral activity which was confirmed in tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) infection test on Nicotiana glutinosa leaves. The molecular mass of Amaranthin was estimated about 30kDa by SDS-PAGE and the pI was measured as 9.8 by isoelectric focusing (IEF) analysis. Cytotoxicity of the amaranthin using in vitro translation inhibition assay was similar to that of pokeweed antiviral protein (PAP) with IC50 of 25 pM Depurination activity (N-glycosidase activity) against animal rRNA was also confirmed. (Kwon et al, 1997).
Ethnobotany:
Digestive (leaf, Lal and Yadav, 1983); Scorpion and centipeds bite (leaf, Das et al, 1983); Vermifuge (leaf, Lal and Yadav, 1983)
Pharmaceutical and Toxicology:
Amasterol was found to be allelochemic in nature. The toxicity of the compound was tested on the seeds lettuce. It induced inhibition of seed germination and growth of seedlings. The hexahydro derivative of amasterol and amasterol acetate were also found to be toxic to lettuce seeds. Moreover amasterol was found to strongly inhibit the growth of Helminthosporium oryzi, a pathogenic fungus. (Roy et al, 1982).
