HABIT & HABITAT

Throughout India,
It is a native of tropical Africa. Its occurrence in the scrubby jungles of the outer Himalayas in what would appear to be a truly wild state, together with the undoubted antiquity of the knowledge of its use as a drug, as revealed by Sanskrit literature, are held to point to its being a native of India as well as of Africa.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

An annual or perennial bush or occasionally a soft wooded small tree upto 6 m. or more.
Leaves green or reddish, 30-60 cm in dean, palmately 5-11 lobed, lobes serrate and petioles with conspicuous glands.
Flowers monoecious, in spikes 30-60 cm long with the lanulate flowers on the lower and the pistillate flower on upper part of the axis.
Fruit a capsule covered with soft spine like processes and dehisicing into 3-2 valved cocci.
Seeds oblong, smooth, variously coloured, mottled, varying much in size.
Two chief groups - the large seeded and small seeded types can be recognize, the former yielding an oil considered to be inferior to that of the latter.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Breeding system - castor is monoecious, self pollination is common but cross pollination is also frequent. Flowers are said to be excellent honey - producers. Breeding work to evolve improved strains combining high yield performance, high oil content and desirable agronomic characters has been carried out in the country.

Breeding system and varietal improvement:

Phenology: Fl - July-Aug.


Pollinators:
Mostly wild pollinated and self pollinated.
If cross pollinated - bees.
Self pollination is common but the flowers are said to be excellent honey producers for bees and hence cross pollination is also frequent. Under natural conditions, the extent of cross pollination may vary from 5 to 15% and might even reach 36%.

Floral and seed biology:

Seedling Establishment :
The time of sowing varies from region to region. In general, it is sown in June-July or later in Sept.-Oct. The seed is usually sown in lines 1-2m apart, depending upon whether it is a pure or mixed crop. Seed rate varies from 4-8 kg/ha to 12-15 kg/ha. Soaking of seed for about 12 hrs. prior to sowing is said to improve germination.

Application of organic manure is said to be beneficial.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts Used:

Stem, seed, leaves, roots, seed oil, seed oil take.


Harvesting Practices:

For Seed :

In case of non-shattering capsules, the spike is harvested soon after it is fully day.

In case of shattering capsules, harvesting of the spikes is done before they dry up and while the capsules are still green. The harvested spikes are stacked in heap still the capsules blacken and they are then spread out in the sun to dry. The seeds are beaten out of the capsules by sticks or threshold under the feet of bullocks.


Produce and Products:
Stem - pulp - writing, printing, wrapping paper, newsprint; straw boards (when pulp is digested with lime).

Seed - castor oil - as a cathartic, lubrication and illumination. Used in abortifacient pastes, in contraceptive creams and jellies; as emollient, as solvent for removing irritating substances in the eyes. Also used in veterinary practice, used in the preparation of liquid disinfectants, as a preservative to food grains and pulses, in preparation of hair oils, air fixers, aromatic perfumes, hair lotions and tonics, lipsticks used in soap making, as an emulsifying agent, as a dispensing and wetting agent in cotton finishing, air deodarants, mining operation and for dispersion of pigments in paints; used in tanneries.

Dehydrated castor oil - quick drying and water resistant varnishes and enamels, in varieties of drying oil products oiled fabrics, linoleum, patent leather, inks, lithographic varnishes.

Turkey red oil - for dyeing, bleaching and sizing in the textile industries, in tanning, as emulsifying agent.

Hydrogenated castor oil - castor wax, opal wax - in polishes, candles, insulating materials, coating compositions for paper, textile and leather, plastic moulding compositions, heat-sealing adhesives and for the preservation of fruits and vegetables.

 


Chemistry:
Castor seeds contain a power - lipase. They also contain amylase, invertase, maltase, endotrypsin, glycolic acid oxidase, ribouclease, and a fat soluble zymogen activated by acids. Catalase, peroxidases and reductase are reported in germinating seeds, use of castor seed lipase has been suggested in laundering and dry cleaning formulations in the concentration of vitamins in the fish oil industry, as a bating aid in the leather industry and in the ripening of cheese.

Castor seeds contain toxic components which are highly poisonous to human beings and animals. Ricin and a feebly toxic alkaloid ricinine are present.

Ricin possesses antigenic or immunizing action when administered in small doses. It loses its toxicity santigenic action on treatment with potassium permanganate.

Castor seed has high concentration of allergen which is protease in nature and stable to heat and chemical. It is non-toxic to unsensitized human.

Ricinine is a water soluble alkaloid concentrated mostly in the seed coat. It is bitter to taste and is irritant to the throat.

Castor oil consists principally of ricinoleic acid. Stearic, olive, linoleic and dehydroxystearic acids are also present in small amounts.


 

Processing Techniques:
Castor Oil - Oil is generally obtained by crushing whole or decorticated seds in power driven closed or open hydraulic presses (capacity, 10-12 tonnes/day), expellers (capacity 3-4 tonnes/day), and rotary mills (capacity of a pair, 0.5 tonnes/day), and in bullock driven ghanis or in manually operated screw presses.

Crude castor oil is yellow to brown in colour and contains mucilaginous matter which is removed by treatment with hot water followed by filtration activated earth or bone black.

Used as an adulterant with rosin oil, blown oil and other unheated oil. Castor cake - as manure, as a source of synthetic fibers, distempers, oil bound water paints, adhesive compositions and moulded products.

Leaves - powdered leaves for repelling aphids, mosquitoes, white-flies and rust mites, in the form of a poultice or fomentation on sores, boils and swelling, infusion of leaves for stomach ache, as a lotion for the eye.

Fresh juice of leaves - as an emetic in poisoning by narcotics.

Roots - as decoction for lumbago and allied complaints in the form of paste for toothache.

Root bark - powerful purgative.
