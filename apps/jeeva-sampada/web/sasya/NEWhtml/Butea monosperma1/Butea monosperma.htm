Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

A deciduous tree, commonly found throughout the India, except in the arid regions. It is capable of thriving where most species will not grow. It is common throughout the greater part of India, ascending the Himalayas up to 900 m and in peninsular India up to 1,200 m. The tree is very typical of open grasslands, where it is frequently gregarious as in the terai in North India. It grows in waterlogged situations, on black cotton soils, even on saline, alkaline and swampy badly drained soils and on barren lands. It is common in certain mixed deciduous forests of an open character.

In the natural habitat of the tree, the absolute maximum shade-temperature varies from 37.80 to 48.90 and the absolute minimum from 3.90 to 15.60 and the normal rainfall from 62.5 to 450 cm or more.

Drought resistant, frost hardy in its natural habitat.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

A deciduous tree with a somewhat crooked trunk, up to 15 m in height and 1.6-2.0 m (sometimes up to 3.8 m) girth. Bark bluish grey or light brown. Leaves long-petioled, 3-foliate, leaflets coriaceous, broadly obovate from a cuneate or deltoid base, glabrescent above, densely finely silky below, reticulate venation.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Flower buds dark brown. Flowers bright orange red, sometimes yellow, 15 cm long racemes on bare branches. 5 petals are apparent i.e. a standard, two smaller wings petals and beak shaped keel. Stamens 10, 9 united and 1 free, enclosed within the keel.

Pods pendulous, silky-tomentose, 10-13 cm long, containing one seed at its apex. Seeds reniform, flat.


Breeding system and varietal improvement:

Phenology:

Leaves begin to fall during November - December and by the end of January the tree is leafless or nearly so. The new leaves appear in April or early May. Flowering begins in January and continues till the end of April, according to the locality. Seasonal conditions influence the time of flowering to a considerable extent, and flowering is earlier than usual in dry seasons. The pods with a seed each at the apex ripen during May-june.

Pollinators:

Floral and seed biology:

Seed Germination & Propagation: Seeds germinate early in the rainy season, not long after the pods fall to the ground. If moisture is plentiful and sustained, germination takes place without difficulty. Natural reproduction from seeds and suckers is profuse, owing to the hardiness of the seedlings, their immunity to damage by grazing and their power of recovery from injury.

It reproduces by seeds and suckers. Seedlings develop a very long taproot at an early age, which reaches a length of 60 cm and a diameter up to 4.3 cm. seedlings die-back to the ground-level 2-3 times due to frost and drought or heavy weeds and stiff soils before they establish well and send permanent shoots.

Artificial regeneration can be effected both by direct sowing and transplanting from the nursery, where direct sowing is more successful.

The tree can be raised by line-sowings, both irrigated and unirrigated, with or without other field crops. The plants are thinned out, if congested, at the end of the first season and this operation is continued regularly. Weeding is done during first year for proper development of the plants.

Vegetative propagation by air-layering. Air layers stablished maximum in May.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts Used:

Moderate fuel. Calorific value - 4,925 cal. Wood is used for making charcoal.

Wood: Wood is white when freshly cut, ageing rapidly to pale yellowish brown, frequently becoming greyish brown or grey from stain. It is lustrous when first exposed, especially on the radial surface, but soon becomes dull from stain; it has rather rough feel. It is soft, light (wt, 545 kg/m3), straight or somewhat cross-grained and extremely coarse-textured. Not a strong timber.

It is mainly used for well-curbs, water-scoops and rough packing-cases. It is a source for charcoal used for gun-powder. It can also be employed as a cheap board-wood. Sometimes it is used for structural work.

Pulp obtained from the mixture of hardwoods containing the wood of B. monosperma is suitable for Braille printing paper. It also can be used for making newsprint. Timber is good under water.


Harvesting Practices:


Wood should be converted as green as possible and open-stacked under cover.

Produce and Products:

Butea gum / Bengal gum - A red juice from natural cracks and also from the artificial incisions in the bark. When fresh, the juice is ruby-red and transparent. It dries to form small elongated tears or irregular, elongated masses, smaller than grains of barley, apparently black and opaque but actually ruby-red and translucent.

Other uses:

Medicinal - bark, roots, leaves, flowers, seeds

Fodder - The green leaves are commonly lopped for fodder; the yield of milk in buffaloes, fed with Butea leaves, is reported to improve.

Leaves - Leaves are used as plates, donas and for parcel wrapping.

Flowers - A brilliant, but very fugitive, yellow dye is obtained in the form of a decoction or an infusion from dried flowers. Flowers yield red dye.

Ornamental - The tree in flowering is ornamental.

A valuable tree for recovering salt lands.

Seed oil called as muduga oil is vermifuge.

Leaves are used to make plates.
Bark is used for cordage and sails.
Gum, known as bengal kino or butea kino, is medicinally important.
Ash of tender branches is medicinally useful.
