 Other Biological Details
HABIT & HABITAT
Large evergreen  tree of the Western Peninsula, from Kanara to Travancore, ascending to 4,000ft.  

It prefers laterite soils with heavy layer of humus in the locality.  In the regions of its natural presence the mean annual rainfall ranges from 2000 to 3000mm.  The optimal mean annual temperature ranges between 24 to 30°C.


Tree is in Ever green and Semi-evergreen forests of the W. Ghats and its foot hills in Karnataka and Kerala.  It is confined between the altitudes of 60m and 1200m.  It occurs most frequently in wet evergreen forests.  It prefers rich soil having a moderately thick top horizon of humus with high moisture content and good drainage.  It mostly occurs in areas having underlying rocks of gneissic complex, often covered with laterite in varying degrees of disintegration.  The tree is hygrophilous and a strong shade bearer.  Fire is very inimical and wipes out the regeneration and sometimes may even kill the mature stand.  Maximum leaf fall is during the month of march.  At the same time copper coloured foliage begins to appear.  The second flush of new foliage starts after rainy season from October to December.

Availability :  

Endemic to Western Ghats in Maharashtra, Karnataka, kerala and Tamil Nadu.  In Karnataka, common throughout coastal forests and the windwrd side of Ghats; also planted along highways.  In Kerala, abundant near stream banks of moist forests.  In Tamil Nadu, reported from evergreen forests of Coimbatore and Tirunelveli districts.  Apart from Ghats, it is also found in the Deccan plateau and along the Arabian Sea Coast, the altitude range being 60 to 1220mts.



Status : Red List Status of this species has been assessed as "Vulnerable (Globally)".

It is a lofty, elegant and evergreen tree; highly desirable for avenue planting.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
It is large tree, young branches and inflorescence stallately tomentose.  Leaves 15-27 x 10-15cm, elliptic-oblong, shortly acuminate or obtuse at apex, rounded at base; lateral nerves 12-15; distinct beneath, petiole 1.5 to 2.5cm long, flowers 6-7mm across, white in axillary or terminal, 12-14cm long panicles.  Calyx tube short; lobes 5, oblanceolate; corolla-lobes5, oblong.  Stamens many; anthers linear, often hairy at base, ovary pubescent; style linear, stigma small, Fruit is ovoid, rough, pale brown in colour, capsule with large cotyledons, fleshy and unequal.  Seeds single.

Seed Biology :

Seeds can be stored in gunny bags for a period of one month after which its viability is lost.  About 50 to 60 fruits weigh one kg.
Best way to collect fruits is from below the mother trees.  Germination starts within 2-3 days, after the fruit has fallen on the ground and may continue for a month.  Fallen fruits are attacked by insects, which eat on the kernel; hence fresh collections are preferable.  The fruit has plenty of food material for the young plant.  Even the insect eaten fruits produce seedlings.  Vivipary is also seen in the species, particularly in those trees where the fruits mature after the start of regular monsoon. Profuse regeneration can be seen below the mother trees, during the rains, but it dies during the summer months, except on stream banks where adequate moisture remains available throughout the year.  In suitable localities natural regeneration is abundant.

Back to Top


Seedling Germination :

Natural regeneration- Ripe fruits are viviparous.  In its natural habitat, regeneration is quite satisfactory.  The heavier seeds fall close to the tree.  They germinate readily and a large number of seedlings spring up around the seed-bearers and in other favourable places where seeds can reach by dispersal.  The seedlings withstand a considerable amount of shade, but are sensitive to drought; both shade and moisture are necessary for their survival.  They are also sensitive to frost.  Retention of 70-100 trees in top canopy for shade and removal of all middle and undestroyed frees it from undue competition and encourage good growth.  The tree is susceptible to fire; it wipes out the regeneration and occasionally mature stands may also be destroyed.

Artificial regeneration:

Freshly collected fruits are sown in polybags.  The fruit is kept on the surface and covered with leaf-litter.  This gives better germination and plant percent than covering the seeds with soil.  It has been observed in the nurseries that certain amount of decomposing leaf-litter is a must for the better start of the seedlings.  It is quite probable that the young roots are adapted to get their nutrition from decomposing leaf-litter.  The litter has something to do with improved aeration or microbial association.  This needs further study.  Germination is complete within 3 weeks.  Seedlings are maintained in the nursery till the next planting season; by then they reach a height of 70-80cm.  Watering and weeding are done judiciously.  Light overhead shade is beneficial for plant-form and vertical growth.

Direct sowing of fresh, ripe seed gives best results.  Shade is required at an early stage and should be protected from excessive sunlight.  The seed has marvelous vitality, even considerably injured sees germinate and the seedlings continue with apparent vigour.  Sowing can be done in strips 3 x 3m apart with a spacing of 3 x 3m along the centre of the strip.  Germination commences in 25 o 150 days and lasts for a period of one to one and half months.  About 80 to 98 percent seeds germinate.  Seedlings are ready for transplanting after two or two and a half months.

Cultivation:
It is observed that the species prefers open space and only needs lateral shade; therefore planting in open patches in suitable localities is advisable.  Seedling raised in polybags may be planted in pits.  The rate of growth of roots is very fast after germination, hence long polythene bags (at least 25cm) are necessary.  The plant succumbs to root damage during transplanting hence planting of bare root seedlings does not succeed.

Planting of 1 to 2 and a half months old seedlings can also be taken up with fairly good results.  Weeding of plants and gradual opening up, both of shade crop and the canopy, is necessary for subsequent growth of the seedlings.  Care and protection is needed against strong westerly winds that precede the South West monsoon which may damage seed crop.  Delayed monsoon and long breaks can cause damage to regeneration and sometimes to trees also.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests: 

A large number of insects have been reported to infest the tree.  The larva of Massicus venustus Pascoe bores the heartwood of stumps, fire-scorched poles and overmature trees.  The beetles and larvae of Sphaerotrypes vateriae Beeson bore the bark.  The dead sapwood is infested by Dinoderus minutus Fabr., Heterobostrychus aequalis Waterh ouse, and Lyctus africanus Lesne, Ptilinus binodulus Mot., and Cyphagogus corporandi Kleine and several others bore the deadwood.  Species of Xyleborus damage newly felled or fallen wood.


Diseases: 

The wood is susceptible to several fungi.  Lentinus praerigidus Berk causes a white spongy heart-rot; Fomes melanoporus Mont. causes butt-rot.  Polyporus gilvus (Schw.)Fr. produces a brownish pocket-rot; Polystictus flabelliformis Klotzsch and P.xanthopus Fr. cause a white spongy rot.  The logs and plants when freshly cut are attacked by Ceratostomella and Graphium spp. which live mainly on the reserve foods and not on the cell-walls.

Uses

The resin exuded by the tree is know as Piney resin, White dammar or Dhupa and is commercially important.  

The resin when it is soft, is called Piney Varnish, but when hard, called Indian Dammar.  The resin readily dissolves in turpentine and is in good demand for the manufacture of varnishes.  In turpentine, it assumes a turbid and milky appearance, but with addition of a little charcoal and careful filtering, the solution becomes clear and transparent.  When spread in thin layer, it dries with a beautiful white surface.  Since it is soluble in camphorated alcohol, ther esin can be used in varnish paints and anatomical preparations.  

It occurs in three forms;1)Compact piney resin is bright orange to dull yellow in colour having a glossy fracture and resembling amber in general appearance.  2)Cellular piney resin is light green to yellow or white in colour and occurs in shining masses; 3) Piney resin having a dark colour is found in the pockets of old, decaying trees.

  • Resin is in demand for varnishes, contain an essential oil  with marked antibacterial activity.

  • Resin is superior to copal in certain properties.  Being readily soluble in turpentine, it is used in home made varnishes.  It is also used for torches or candles along with coconut oil.  Its solution in chloroform can be used as a substitute for amber in phtographer's varnish.

  • Resin is considered tonic, carminative and expectorant and used for chronic bronchitis and throat troubles, piles, diarrohea, rheumatism, tubercular glands, and boils.  It is used for  the treatment of cough, asthma, leprosy, skin eruptions, crack infection, whitlow, wounds, ulcers, gonorrhoea, dysentery, anemia, chronic bronchitis, tubercular glands, diarrhoea and ringworm.

  • Resin forms a constituent of  an  ointment  for carbuncles and is a good emollient for plasters and ointment bases.

  • Resin is used for incenses, caulking boats, and for preparing  bases for setting gold ornaments.

Harvesting Practices

Two methods of tapping of resin are employed in South Kanara. In one, the bark is wounded in the beginning of  the dry season. The resin begins to ooze within 3 or 4 days and continues flowing for 2-3 months. The second method is to light a fire round the base of the tree so as to scratch  the bark, which then splits and the resin exudes. The latter method is considerably harmful to the tree.

Generally the resin is obtained by tapping the tree by making semicircular incisions on the stem through the cork cambium, up to the surface of the sapwood.  Blazes or cuts are so spaced as to cause the least damage to the tree.  The resin starts oozing from the incisions in 3-4 days and continues for 60-90 days. 

The resin that exudes naturally usually contains much impurity.  

Resin occurs in three forms:

1) Compact piney resin, in lumps of all shapes which varies in colour, on the outside, from bright orange to a dull yellow, has a bright vitreous fracture and internally presents all shades of colour from a light green to a light yellow. It is very hard, and bears a general resemblance to amber, from which characteristics, added to its colour, it may be easily distinguished from all other Indian resins.

2)Cellular Piney resin: Occurs either in small lumps or in large masses, generally of a shining appearance and balsamic smell.  It has a distinctly cellular structure, partly owing to the mode of collection, and partly to the age of the tree.  Notches are cut in the trunk of the tree sloping downwards and inwards, the resin collects in the cavity and is either permitted to dry in situ, or is collected and dried by heat.  It varies in colour from light green to yellow or white, and is more opaque, of a dull green colour, and full of air bubbles.  

3)Dark Coloured Pine Resin: It is occasionally obtained on splitting open old and decayed trees.  It has the solid consistence of the first, but the inferior quality of the second variety.

Chemistry

The resin is a complex mixture of several triterpene hydrocarbons, ketones, alcohols and acids, along with small amounts of sesquiterpenes.  

The resin yields, on distillation, 82 percent of a volatile oil of agreeable odour, but which does not differ essentially from that obtained from cheaper resins.

Other Uses
  1. Wood is much in demand for plywood making and marketed as Malabar white pine. Also suitable for railway sleepers, packaging-cases, tea-boxes, ammunition-boxes, cheap interior fittings and flooring in buildings, and  temporary  structures.
  2. Also used for for shuttering, centering, and scaffolding; and for oars, masts etc.
  3. Seeds yield a fat called Piney Tallow, Malabar Tallow, or Dhupa fat. Tallow may be used after refining for edible purposes in confectionery and as an adulterant of ghee; also used for soaps and candles. 
  4. Seeds cake is used as manure.
  5. Fruit shell contains 25% tannin.
  6. Bark is alexipharmic, used medically  to check diarrohea.
  7. The fatty oil is antibacterial and is useful in controlling chronic rheumatism.
