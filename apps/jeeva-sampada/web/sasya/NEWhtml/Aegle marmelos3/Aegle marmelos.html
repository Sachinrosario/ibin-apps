Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Aegle marmelos is a native of North India in the sub Himalayan tract and outer hills from Jhelum eastwards and grows as a forest tree in northern dry mixed deciduous forests, dry plains sal forests. It is found along the foot hills of Himalayas throughout U.P., Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, the Deccan plateau and along the east coast. It is now naturalized in Sri Lanka, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Myanmar, Thailand and most of southeastern Asian countries. It is, however cultivated for its fruits throughout the country except the temperate zone where it cannot grow because of the cold climate. It is a tree of subtropical range, In its natural range, the absolute maximum shade temperature 400 to 46°C, the absolute minimum temperature from -1 to 7°C and the average annual rainfall from about 570-2,000mm. It is said to do best on rich, well-drained soil, it grows well in swampy, alkaline or stony soils. Grows luxuriantly in the soils having pH range from 5 to 8. In India it has the reputation of thriving where other fruit trees cannot survive (3).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
The bael fruit tree is slow-growing, medium size, up to 40 or 50 ft (12-15 m) tall with short trunk, thick, soft, flaking bark, and spreading, sometimes spiny branches, the lower ones drooping. Young suckers bear many stiff, straight spines. A clear, gummy sap, resembling gum arabic, exudes from wounded branches and hangs down in long strands, becoming gradually solid. It is sweet at first taste and then irritating to the throat. A thorny deciduous tree with trifoiliate, alternate leaves, borne singly or in 2's or 3's, are composed of 3 to 5 oval, pointed, shallowly toothed leaflets, 1 1/2 to 4 inches (4-10 cm) long, 3/4 to 2 inches (2-5 cm) wide, the terminal one with a long petiole (7). New foliage is glossy and pinkish-maroon. Mature leaves emit a disagreeable odor when bruised (2).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Fragrant flowers, in clusters of 4 to 7 along the young branchlets, have 4 recurved, fleshy petals, green outside, yellowish inside. The bisexual flowers are nearly 2 cm wide, borne in clusters, sweet scented and greenish white. The shallow calyx has 5 short sepals and is pubescent on the outside. The 5 petals are oblong ovoid, blunt, thick, pale greenish white and dotted with oil glands. Stamens are numerous, sometimes coherent in bundles. Ovary are oblong ovoid, slightly tapering, axis wide, cells numerous (8–20), small arranged in a circle with numerous ovules in each cell (4, 10).

Breeding System :
Self-pollinated.

Phenology :
The old leaves are shed in April-May and new ones appear in May-June. Thus the trees are leafless for a short period in summer. The flowers, which are fragrant and greenish white in colour appear in the summer season from March to May in North India. The flowers are in lateral panicles arising from the axils of leaves (3).

Seed Biology :
The fruit, round, pyriform, oval, or oblong, 2 to 8 inches (5-20 cm) in diameter, may have a thin, hard, woody shell or a more or less soft rind, gray-green until the fruit is fully ripe, when it turns yellowish. It is dotted with aromatic, minute oil glands. Inside, there is a hard central core and 8 to 20 faintly defined triangular segments, with thin, dark-orange walls, filled with aromatic, pale-orange, pasty, sweet, resinous, more or less astringent, pulp. Embedded in the pulp are 10 to 15 seeds, flattened-oblong, about 3/8 inches (1 cm) long, bearing woolly hairs and each enclosed in a sac of adhesive, transparent mucilage that solidifies on drying. About five seeds weigh to a gram (2).

Seedling Germination :
The seeds have no dormancy and start germinating when provided with favourable conditions. Germination is hypogeous. Seeds are sown in well prepared nursery beds in lines 20 cm apart and with a spacing of about 5 cm between the seeds. Depth of sowing is generally 1.5 cm. Germination starts in about two weeks and takes abbut six weeks to complete. The germination percentage is generally 50 to 60. The seedlings have moderately thick and long primary root with only a few 1ateral roots. The growth of the seedlings is slow and they do not grow taller than 10 cm in the first season (3).

Cultivation:

Natural reproduction is mainly through coppice, and root suckers and seedling regeneration is not very reliable because the seeds are liable to insect attack and somewhat perishable. The seedlings grow slowly and are trampled down and browsed. The coppice shoots grow faster than the seedlings and, attain a height of about 3 m in 11 years. The coppice shoots need tending and care until these are out of browsing and fire damage (3).

The bael is commonly grown from seed in nurseries and transplanted into the field. The seeds are recalcitrant and can not be stored for longer periods under normal storage conditions. Sowing in the nursery is done in May-June. Entire plants of two years age are planted out in July in 30 cm3 pits dug in advance. The plants are uprooted from the nursery with balls of earth and the root portion is wrapped in moist gunny bags. Planting should preferably be done on cloudy or rainy days.

Budding, patch or shield on seedling rootstocks in June or July gives very good success. Air layering is also successful under humid tropical conditions. In vitro propagation has also been standardized but it is not feasible commercially (4).

The tree has no exacting cultural requirements, doing well with a minimum of fertilizer and irrigation. The spacing in orchards is 25 to 30 ft (6-9 m) between trees. Seedlings begin to bear fruits in 6 to 7 years, vegetatively propagated trees in 5 years. Full production is reached in 15 years. In India flowering occurs in April and May soon after the new leaves appear and the fruit ripens in 10 to 11 months from bloom–March to June of the following year (2).

Following are the best cultivars with regards to yield and fruit quality.

NB 5—Fruit size medium, round having smooth surface at maturity, low mucilage, moderately fibrous, soft flesh with excellent taste.

NB 6—Fruit size medium, round with smooth surface, thin rind, few seeds, soft flesh, low mucilage, mild acidic.

Pant Shivani—Mid season cultivar, shape ovoid oblong, size 2 kg, color lemon yellow at ripening, fiber and mucilage content medium, rind medium thick, pulp light yellow with very good taste and pleasant flavor

Pant Aparna—Late cultivar, fruit size small (0.6-0.8 kg), globose, seed, mucilage, fiber and acidity low. Flesh yellow, sweet, tasty and having good flavor rind medium thick.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

A number of insect pests damage the foliage, fruits and wood. The beetles of Amblyrrhinus poricollis , Myllocerus discolar and the larvae of Papilio demoleus demoleus Linn defoliate the trees. A number of insect pests of Coccidae family feed on the sap. The insect pests of the fruit are Chaetodacus zonatus, Argyroploce illepida , Euzophera niveicostella and E. phimbeifasciella. The seedlings are browsed by cattle. Porcupines also do considerable damage to the seed1ings (3).


Diseases:

Bacterial shot hole, fruit canker and gummosis are the serious diseases. Fruit cracking is the physiological disorder in some genotypes of Bael which occurs just before ripening (3). Stenella aegles causes leaf spots.

Patel et al, (1953) reported that bacterial shot-hole and fruit canker of bael is caused by Xanthomonas bilvae. The symptoms on the leaves are: characterized by round, water soaked spots (0.5 mm) surrounded by a clear halo. Gradually, the spots increase in size (3 mm to 5 mm) and form brown lesions with saucer like depressions in the centre surrounded by oily, raised margin. The preliminary localised lesions allover the leaf are always followed by falling-out of the dead tissues leaving circular or slightly irregular perforation: or shot-holes. The pathogens also infect the fruit, twigs and thorns (10).

Economic Potential and Uses

A bitter, light-yellow oil (11.9%) extracted from the seeds is given in 1.5 g doses as a purgative. The limonene-rich oil has been distilled from the rind for scenting hair oil. Sometimes the glue from the seeds is used as a soap-substitute. Seed and leaves has the insecticidal properties (2).

Uses

The fruit pulp has detergent action and has been used for washing clothes. fruit is employed to eliminate scum in vinegar-making. The gum enveloping the seeds is most abundant in wild fruits and especially when they are unripe. It is commonly used as household glue and is employed as an adhesive by jewelers. It is mixed with lime plaster for waterproofing wells and is added to cement when building walls. Artists add it to their watercolors, and it may be applied as a protective coating on paintings. The shell of hard fruits has been fashioned into pill and snuff boxes, sometimes decorated with gold and silver. The rind of the unripe fruit is employed in tanning and also yields a yellow dye for calico and silk fabrics.

Leaves: In the Hindu culture, the leaves are indispensable offerings to the 'Lord Shiva'. The leaves and twigs are lopped for fodder.

Flowers: A cologne is obtained by distillation from the flowers.

Wood: The wood is strongly aromatic when freshly cut. It is gray-white, hard, but not durable, has been used for carts and construction, though it is inclined to warp and crack during curing. It is best utilized for carving, small-scale turnery, tool and knife handles, pestles and combs, taking a fine polish (2).

Harvesting Practices
Normally, the fruit is harvested when yellowish-green and kept for 8 days while it loses its green tint. Then the stem readily separates from the fruit. The fruits can be harvested in January (2 to 3 months before full maturity) and ripened artificially in 18 to 24 days by treatment with 1,000 to 1,500 ppm ethrel (2-chloroethane phosphonic acid) and storage at 86º F (30º C). Care is needed in harvesting and handling to avoid causing cracks in the rind. The fruit should be picked individually from the tree with a portion of fruiting stalk and should not be allowed to drop. Harvesting by shaking the tree is discouraged as the fruits are likely to develop cracks on impact because of the very brittle peel.

The number of fruits per tree may go up to 200 to 400 at the age of 10 to 15 years. However, a crop of 800 to 1,000 fruits on 40 to 50-year-old tree is not uncommon, but an average crop is 150 to 200.


Produce and Products

Fruit is used in vinegar making. Oil is extracted from the fruits. A popular drink is made out of it in India. Wood is used in small scale turnery. Leaves and bark has many medicinal properties.

Processing Techniques

Ripe fruits are collected from the trees in May-June. These are broken open to take out the pulp containing the seeds. The pulp is washed to extract seeds which are dreid in shade. Oil can be extracted by pressing softer oilseeds and nuts. Pulped or ground material is loaded into a manual or hydraulic press to squeeze out the oil-water emulsion. This is more efficient at removing oil than traditional hand squeezing.

Other Uses

Various parts of the tree are used for its curative, pesticidal and nutritive properties. Fresh half ripe Bael fruit is mildly astringent and used to cure dysentery, diarrhoea, hepatitis, tuberculosis, dyspepsia and good for heart and brain. Roots have antidiarrhoetic, antidote to snake venom, anti-inflammatory and wound healing properties. The Bael fruit is one of the most nutritious fruits, rich in riboflavin and used for the preparation of a number of products like candy, squash, toffee, slab, pulp powder and nectar (4).

The bitter, pungent leaf juice, mixed with honey, is given to allay catarrh and fever. With black pepper added, it is taken to relieve jaundice and constipation accompanied by edema. The leaf decoction is said to alleviate asthma. A decoction of the flowers is used as eye lotion and given as an anthelmetic. The bark decoction is administered in cases of malaria. Decoctions of the root are taken to relieve palpitations of the heart, indigestion, and bowel inflammations; also to overcome vomiting. The fruit, roots and leaves have antibiotic activity.

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

The importance of bael seems largely due to its medicinal properties. All parts of this tree, viz., root, leaf, trunk, fruit and seeds are used for curing one human ailment or another. The fruits of Aegle marmelos contain a furocoumarin marmalosin, which is responsible for its medicinal properties. The bark contains umbelliferone and other coumarins. Considering all these properties of this wild fruit-tree, there should be a large scope for exploiting it for commercial cultivation in the hills (6).

Miscellaneous Information

There is no systematic work on the breeding and varietal improvement on bael. However, bael provides an excellent scope for improvement of fruit quality by breeding as, in India, different strains are available. If the cultivation of bael is intensified after selecting an ideal variety, this fruit might emerge as a potential fruit for the processing industry.
