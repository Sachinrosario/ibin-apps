Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
A  thorny deciduous shrub or small tree growing to 9m, native to northern Mediterranean coast, introduced and naturalized throughout the tropics. In India, it occurs often gregariously in loose sandy soils of riverbeds throughout the country; it often cultivated in gardens. It can fix nitrogen.     

It's often inadvertent introduction and subsequent naturalization is due to its flexible ecological requirements and high seed germination rate which makes it able to colonize disturbed land left open by the elimination of native vegetation. It is also one of the few species which can persist along highly saline artesian watering drains for sheep and cattle, where it also provides valuable shade.

The plant prefers light (sandy), medium (loamy) and heavy (clay) soils,  and can grow in nutritionally poor soil. The plant prefers acidic, neutral and basic (alkaline) soils and can grow in very acidic, very alkaline and saline soils. It cannot grow in the shade. It requires dry or moist soil and can tolerate drought.

Requires a dry tropical climate. Ranging from warm temperate dry through tropical desert to moist forest life zones, cassie is reported to tolerate annual precipitation of 6.4–40.3 dm, annual mean temperature of 14.7–27.8°C, and pH of 5.0–8.0.

Availability : 

A. farnesiana originates from North and Central American tropics and subtropics : South USA, North Mexico where it is known as "Huisache". It was introduced to Europe in the 17th century, hence spread and became naturalized in many of the world's subtropical and tropical arid lands : North Africa, Sahel, East Africa, Arabian Peninsula, Pakistan, India, Australia. Now common throughout the tropics. Formerly a garden plant. Common in Viti Levu (Fiji), and northern Australia, southern Australia, southern France and Africa.

Both A. farnesiana and its var cavenia are extensively cultivated in and around Cannes, southern France, which is the center for production of the perfume. The variety seems to be more resistant to drought and frost. Assigned to the South American Center of Diversity, cassie or cvs thereof is reported to exhibit tolerance to drought, high pH, heat, low pH, salt, sand, slope, and Savanna. 

It is cosmopolitan in the tropics and found in the greater part of Indian subcontinent, sometimes growing gregariously in the loose sandy soils of river beds in northern India. Found from North Gujarat to Eastern India ( East Andhra , Mysore & Eastern slopes) and in Western Ghats. Live trees make a good fence. In Tamil Nadu, it is distributed mostly in Chengalpattu, Coimbatore, Dharmapuri, Ramanathapuram, Tiruchirapalli and Tirunelveli districts.


Status : Not threatened.
Naturalized  throughout India and also  planted in gardens. Trees often planted as an ornamental. It is recommended for arid and semi-arid, saline and alkaline soils.  It makes good fence.  It is an important nitrogen fixing plant.  Also used as fuel wood. 

A. farnesiana makes good defensive hedges, it is sometimes planted for its flowers that provide a fragrant essential oil used in the perfume industry as a violet scent substitute.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Tree up to 8m tall, Branches warty, slender often zig-zag covered with stipular spines which are unequal, 2-2.5 cm long and straight. Leaves 4-5 cm long ,  in clusters, main rachis slightly pubescent, glandular, petiole 0.5-0.7 cm, usually furnished with a minute gland  about the middle; leaves bipinnate with stipular spines.  Pinnae 4-7 pairs, 1-2 cm long; leaflets 13-17 pairs, elliptic, overlapping, glabrous, base truncate, margin entire, apex obtuse, nerves prominent on lower side.  Flowers in globose heads, creamy white, fragrant, peduncle 0.75-1.0 mm long, pubescent, calyx 5-toothed up to 1.3mm long , teeth short, acute, triangular; petal 5, long, lobes very short, pubescent,  stamens numerous, free, filaments up to 4mm, anthers small, basically connate, ovary stipitate, style filiform, pods 5-8cm long, dull brown, sub-cylindrical, obtuse or hooked at apex, dehiscent, slightly curved, glabrous, pulpy, turgid, brown.
Chromosome no(2n = 52, 104).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Breeding System :
The scented flowers are hermaphrodite (have both male and female organs). 


Phenology :
Tree begins to flower when it attains the age of 3-4 years.

Flowering season is somewhat irregular, but lasts chiefly from November to March; the pods are still green by May.  They commence ripening about July.  The leaves commence falling in November-December and the plants are leafless or nearly so in January-February, the new leaves appearing in February-March.

Seed Biology :
The fruit is a green pod, turning black or dark brown at maturity, thick, indehiscent, cylindrical, 4-8 cm long and 0.8-1.5 cm in diameter, each contains 12 to 14 seeds. Seeds transversally set in the pods, ovoid, compressed, 6-7 x 4-5 mm, coated with a characteristic whitish medulla. Life span 10-50 years.

The seeds often germinate within the pod, a dense clump of seedlings resulting germination taking place during the rains, but many seeds remain on or in the ground for a whole year, germinating in the second season during rains.

Seedling Germination :

Propagation is by natural regeneration and raising of seedlings.
Seeds - best sown as soon as it is ripe in a sunny area in a warm greenhouse. Stored seed should be scarified, pre-soaked for 12 hours in warm water and then sown in a warm greenhouse in March. The seed germinates in 3 - 4 weeks at 25°C. As soon as the seedlings are large enough to handle, transplant them  into individual pots and grow them in a sunny area in the greenhouse for their first winter. Plant them out in late spring or early summer, after the last expected frosts, and consider giving them some protection from the cold for their first winter outdoors.

Cuttings of half-ripe wood with a heel should be planted in July/August in individual pots in a frame. Over winter in a greenhouse and for the first winter plant out in their permanent positions, in late spring or early summer. Fair percentage of plants will establish.

Cultivation:
Prefers a light sandy loam and a very sunny area sheltered from strong winds. Plants can grow well in pure sand. Most species in this genus become chlorotic on limey soils. Established plants are very drought tolerant. The species and its cultivars are reported to exhibit tolerance to drought, high pH, heat, low pH, salt, sand, slope, and Savannah. Plants tolerate a pH range from 5.0 to 8.0.

Whilst this species is not very tolerant to cold, being damaged by even a few degrees of frost, the variety A. farnesiana cavenia seems to be more resistant to both drought and frost.

This species has a symbiotic relationship with certain soil bacteria, these bacteria form nodules on the roots and fix atmospheric nitrogen. Some of this nitrogen is utilized by the growing plant but some can also be used by other plants growing nearby.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests: Trees are infested by tamarind bruchid beetle Pachymeres gonagra Fabr. The grub borer infect  the seeds and feeds on the cotyledons.

Diseases:  

Fungi reported on this plant include: Camptomeris albizziae, Clitocybe tabescens, Hypocrea borneensis, Lenzites palisoti, L. repanda, Phyllachora acaciae, Phymatotrichum omnivorum, Polystictus flavus, Ravenelia austris, R. hieronymi, R. siliquae, R. spegazziniana, Schizophyllum commune, Systingophora hieronymi, Tryblidiella rufula, and Uromycladium notabile. 

It may also be parasitized by the flowering plants Dendrophthoe falcata and Santalum album.

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Flowers, ripe seeds, tender leaves, bark, wood

Uses

  • A mucilage can be manufactured from the gummy sap. A gum exuding from trunk is considered to be superior to gum arabic in arts.  Gummy substance obtained from pods is used in Java as cement for broken crockery. This gum is marketed with other Acacia gums. Used as substitute in confectionery. The gum is also used in tonic and as aphrodisiac. A decoction of the gum from the trunk has been used in the treatment of diarrhoea.
  • The bark and the fruit are a source of tannin and used in making dyes and inks. Bark also used for tanning and dying leather in combination with iron ores and salts.
  • Pods contain 23 percent tannin, a glucoside of ellagic acid, and are used for tanning leather. 
  • In Bengal and West Indies, pods are used to obtain  a black leather dye. 
  • An essential oil called Cassie is distilled from the flowers. Cassie absolute is employed in preparation of violet bouquets and is extensively used in European perfumery.

Chemistry

Acacia farnesiana L. contains anisaldehyde, benzoic acid, benzyl alcohol, butyric acid, coumarin, cresol, cuminaldehyde, decyl aldehyde, eicosane, eugenol, farnesol, geraniol, hydroxyacetophenone, methyleugenol, methyl salicyclate, nerolidol, palmitic acid, salicylic acid, and terpineol.

Processing Techniques
Cassie perfume is extracted by macerating the fragrant flowers and placing them in oil for several hours, the flowers used for extraction are replaced with  fresh  flowers  and the process  repeated till the oil becomes saturated with the perfume. From this saturated oil, the  perfume is strained and cooled in the form of a concentrate or pomade with an odour like that of violet, but more intense. The pomade is then ad mixtured with alcohol and distilled. The “absolute”  is obtained as a fine olive-green of cassie flowers.

An inferior quality oil is obtained by  direct distillation or solvent extraction of flowers.

Other Uses  

  1. Bark: Used for tanning purposes, it is acrid, astringent , alexiteric, anthelmintic and antidysenteric. Bark is also used in the form of a decoction for treating polyuria.  The bark is astringent and demulcent. Along with the leaves and roots it is used for medicinal purposes. Colombians bathe in the bark decoction as a treatment for typhoid.
  2.  Flowers: flowers that provide a fragrant essential oil used in the perfume industry as a violet scent substitute ie., source of much valued cassie perfume.  An infusion of the flowers has been used as a stomachic. It is also used in the treatment of dyspepsia and neuroses. The flowers are added to ointment, which is rubbed on the forehead to treat headaches.
  3. In Ayurvedic practice, the bark and heartwood are used as a substitutes  for those of Acacia catechu in the treatment of leprosy, pruritis, wounds, bronchial asthma and stomatitis.
  4.  In suitable climates the plant is grown as a hedge. The trees have also been used for erosion control in sandy soils.
  5. Pods are eaten by stock before maturity, at maturity they become woody, hard, unpalatable.  The green pods have been decocted and used in the treatment of dysentery and inflammations of the skin and mucous membranes. An infusion of the pod has been used in the treatment of sore throats, diarrhoea, leucorrhoea, conjunctivitis, and uterorrhagia.
  6. Seeds: yield an oil with fatty acids.
  7. Tenders leaves: prescribed  for gonorrhoea. Substitute for tamarind in chutneys. The powdered dried leaves have been applied externally as a treatment for wounds.
  8. The gummy roots have been chewed as a treatment for sore throat.
  9. The plant is one of the ingredients in Sushruta’s "Ksharaganda", a preparation for the treatment of snakebite.
  10. Wood: Fine textured heavy wood, amenable to a nice polish, durable in the soil, close-grained. Used for fencing posts, agricultural implements, pegs, woodenware, tent pegs, and sheep knees, etc.  Locally employed for making cartwheels, framework over wells, posts, beams, minor types of railway work, and brake blocks etc.
