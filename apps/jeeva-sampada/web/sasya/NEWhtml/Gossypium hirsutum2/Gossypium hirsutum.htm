HABIT & HABITAT

Upland cotton is an annual or perennial shrub. It is a native of Mexico and Central America. It is cultivated in US, Turkestan, South Brazil, Uganda, South and West Africa, Iraq, Parts of China, Turkey, Greece, Mancuria, India, Pakistan and Australia.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

The plant is 3-15 ft. high, Stems usually green, brown or reddish brown. Leaves large as small, cordate ½-2/3 cut into 3-5 lobes, lobes broad, triangular o ovate-acuminate with sinuses open or slightly overlapping. Bractioles longer than broad, cordate, cut into 7-12 acuminate teeth.

Capsules variable in size, rounded, 3-5 locular, with a few inconspicuous oil glands. Seeds bear copious lint hairs and usually, also fuzz hairs. Lints variable in quality, white, brown or rust coloured.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

About six or eight flower buds are produced on each fruit limb, one arising at each joint. Usually large plant may have many more flowers. The buds appear at first as small, pyramidal green structures called squares. Flowers open about 21 days after the squares are first recognizable. A flower bud tat is to open the following day may be recognized by its enlarged but well twisted corolla; flowers used in hybridizing work are emasculated at this stage .

Flowers are complete, perfect, protoandrous, solitary, axillary or in an elongated inflorescence, pale yellow with or without purploish blotches to purple red with a darker center. Flowers are surrounded by an involucre of large, leafy bracts (epicalyx) that are generally persistent. The corolla is 2-3 times as long as the involucre . Inside the corolla and attached to it at the base is a whorl of numerous stamens. The filament are united below to form a tube which surrounds the pistil. The staminal tube shows ten ridges. The pistil consists of a small conical ovary, a slender, thread-like style, and a stigma. The vary is superior. The stigma appears to be simply the outer end of the style slightly enlarged, grooved or lobed, and covered with a stigmatic surface, which is receptive.

There are four sets of nectarines, one inside the flower and tree extra floral. The floral nectarines form a ring about 1/16 inch wide on the inside of the calyx near its base. The secretary part of the nectary consists of numerous pluriseptate glandular hairs, or papillae closely aggregated.

The nectarines secret a sweetish liquid, which attracts bees, butter flies and certain other insects.

Bulging of pollen tube is common in G. hirsutum. The interval between pollination and fertilization in 24-48 hours. The primary endosperm nucleus divides 24 hours after pollination. About 2000 free nuclei are produced on the 7th/8th day after pollination


Breeding system and varietal improvement:
Flowers are usually self-fertilized. Fertilization with pollen from other plants may range from less than 5% to as much as 50% depending on the activities of insects. There probably is little crossing, aside from that by insects. Insects such as bees and butterflies are attracted by nectarines, floral and extra floral both. Bees visit extra floral nectarines, avoid the floral ones. Self incompatible clones have also been observed.

MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
The Mexican boll weevil (Anthomus grandis Boh.) and the pink bollworm (Pectinophora gossypiella Saund) are considered the most destructive pests. The adult female Mexican boll weevil bores into flower bud and lay her eggs; the resulting larvae attack and complete the resin of the boll. The adult female pink follwarm lays her eggs in flowers and on the green bolls; when the larvae emerge, they bore into the bolls and attack the seeds. Preventive measures against these pests include destruction of aged cotton plants and infested bolls, planting as early in the season as possible, and the use of early maturing varieties. The best direct protection against boll-weevil infestations is to spray the plants with calcium arsenate or one of the newer organic insecticides such as aldrin or dieldrin; disinfection of the seeds before planting is recommended for avoiding the pink bollworm.

Other pests include, the cotton leaf worm or cotton caterpillar (Alabama argilacea Huba) attacks leaves, flowers, young bolls, and other tender parts of plant; Southern grassworm (Laphygma frugiperda S. and A.) grow on stems and eat the growing tips of cotton plants; the cotton Colluworm (Chloridea obsoletea Hub) destroys cotton squares and bolls; cotton square-bores (Uranotes melinus Hubn); Cotton red spider (Tetianychus telarius L.); Cotton Aphis, or cotton louse (Aphis gossypii Glover) on leaves; Cotton stainer (Dyducus suturellus H. Schf) damages bolls and squares; cotton wire worms (Monocrepidius vesperinus Fabricius)feeds on roots; cotton Flea Hopper (Psallus seriatus Rent); Tarnished plant bug (Lygus pratensis L.) punctures young squares; Rapid plant bug (Adelphocoris rapidus Say); Thrips; cotton root Aphis (Aphis maidiradicis Forbes) found on the taproot of cotton.



Diseases:

Fusarium Wilt is caused by Fusarium oxysporum f. vasinfectum (Ath.) Snyder and Hansen (F. vasinfectum Atk.). It attacks the older plants and can be controlled by using resistant varieties and burning of diseased plants.

Verticillium Wilt caused by Vertcillium albo-atrum Reinke and Berth is not so prevalent or harmful. No control measures are known. Root knot of cotton is caused by a nematode Heterodera radcicola Greef and Muell which enters the roots. Various chemicals, such as sulphur, formaldeyde, carbon bisulphide, calcium carbide, potassium sulphocarbonate and ammonium sulphate, have been applied to the soil in the effort to eradicate nematodes. Chemical fertilizers that will stimulate plants to increased growth and vigor may be helpful as control measures. Cotton Anthrachose is another disease caused by Colletotrichum gossypii South also called pink boll rot is widespread in most of the cotton rowing regions of the world. The annual loss due to this disease amounts to million of dollars. Planting only seed from disease free fields, the use of crop rotations and fall plowing have proved to be effective in controlling this disease. Other methods are sterilizing seed by the use of conc. H2SO4, HgCl2, soaking seed in hot water for a few minutes, spraying Bordeaux mixture.

Other diseases are Texas Root Rot cased by Phymatotrichum omnivarum (Shear) Duggar; Diplodia boll Rot by Dipodia gossypina Cooke; Smut on leaves by Doassansia gossypii Lag; Red leaf blight due to poor soil; True rust caused by Kuehnuela gossypii (Lag), Crown gall by Pseudomonas tumeficiens; Crazy top by Acromania causes abnormal branching and fruiting of the plants; Cluster cup rust by Accidium gossypii; Areolate mildew on leaves by Septocylindrium areoles (Atk)

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
fiber

Cotton fiber is used in making articles of clothings which include shirtings, outerwear, underwear, gloves, hosiery and handkerchiefs. Household articles include bed sheets and covers, pillow cases, towels, table cloths, mosquito netting, flannel, blankets, tapestry fabric etc. Among the industrial articles, we have a still large umber of items such as bags, belli, belting, industrial thread, awnings, tents, tarpaulins, industrial hoses, felts, insulation, cellulose etc. The linter is used for sliffing cushions, pillows, mattresses etc.

PULP

Stalks of cotton plant yields tough fiber used in making rough paper. The hill of cotton seed also used in making paper and fiber board.

FEED

Leaves are sued as fodder. Oil seed cake is used as stock feed and hull is used as low grade roughage.

MEDICINAL

The bark of the stem and of the roots possess medicinal properties as a substitute for Ergot.

OIL

The Kernel or meat yields oil which is used in making cooking oil, salad oil. It also yields low or inferior oil for soap making. The refined oil is also largely converted by deodorizing and hydrogenation into solid vegetable fats and finds use in this form in the preparation of margarine.

OTHERS

Oil cake is also used as fertilizer. Stalks yield pyroligneous acid .

Miscellaneous Information

 Ten intra-hirsutum cotton hybrids were obtained by crossing nine naturally colour linted germplasm of hirsutum. The hybrids on evaluation using two chcks showed that three coloured lint hybrids excelled Savitha (check in Kapas yield by 8.5 to 34.4 percent, while six coloured lint hybrids excelled RCH-2 by 9.4 to 82.8 percent. Three hybrids were superior to both checks in yield. All hybrids were moderately resistant / tolerant to jassid and bollworms, except one green linted hybrid which showed moderate susceptibility to jassd. The boll weights in six hybrids were comparable to both checks .

A high yielding and better quality varieties of upland cotton resistant to bacterial light (Xanthomonas campestris bv. Malava cearum) has been developed.

Dr. K.C. Mandloi in Madhya Pradesh has produced improved hirsutum variety with natural shining almond brown colour lint.

The higher yield potential from hirsutum varieties and in recent years from intra hirsutum hybrid cottons, combined with a fiber quality that commanded a price premium in market over arboreum varieties has resulted in large scale replacement of arboreum cottons in rainfed areas.
