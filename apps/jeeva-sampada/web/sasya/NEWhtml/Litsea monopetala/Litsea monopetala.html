DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:
Large shrub or tree, 5-15 cm tall. Leaves spirally arranged, oval to elliptic, 5-17 x 2.5-9 cm, glabrous above and hairy beneath, lateral veins 7-15 pairs. Flowers arranged in 5-8 flowered fascicles or short racemes.
REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:
Flower covered with involucres of bracts. Berry globose, 6-8 mm in diam., purple during ripening.

Economic Potential and Uses
UTILIZATION:
Bark is useful in astringent, ulcer and diarrhoea. Fat obtained from seeds are used as ointment for rheumatism.


Chemistry:
b-sitosterol and actinodaphnine from bark. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1960-1969).

Polysaccharide isolated from leaves, contained xylose and arabinose in molar ratio of 1:4. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

An arabinoxylan consisting of xylose and arabinose in a molar ratio of 1:2, isolated from leaves. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989).

The major components of the flower oil were a-caryophyllene alc. (13.9%), pentacosane (11.4%), caryophyllene oxide (9.5%), humulene oxide (9.5%) and tricosane (8.1%).  The more volatile fruit oil consisted mainly of decanal (26.7%), nonanol (16.8%) and capric acid (15.5%).  The bark oil was mainly composed of aldehydes, alcs. and acids, of which tetradecanal (30.2%), tridecanol (11.3%), myristic acid (10.5%) and tridecanal (9.4%) predominated.( Choundhury et al, 1997)


Pharmacology:


Ethnobotany:
Medicine for astringent (Bark, Sudhakar and Rolla, 1985); Boils (Leaf, Harja and Baishya, 1981); Rheumatism (Fruit, Sudhakar and Rolla, 1985); Veterinary sores, ulcers (Bark + Leaf, Saxena et al, 1981); Edible as vegetable (Leaf, Harja and Baishya, 1981).
