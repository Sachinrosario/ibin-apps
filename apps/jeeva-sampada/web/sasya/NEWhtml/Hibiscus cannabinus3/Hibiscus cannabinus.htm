Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Kenaf is an erect, herbaceous annual with a straight slender, glabrous or prickly stem. It is a native of tropical and subtropical Africa, has been grown for centuries in Egypt and India as fiber plant. At present, India, Thailand, Brazil, China, the US, Cuba, Mexico, South Africa and the USSR are the major Kenaf producers . Kenaf grows well on an extremely wide rage of soils including acid peats, alluvial and colluvial loams, sandy loams, sandy clay loam and others.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

The stem of Kenaf is cylindrical, branched or unbranched, reaching a height of 1 to 4 mt, either entirely green, green with pinkish or reddish pigmentation or red.

Leaves entirely cardate and very shallowly lobed with serrated margins or 3-5-7 deeply palmate, alternate, stipulate. Stipules free lateral, pubescent 0.5 to 0.8 c long. Petiole 3.0 to 18.0 cm long, finely pubescent on the adaxial surface and bristled on the aboxial surface, green to red, scabrous.

Flowers are large, 3-4 mt. yellow crimson center. Capsules globose, pointed, bristly. Seeds large, brown, nearly glabrous.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Flowers are solitary, axillary, pedunculate, peduncle 0.2 to 0.4 cm, pentamerous, 8.0 to 10.0 cm in diameter when open, showy, epicalyx 7-9. Sepals are 5 in number, lanceolate, cannate below the middle into a cup, green, bristled and tormentosed with a large green gland on the back of each sepal. Petals 5 in number, large, spreading, bell shaped, very slightly united at the base, outerside pubescent. Stamens are numerous. Stigma 5 in number, hairy, capitate. Seeds are triangular, angles more or less acute. Hilum brown, relatively small.

Breeding system and varietal improvement:
Cross fertilization is very common when the various forms are grown together and allowed to flower freely. Howard and Howard stated that the opportunities for cross pollination by insects were very great from the time the flowers of this species opened in the early morning until they began closing about noon. The closing of the corolla is fairly riped and is accompanied by a twisting of the petal limbs which effects self pollination. Thus the Kenaf flowers are adapted both for cross and self pollination. Horst (1924) wrote that Kenaf normally was self-pollinated, although he did report a few variations were due to mutation or natural crosses.

There it has been many attempts to carry out interspecific crosses in order to develop Kenaf hybrids possessing better characteristics of stability, uniformity, desired variations of maturity to permit a wider range of harvest, higher fiber and seed yields, and most of all, high resistance to stem and rot diseases, particularly root-knot nematodes.

One of the combinations often attempted has been a Kenaf x Roselle cross to combine the cartiness and adaptability of the former with the high fiber yield and nematode resistance of the latter, although Kenaf is a diploid (2n=36) whereas Roselle is a tetraploid (2n=72).

Ustnova (1937, 1938) in the USSR reported successful interspecific crosses involving Kenaf (H. esculentus) and H. manihot, but did not go into details regarding progeny performance.


Phenology: Flowering occurs in October to November


Pollinators: Chief pollinator is honeybee (Apis mellifera t.) second pollinator is a wasp (Campsomeris trifasciata Fabr.) Others include bumble bee are bumble bee (Xylocopa cubacoda Lucas), wild bee (Exomalopsis similes (ress)). Occasionally wind also has very little importance in cross pollination of Kenaf.

Floral and seed biology:
Seedling germination :

The seedbed of Kenaf should be carefully prepared in advance of planting. The seeds are usually drilled in rows with a spacing of from 3.5-5 cm for fiber (and upto 15 to 20 cm for seed crops). The rows are spaced 15-18 cm for the fiber crop; 30-40 cm or more are used for the seed crop to encourage branching. The amount of seed required per hectare is 25-30 kg. The young plants grow so rapidly that cultivation may not be necessary in the crop grown for fiber.



MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:

The insect pests of Kenaf are numerous although these that have proven to be serious are relatively few in number. Podagrica sp. is considered a major pest of Kenaf.

Since Kenaf and cotton belong to the same family (Malvaceae), they both are attacked by many of the same insect species, several of which are extremely serious to the seed crop. These include the spiny bollworm (Earias sp.), the cotton bollworm (Heliothis sp.), pink bollworm (Pectinophora gossypiella Saund), and cotton stainer Dysdercus sp.). It is therefore, a good rule never to plant Kenaf near cotton.

Other serious pests of Kenaf are the European corn borer (Aphis gossypii Glover), and the leaf cutting ant (Atta insularis Guern) which has been a serious pest in Cuba, El Salvador and Brazil. The spiral borer (Agrilus acutus Thunb) is a serious pest of Kenaf in India. Potentially serious pests because of the difficulty in control are the stem borers such as Hippois lemniscata (Fab.) and Apion subangulirostre Wag.

Many of the insects attacking Kenaf are partially controlled by predators, particularly wasps and Hymenoptera, and by diseases and unfavourable weather. The stem four (Apion sbangulirostre) is parasitized upto 37.5% durin August in the Central African epulic by various micro-Hymenoptera such as Elasmadie, a spcific parasite of Lepidopterous larva, and by Eulophidae. Nocturnal cutworms are parasitized by the branconid wasp andichrumoids. Many hemoptera prey upon the Anomis sp. The cotton aphid and white mealy bug are highly parasitized by the predatory lady bird beetles of the family Coccinellidae.



Diseases:

Anthraceae is the most widespread and destructive disease attacking Kenaf. It is caused by Colletotrichum hisbisci Polacci. The disease attacks young seedlings are damping off. The leaves or petioles may be attacked; with the development of lesions on the petiole, early defoliation occurs. Flowers, seed capsules and older stems issues are also susceptible to attack. Control measures for anthracnoseis use of resistant varieties or seed treatment with tetramehtylthiuran disulphide (thiran) at the rate of 125g/100 kg of seed which will materially reduce injury from attacks during the seedling stage in susceptible varieties.

Stem and seedling rot caused by Macrophomina phaseoli (Maubl) Ashby is also a serious disease of Kenaf, especially in Asia. In the United States the sclerotial stage of this fungus (Sclerotium bataticola Taub.) attacks oviposition and emergence holes caused by boring insects. Besides the use of resistant variety some degree of control for susceptible varieties may be obtained by seed treatment with 0.5% ethylmercury chloride.

Collar rot (Sclerotium rolfsii Sacc) is a major disease of Kenaf that usually attacks plants that are 0.5-1.0 m in height, causing the formation of deep-seated lesions on the stem at ground level. Control measures for collar rot include seed treatment with a fungicide, isolation of infected areas, deep plowing to inactivate the sclerotia, and crop rotation. No natural resistance to the disease has been found.

The leaf spot diseases (Cercospora hisbisci - cannabini Sawada = Cercospora cannabini Tracey and Earle) may be classified as major diseases of Kenaf based upon reports from India, Japan and Taiwan. The fungus attacks th crop during the flowering stage, resulting in complete defoliation of the plants, seed production is completely hampered by the attack of this fungus. Control measures include seed disinfection, early harvest or isolation of infected areas and crop rotation.

Leaf spot (Thanatephorus cucumeris (Frank) Donk) has been reported in a number of countries.

Damping-off (Rhizoctonia solani Kuehn, the imperfect stage of I. cucumeris, attacks young Kenaf seedlings at ground level, causing lodging and death. The only practical effective control of this disease is by the use of penta chloronitrbenzene (PCNB) to treat the soil before planting at the rate of 5 kg/ha 75% PCNB in 750 ltr. of water.

An unidentified species of Volutella seriously attacks Kenaf in India. The disease locates in the apical region of the stem, causing deformation or death of the infected plants.

Other diseases are Eyerot (Myrothecium roridum Tode ex. Fr.) which causes microbic spots on stem and internodes, powdery mildew (Leveilulla taurica (Lev.) Arn.) which cause partial or complete defoliation of infected plants, gray mold (Botrytis cinerea Pers.) that infects leaves, Fusarium sp. which causes damping off in young seedlings, carbon rot (Sclerotium bataticola Taub), a damping off disease by Alternaria sp.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
fiberS

It is a non-textile fiber. It is used for rope making and cordage. Also used for making fishing nets, strings for tying rafters, coarse canvas, sacks, gunny bags, floor matting, rug backing and chair backing.

PULP

The whole dry stems furnish a raw material for pulp. fiber of poor quality is used for making paper.

FOOD AND FEED

The leaves of Kenaf are used for food in a number of African countries. Most varieties contain a high percentage of crude protein and carotene and have a fairly high calcium content.

Seeds is also sometimes used for human consumption. The press cake of Kenaf resembles that of linseed or rapeseed and is highly palatable to cattle.

 

Parts Used:

Stem, seed, leaves.


Harvesting Practices:
Kenaf plants crop is harvested 3-5 months after sowing. For fiber, harvesting is done at flowering stage before and setting. Kenaf plants may be harvested by hand in the same manner as jule by using a knife with a curved blade or a machete, even if it is to be ribboned by hand or mechanically before retting. Usually a larger-estate operations the crop is hand-harvested, mostly on a contract basis. Sometimes the crop is pulled from the soil if the soil is sandy, and the root ends are either cut of later or go through te retting process. Removal of the entire root system from the land may actually be helpful to the soil by preventing the build up of harmful fungi on the decaying roots.

Kenaf also can be harvested with a reaper binder; however, these machines are no longer used in many countries, having been replaced by the combine for small grains. The crop can be cut with an ordinary moreover which, if equipped with a side delivery arrangement such as a reaper, does a fair job. But if this crop is cut with a n ordinary manner, the plants fall to the ground in an overlapping manner which makes separation and alignment (because of the leaves) even more difficult than hand harvest.

Processing Techniques:
The retting of Kenaf is carried out primarily by aerobic bacterial organisms. The organisms that retted Kenaf rapidly were identified as Athrobacter species, two Pseudomonas species, Aerobacter species, Bacillus vulgatus and Bacillus cereus. Rets made with the pure cultures of the above species were much faster than uninoculated rets, and once a good retting population is built up, the retting liquor will serve as a culture and may be used to inoculate new rets.

The retting of Kenaf stems or ribbons is carried in water retting in the same manner as jute and other lignified stem fibers which do not dew ret properly.

It is highly important that the leaves of Kenaf be removed by shocking or piling the bundled plants for several days in the field after harvest to promote defolation by abscission, thereby adding valuable nutrients to the soil. In addition, the delay in retting greatly facilitates the retting operation, and the removal of leaves produces a cleaner retted fiber.

Most stem retting is carried out in pods, canals, slow moving streams or ponds at a shallow depth of 2m or less so that the water temperature will be uniform. The uniformity of retting temperatureis a highly important factor in producing a uniformly retted fiber. Other important factors include bundle size, which should not exceed 20 cm in diameter, removal of the tip ends before retting since the tips contain immature fiber that tends to overret and produce hard ends, and total submersion of the bundles during the retting period. Stem grading into small, medium and large sized stems which are retted separately also will produce a higher and more uniform grade of retted fiber. River retting is not recommended unless the water is shallow and water movement slow, since heavy rains often may muddy the stream and fast currents will move the bundles downstream.

Judging the completion of the retting process calls for experience and prompt action of the retter to strip the fiber from the stems before it overrets, since overretted fire has poor strength and little value.

Retted bundles are removed and the bark peeled off from the root upwards. Strips are gently beaten with a mallet, rinsed in water to separate the fiber. Clean fiber is washed, dried in the sun and made into bundles.


Other uses:

Kenaf is used on a large scale by the jute mills, which use as 40 to 60 percent of the fiber in admixture with jute, for hessian and sacks.

Stalks can be used as fuel, dried stalks for match splinters.

Seed oil is used as a lubricant, for lighting, suitable for soaps, manufacture of linoleum, paints, varnishes. Refined oil is edible, used as salad oil and for cooking.
