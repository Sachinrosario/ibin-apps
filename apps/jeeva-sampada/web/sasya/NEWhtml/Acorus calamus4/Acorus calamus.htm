Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

A perennial herb having creeping stems, semi–aquatic, thrives best in marshy and moist areas (2). National populations occur in the marshy tracts of Kashmir and Himachal Pradesh and in Manipur, Nagaland, and Western Ghats. 

Climate and soil
Acorus grows well in a semi-aquatic environment, especially marshy areas and clayey loam soils.

Availability :

Distributed in marshy areas and plains of the country. Occur from plains to altitudes of about 2000 m or more, also in marshes, wild and cultivated throughout India (2). Acorus is also cultivated in certain areas in Karnataka (Tumkur Dt) (1).

Conservation/ Threat Status : Wild and cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Acorus is a semi-aquatic rhizomatous herbaceous perennial, much branched, as thick as the middle finger, rhizomes grow horizontally, somewhat vertically compressed and spongy within 1-2 cm thick, light brown or pinkish brown externally, white and carries a tuft of grass-like or sword-like leaves. Leaves are bright green, long, glabrous, simple, alternate, distichous, closely arranged, linear to narrowly ensiform, acute at tip, amplexicaul at base, thickened at the middle, middle margins wavy and possesses aromatic taste (1.)

CUTLIVATION ASPECT:

Related taxa :
Related species – A. gramineus; Soland, a Japanese species, but occur sporadically in Eastern Himalayan region at about 2000 m altitudes. The taxonomic position of Acorus in problematic. Some prefer to include this under a separate family – Acoraceae and some in Araceae.

Selection of planting material:
Top portion of the shoot consisting of a portion of rhizome along with the tuft of leaves is used. Rhizome bits can also be used for propagation.

Planting:
For cultivation, field is ploughed and stem tops with leaves are planted, leaves are kept above the soil. 

Management after planting:
Addition of organics is required for better growth. Copious watering, or even flooding the field occasionally are required for luxuriant growth.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Mostly used in medicines. Powdered rhizome is used as insecticide against fleas, bedbugs, moths, lice etc. oil is used in the preparation of aromatic cordials and liquors, for flavouring beers and also in perfumes and dental preparations (2). 

Parts Used: Dried rhizomes.


Harvesting Practices: Rhizomes are harvested after one year. 

Post Harvest Handling
Rhizomes are dug out, cleaned thoroughly, cut into pieces and dried in the sun. After drying the rhizome pieces are rubbed on concrete slabs or flooring to remove the adhering scale leaves and roots. 

Produce and Products: Dried rhizomes and calamus oil.
Calamus oil is viscous liquid of a warm, woody-spicy and pleasant odour with an increasing sweetness. It is obtained from fresh rhizome, dried rhizomes and fresh aerial parts.

Chemistry: The rhizome (and also roots and leaves) yields a brown to brownish yellow volatile oil, called calamus oil. This has warm, spicy and pleasant odour and is sweet to taste. Dried rhizome contains 1.5-4% oil and leaves 0.2-0.3% oil. The oil content is highest in the product from Jammu-Kashmir (about 3.1-3.5 %). Japanese population of Acorus is known to have the highest oil content (4.5-5 %). Major component of calamus oil in Asarone 80%. Other components are calamenol (5%), calamene (0.4%), calamenone (1%), methyl eugenol (1.0%), -pinene (0.2%), camphene (0.2%) and traces of palmitic, heptylic and butyric acids, asaronaldehyde, calamol, calamone and azulene. sesquiterpene ketones like acorone, calarene, calarene, calacone, calacorene, acorenone, acolamone, isoacolamone, epihyobunone, shyobunone, isoshyobunone and acoragermacrone, and alcohols like isocalamendiol and preisocalamendiol are present. Hydrocarbons present in the oil are elemene, caryophyllene, calamenene, cadalene and humulene (Guenther, VI, 109; Chem Abstr, 1961, 55, 18796; 1963, 58, 1497; 1976, 84, 40737; 1953, 47, 8704; Iguchi et al., Tetrahedron Lett, 1968, 5315).

Choline (0.26%) and acoric acid have been isolated from roots. A bitter aromatic principle, acorin and a flavone dyglycoside, luteolin 6,8-C-diglucoside have been separated from the plant. tannins, mucilage and calcium oxalate are also reported (Chem Abstr. 1966, 65, 14091; 1974, 80, 121271; Birch et al., J. Chem. Soc., 1964, 2923) (2).

Other uses: Medicinal uses
In India, Acorus is most commonly used in indigenous medicine. Administering a paste of rhizome in honey to new born babies is the first post-natal ritual in most Indian homes. This might have developed from the age-old belief that Acorus contributes to proper development of mind and is capable of warding off evil spirits. Acorus rhizome is an ingredient in many ayurvedic drugs. It is found to be effective in the treatment of bronchial asthma and nutritive tonic for invalids and also in the treatment of gastritis, dysentery, constipation and general debility. Asarone is a mild sedative and a potent tranquilizer. 

Rhizome is acrid, bitter, thermogenic, aromatic, intellect promoting, emetic, laxative, carminative, stomachic, anthelmintic, emmenagogue, diuretic, alexeteric, expectorant, anodyne, antispasmodic, aphrodisiac, anticonvulsant, resuscitative, anti-inflammatory, sudorific, antipyretic, sialagogue, insecticidal, tranquilising, nervine tonic, sedative and tonic. It is useful in the vitiated conditions of vāta and kapha, stomatopathy, hoarseness, colic, flatulence, dyspepsia, helminthiasis, amenorrhoea, dysmenorrhoea, nephropathy, calculi, strangury, cough, bronchitis, odontalgia, pectoralgia, hepatodynia, otalgia, inflammations, gout, epilepsy, delirium, amentia, convulsions, depression and other mental disorders, tumors, dysentery, hyperdipsia, haemorrhoids, intermittent fevers, skin diseases, numbness and general debility(1.)
