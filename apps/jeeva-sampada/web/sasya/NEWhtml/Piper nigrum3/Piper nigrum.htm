HABIT & HABITAT

 

Black pepper, King of Spices, is the most important and most widely used spice in the world. The black pepper of commerce is the dried, mature fruits (commonly called berries) of the tropical, perennial woody climbing plant, P. nigrum L. More than 1000 species are included in the genus Piper, of which 110 are of Indian origin (Ravindran and Nirmal Babu, 1994).

Climate and soil
Pepper requires a warm and humid climate, with a temperature range of 10-39˚C and thrives in places where the annual rainfall is well over 200 cm and not less than 125 cm (2).  

Naturally pepper grows best on virgin, well-drained, red laterite or alluvial soils rich in humus and other plant nutrients. Soils 1.2 to 1.5 m in depth with granite rock underneath, and intermixed with boulders are considered best for pepper. 

Availability :

Pepper is distributed extensively in the moist evergreen forests and to a lesser extent in semi-evergreen and moist-deciduous forests of Western Ghats, which is the centre of origin and diversity for black pepper of South India, growing from almost sea level to an elevation of around 1500 m (2). Black pepper is grown commercially in the South Western regions of India, comprising of the states of Kerala, parts of Karnataka, Tamil Nadu (mainly); also in Goa, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Assam, Tripura, Meghalaya, Arunachal Pradesh, West Bengal, Orissa and Andaman and Nicobar Islands. Currently pepper is grown in twenty six countries.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES: Perennial climber, climbing by means of ivy-like roots which adheres to the support tree. Vigorous vine, old stem thick and rough, runner shoots arise from the base. Leaves thick, coriaceous, glabrous, commonly ovate, elliptic or elliptic-lanceolate; size varies from small to large, base round, acute or cordate, tip acuminate; lateral ribs 2 or 3 pairs, prominent, the upper most one 1-2.5 cm above the leaf base, upper surface dark green to light green, lower surface dull green; petioles short or long, grooved, leaf margins wavy or even (8).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:
                                                                                                                                                                                      

In Piper, male, female and hermaphrodite forms exist. The cultivated P. nigrum is monoecious having hermaphrodite flowers, while the wild ones are mostly dioecious. Pepper is predominantly self-pollinated and the pollen dispersal is aided by rain or dewdrops, and also by the gravitational descending of pollen (geitonogamy). Spikes filiform; pendulous, young ones green, whitish green, or purple; mature ones green or pale yellow. Peduncle glabrous, bracts oblong, decurrent, sessile with free upper margin, develop into a shallow cup in female spikes, rachis and bracts glabrous; stamens 2, anther dithecous, carpel single, ovary spherical, style 0, stigma 3-5 lobed, papillate (1).
Anther dehiscence: Anther dehiscence is controlled by temperature and relative humidity (Iljas 1960, Martin and Gregory 1962). Under Kerala conditions anther dehiscence takes place around 4 PM. 

Pollen production: The total amount of pollen per anther varies with the cultivar. Each spike of Indian cultivars yielded 500,000 – 700,000 pollen grains, each 10 μm in diameter (Marinet, 1953). 

Pollen grain: Pollen grains are small, spheroid to suboblate, size varies from 9.5-13.0 μm, monosulcate (Rahiman 1981). 

Fruit: The fruit is botanically a drupe (berry), single seeded, changes to red on ripening, seed mostly spherical, pungent.

The cultivated P. nigrum is monoecious having hermaphrodite flowers, while the wild ones are mostly dioecious. The cultivars vary from dominantly female to purely bisexual. High percent of bisexual flowers are essential for good fruit set. Pepper is predominantly self-pollinated.

Phenology:  In India, pepper plants start flowering during May-June with the onset of South West monsoon. In Indonesia and Malaysia flowering is in September-October. 

Pollinators: Natural pollinations are wind pollination, geitonogamy (self-pollination mechanism, which involves gravitational descending of pollen grains by action of rainwater or dewdrops) and apomixes. Insect activity is rare in pepper plants except for ants and certain other crawling insects and pollu beetle. Bees do visit male spikes of certain Piper species (1).  

Floral and seed biology: Seed germination: Pepper fruit takes 6-8 months for full maturity from flowering. Seed is recalcitrant and viability is retained only for about a week. Storing at 50C after removing seed coat prolongs viability. Germination is epigeal. If sown within a few days after harvesting, ripe seeds germinate in 20-25 days. Time required for completing germination is 50-77 days. 

Seedling development: Pepper seedlings developed from stem cuttings (normally), by tissue culture techniques and also from seed germination (1).

Selection of planting material: Runner shoots produced at the base of mother plants are selected and separated during February-March. The middle one-third portion of runner shoots is preferred for planting with 2-3 nodes in each piece; very tender and too hard portions of the shoots are to be avoided. Leaves are to be clipped off leaving a small portion of the petioles on the stem. Dipping the lower cut end (up to 2 cm) of the cuttings in 1000 ppm solution of 3 Indole Butyric Acid for 45 seconds will substantially increase root formation and development. Plant the treated cuttings (at least one node deep in the soil) in nursery beds or preferably in polythene bags or baskets filled with potting mixture (1 part of fertile topsoil, 1 part of river sand and 1 part of well rotten cattle manure). They are then kept under good shade and well protected from direct sunlight. Frequent watering is recommended in the nursery to maintain a humid and cool atmosphere around the cuttings. 


MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES: 
Pests
Black pepper is infested by 56 genera/species of insects in India. Pollu beetle (Longitarsus nigripennis Mots.), top shoot borer (Cydia hemidoxa Meyr.), leaf gall thrips (Liothrips karnyi Bagn.) and scale insects (Lepidosaphes piperis Green and Aspidiotus destructor Sign.) are considered as major pests. Information on insect pests of pepper in India is available mainly from Devasahayam et al. (1988) and Premkumar et al. (1994) (13).  
More on pests

Diseases
Fungi, bacteria, virus and mycoplasma, besides nutritional disorders affect black pepper. In India, Indonesia and Malaysia Phytophthora foot rot is the major disease (Sarma et al. 1992c, Holliday and Mowat 1963, Keuh and Sim 1992d, Manohara et al. 1992). Other diseases include slow decline (quick wilt and slow wilt), anthracnose, viral diseases (Sarma et al. 1991), stunted growth (Sitepu and Kasim 1991), wrinkled leaf disease (Kueh and Sim 1992b) etc. (12).
More on diseases

Nematode Induced Diseases
Pepper is infested by 30 genera and 54 species of nematodes (Ramana and Eapen 1998). In India, 17 genera of nematodes are recorded in Kerala and Karnataka (Sundararaju et al. 1980). Meloidogyne spp., Radopholus similis, Trophotylenchulus piperis, Helicotylenchus sp. and Rotylenchulus reniformis are predominant in India (Jacob and Kuriyan 1979b, 1980, Ramana and Mohandas 1987, 1989) (14).  

Major Insect Pests
Pollu beetle (Longitarsus nigripennis)
Most destructive pest of pepper in India. Its incidence is higher (20-30 percent) in the plains and areas below 300 MSL (North Kerala), and lower (5-10 percent) in areas above 900 MSL (South Kerala). Adult beetles feed on tender shoots, leaves and spikes, by scraping the tissues and feeding on them.
Symptoms: Black sunken patches develop. In severe infestations, the shoots, leaves and spikes rot and fall. However, the damage caused by grubs is more severe. They bore into tender berries and feed on internal contents. Infestation is more severe in shaded areas. 
Control: Spraying of insecticides namely Endosulphan, Dimethoate, Quinalphos or Monocrotophos, all at 0.05% concentration, at the time of spike emergence (June-July), at berry formation (September-October). It can also be controlled by spraying Cypermethrin (0.01%) twice, once at berry formation stage and the second 1 month after the first spray (September-October). Natural pests like Beauvaria bassiana Bals. (Vuill.) and B. brongniartii (Sacc.) Perch. can destroy the grubs. Leaf extracts of Chromolaena odorata L., neem (Azadirachta indica A. Juss.) and nux-vomica (Strychnos nux-vomica L.) and neem oil, neem seed kernel extract and custard apple (Annona squamosa L.) seed extract possessed appreciable antifeedant activity against pollu beetle (Devasahayam and Leela 1997). 

Top Shoot Borer (Cydia hemidoxa)
A serious pest in young plantations (up to 2-3 years old), widely distributed throughout Kerala in plains and high altitudes. Larvae infest tender, terminal orthotropic shoots especially those that adhere to the standards and leaves, resulting in decay and drying up of infested shoots. 
Control: Spraying Monocrotophos or Endosulphan (0.05%) twice a year during June and September has been suggested for controlling this pest (IISR 1990).
Leaf Gall Thrips (Liothrips karnyii)
Persistent pests of pepper, more serious on younger vines especially at high altitudes and in nurseries in the plains. Infestation is more serious in Wynad, Idukki, Thiruvananthapuram (Kerala), Shimoga, Kodagu (Karnataka) and Nilgiris (Tamil Nadu).

Symptoms: Initially infest tender leaves causing the leaf margins to curl downwards and inwards, resulting in the formation of marginal, tubular, hypophyllous galls. As the infested leaves grow they become crinkled, malformed and reduced in size; the leaf margins also turn chlorotic later. 

Control: Monocrotophos (0.05%), Dimethoate (0.06%) or Phosphamidon (0.03%) may be sprayed during the emergence of new flushes in June/July. 

Scale insects – Mussel Scale (Lepidosaphes piperis) and Coconut Scale (Aspidiotus destructor)
Scale insects are major pests in higher altitudes (above 600 MSL). Mussel scales encrust the main stems of younger vines, stems of lateral branches, mature leaves and berries. Coconut scales infest leaves and very rarely stems of lateral branches and berries.

Symptoms: The pest infestation results in chlorotic spots/patches, yellowing and drying of leaves subsequently. Lateral branches wilt and dry resulting in vacant spaces in the canopy. 
Control: For the control of Mussel scale spraying of Malathion, Dimethoate and Monocrotophos (0.1% each) are effective when sprayed twice at 30 days intervals during January/February (Devasahayam and Koa 1994a). Neemgold (a chemical formulation of neem) is also effective.

Minor Insect Pests
Grasshoppers - (Xenocatantops humilis (Serville) feeds on older leaves and leaf lamina causing irregular large holes of pepper vines. 

Leafhoppers – The cotton leaf hopper (Amrasca devastans Dist.) infests the foliage (Hill 1983).

Whiteflies – Pepper whitefly (Aleurocanthus piperis Mask.) Tobacco whitefly (Bemisia tabaci (Genn.) They infested very young leaves causing light yellowish discolouration on them; these spots turned brown after becoming large, under severe attack. Mealy whitefly (Aleurodicus disperses Russell) has also been observed (Ranjith et al. 1996).

Aphids - Colonies of black citrus aphid (Toxoptera aurantii B. de F.) infest tender shoot and leaves, both in field and nursery, causing yellowing symptoms in severe cases of infestation (Gautham 1980). 

Mealybugs – Nine genera/species of mealy bugs, namely, Icerya sp., I. Aegyptiaca (Dgl.), Planococcus sp., P. citri (Risso), P. minor (Mask.), Ferrisia virgata (Ckll.), Pseudococcus sp., P. longispinus (Targioni) and P. orchidicola Takahashi have been reported to infest pepper (Devasahayam et al. 1988; Koya et al. 1996). The mealy bugs normally infest the tender portions of the vines such shoots, leaves and berries. Generally no visible symptoms are observed in the field; however, severe infestations result in wilting of young plants. 

Scale Insects – Apart from L. piperis and A. destructor, 12 genera/species of scale insects, namely Coccus piperis Green, Marsipococcus marsupialis Green, Protopulvinaria longivalvata Green, Parlatoria pergandii Com., Pinnaspis aspidistrae Sign., P. marchali Ckll., P. strachani (Cooley), Chionaspis raricosa Green, Pseudaulacaspis sp., P. cockerelli (Cooley), Unaspis sp. and Anmalococcus indicus Ayyar infest pepper (Devasahayam et al. 1988; Koya et al. 1996; Suresh and Mohanasundaram 1996). Infestation results in subsequent attack by sooty moulds which accelerates the deterioration of the infested plants. The other species occur either on stems and/or on mature leaves causing localised chlorotic patches/spots.

Mirid Bugs – The tea mosquito bug, Helopeltis antonii Sign., is observed occasionally on young pepper vines in the field and also in the nursery. Infestation results in the formation of necrotic lesions on tender stems and angular necrotic spots on tender leaves (Devasahayam et al. 1986).

Pentatomid Bugs – The feeding activity of stink bug, Cyclopelta siccifolia Westw. and Udonga Montana (Dist.) causes defoliation and spike shedding (Mathew and Sudheendrakumar 1991).

Thrips – Two other species of Liothrips i.e., L. pallipes Karny and L. chavicae (Z.).

Root Grubs – Grubs of Holotrichia fissa Brenske occasionally feed on roots of young vines causing their mortality. 

Stem Borers – Grubs of Diboma procera Pasc. and Pterolophia annulata Chevr. tunnel into and feed the inner portions of the stems of pepper vines around the collar region. P. griseovaria Breuning also attacks pepper in many parts of Kerala and Karnataka. The Sweet potato weevil Cylas formicarius F. bores into orthotropic shoots of pepper vines, resulting in yellowing and drying of leaves and subsequently the whole vine (Ranjith 1985).

Leaf Feeding Beetles – Includes Neculla pollinaria Baly., Pagria costatipennis Jacoby (nair 1975), Hermaeophaga sp. and Lanka sp. (Premkumar and Nair 1987b) in Kerala and Tegyrius sp. in Karnataka. Generally feed on tender leaves making small holes. The weevils feeding on foliage include Eugnathus curvus Faust. (Nair 1975) and Myllocerus sp. (Pillai 1978).

Gall Midges – Maggots of the gall midge, Cecidomyia malabarensis Felt, develop within berries, leaf stalks, leaf veins and shoots of pepper vines resulting in swelling of infested tissues; more common in nursery. Two unidentified species of cecidomyiids causing hypophyllous globose solitary pellet shaped galls on the leaf and minute irregular rinden galls on the stem are also reported (Mani 1973).

Leaf Feeding Caterpillars – The armyworm, Spodoptera litura F., damages young pepper vines and also young cuttings in the nursery by feeding on the leaves. The semilooper Synegia sp. damages tender shoots, leaves and spikes especially in young vines. Other leaf feeding caterpillars recorded include Latoia lepida Cram. (Fletcher 1914), Cricula trifenestrata Helf. (Ayyar 1940) and Thosea sinensis Walk. (Hill 1983) (13).

Major Diseases 
Phytophthora Diseases
P. capsici occurs on all parts of the plant and cause economic damage. Phytophthora infections in pepper is broadly classified into aerial and soil infections. Aerial infection occurs on the runner shoots, foliage, spikes and branches causing blight, spike shedding, defoliation and die back and at times death of plants. Infection on the runner shoots often reach the collar causing foot rot. 

Symptoms of foliar infection 
On leaves one to many dark spots having characteristic fimbriate margins occur, which later coalesce leading to defoliation. The fungus sporulates abundantly forming a white covering on the blighted tender shoot. Infection on tender shoots upon reaching the stem, cause, collar infection and sudden wilting of plant. Infection of spikes causes blackening of developing fruits and peduncle. Infection on branches causes drying and defoliation. 

Symptoms on roots and collar
Infections of feeder roots cause their rotting and degeneration resulting in yellowing, defoliation and drying up of plants. Feeder root infection reaches the collar through main roots and causes foot rot (Anandaraj et al. 1994). If the infection reaches the collar, either through the runner shoots or through the roots closer to the soil level, plants only show wilting symptoms, whereas, infection reaching the collar through roots of the lower nodes leads to yellowing and defoliation before succumbing to the infection (Anandaraj 1997). 

Control: Spraying 1% Bordeaux mixture (BM), pasting the collar with BM and drenching the basins with either BM or copper oxychloride (Ramachandran et al. 1991, Sasikumaran et al. 1981, Nair and Sasikumaran 1991, Malebennur et al. 1991, Lokesh and Gangadarappa 1995). Among systemic fungicides, Ridomil granules and Ridomil-ziram are effective against foot rot of pepper (Ramachandran and Sarma 1985, Ramachandran 1990, Ramachandran et al. 1988b, 1991, Sarma et al. 1992c, Kueh and Sim 1992a, Kueh et al. 1993). Several antagonistic microorganisms such Trichoderma and Gliocladium, occurring in rhizosphere of pepper, prevents the population build up of Phytophthora capsici. VAM alone or in combination with other beneficial microorganisms like Azotobacter and Azospirillum enhances rooting, growth and suppress root damage caused by P. capsici. Organic matters such as neem oil cake (Sadanandan et al. 1992, Nair et al. 1993) soyabean meal, groundnut cake, coconut cake and chicken manure are added to the soil to supplement nutrition and enhance the growth of saprophytes (Kueh and Sim 1992a), which causes the decline in P. capsici population.

Control: Phytosanitary measures like removing the infected plants and burning and application of 200 g calcium cyanamide per mound are practiced. Prophylactic application of fungicides at fortnightly interval, systemic fungicide followed by contact fungicide to the foliage and soil drenching with Benomyl (0.05%) and Thiobendazole (0.06%) were also effective. 

Slow Decline of Pepper caused by P. capsici, Radopholus similis and Meloidogyne incognita, either alone or in different combinations.

A debilitating disease, where the affected plants survive for several years and death of plants occur gradually over a period of 3-4 years (Anandaraj et al. 1996a, 1996b).

Anthracnose of Pepper caused by Colletotrichum sp. and C. gloeosporioides
Referred as “pollu” disease in India, meaning hollow fruits and as “black berry” disease in Malaysia and Indonesia. This occurs both on leaves and on spikes. 

Symptoms: On younger fruits infection leads to blackening and on older fruits brownish lesions are formed (Ayyar et al. 1918, Sebastian 1982). If the infection occurs on the stalk end of spike, the entire spike is shed prematurely, and brown lesions are produced on the leaves. The fungus is reported to survive on Dioscorea triphylla as an alternate host (Wilson 1960).

Control: Bordeaux mixture (1%) spray is recommended (Sundararaman 1928, Nair et al. 1987). Difolatan (0.2%), Benomyl, Thiophanate ethyl, Thiophanate methyl, Carbendazim, Captofol and Tridemefron are also effective. 

Stunted Disease
Also known as “little leaf disease” (Randombage and Bandara 1984) or “mosaic disease” (Prakasam et al. 1990) in Sri Lanka, “mosaic disease” in India (Prakasam et al. 1990), “wrinkled leaf disease” in Malaysia (Kueh and Sim 1992b) and as “stunted disease” in Indonesia (Sitepu and Kasim 1991, Firdausil et al. 1992). 

Symptoms: The leaves become narrow and leathery in texture, with puckering and chlorotic streaks, shortened internodes and branches appear as witches broom, at times the leaves show narrowing and vein banding. 

Control: Rouging of infected plants and quarantine measures are to be followed. 

Minor Diseases
Phyllody 
Reported from Wynad district of Kerala (Sarma et al. 1988, Sarma and Anandaraj 1992), also called as “antholysis” (Remila and Neelakandan 1994). 

Symptoms: The spikes and flowers are converted to leaf like structutes and the stalk are also elongated. The leaves become smaller and show chlorosis. 

Control: As the disease is caused by phytoplasma, removal of affected plants and following quarantine measures are suggested (Sarma and Anandaraj 1992). 

Bacterial Leaf Spot - caused by Xanthomonas compestris cv beticola. 
Symptoms: Small water soaked lesions on leaf lamina and margins which later become dark with a chlorotic halo and at time cause defoliation. 

Control: Chloramphenicol @ 200 ppm was effective in inhibiting the growth of this bacterium (Mathew et al. 1979). 

Thread Blight – caused by Pellicularia filamentosa (Corticium solani)
Occurs on pepper leaves and spikes (Ramakrishnan 1957). The fungus grows underneath the leaves and on stem causing drying up of leaves and spikes. 

Stump Rot - caused by Rosellina bunodes. The fungus affects the root system which results in drying up of plants. Isolation of affected plants by making trenches is recommended to prevent the spread of this disease. 

Red Rust - caused by the alga Cephaleuros mycoidea, on older leaves and affects the appearance of berries thereby reducing the quality in certain pockets. This causes black fruit in Malaysia (Menon 1949, Sarma et al. 1991). 

Dodder
The occurrence of parasitic flowering plant (Cuscuta reflexa – Convolvulaceae) on pepper is recorded in some pepper growing areas. It fully covers the plant and the productivity of vine is affected. Removal of the affected branches controls it (Sarma et al. 1991). 

Velvet Blight
Caused by fungus Septobasidium sp. It grows on surface of fruits and forms a coat, but not penetrate the fruit. When it occurs on branches, die back symptoms are observed. Pruning of the affected branches would prevent the spread of the disease (Sarma et al. 1991).

White/Yellow spots - caused by lichens
Infects on the stem. Occurs seldom and does not cause serious losses. 

Basal wilt – caused by Sclerotium rolfsi
Causes rotting and wilting of the cuttings. The affected plants show greyish lesions on stems and growth of the fungus as whitish mycelium with numerous sclerotia. On the leaves greyish spots with whitish mycelium at the advancing edges of lesions and sclerotia on lesions are seen. Carbendazim and mercuric fungicides can check the germination of sclerotia under (Choudhury 1943, Brahma et al. 1980) (12).

Nematode Induced Diseases
Root knot nematodes - Meloidogyne incognita, M. javanica, M. arenaria and M. hapla.
They are sedentary obligate endoparasites, most destructive of all the nematode parasites. Of these M. incognita and M. javanica are widely distributed in tropical, subtropical and warm temperate regions.

Symptoms: Infestation leads to the development of elongated swellings on the thick primary roots and the typical knots or galls on secondary/fibrous roots due to hypertrophy and hyperplasia of the infested tissue. They feed on the vascular tissues, affecting the absorption and translocation of water and nutrients. Other symptoms are foliar yellowing, poor growth and gradual decline in health and vigour. 

Burrowing nematodes (Radopholus similis)
Obligate migratory endoparasites, widely prevalent in most of the tropical and subtropical regions of the world. They feed on cortical tissues and producing dark brown necrotic lesions on roots. Other symptoms include foliar yellowing, defoliation, lack of vigour and retardation in growth. 

Pepper nematodes (Trophotylenchulus piperis)
A sedentary, semi-endoparasite found in the roots of pepper. The adult females on the root surface are covered with hard dark brown cases. Necrotic lesions develop at the feeding site, which further leads to shrinkage and drying of the infested roots (Ramana and Eapen 1997). 

Control: Fumigants like Methyl Bromide, Ethylene di Bromide, are highly effective in eliminating nematode populations in the nursery mixture. Nematicides like Phenamiphos (Nambiar andSarma 1980), Aldicarbsulphone (Venkitesan and Setty 1979), Phorate, DBCP (Venkitesh and Charles 1980), Thimet 10 G (phorate), Furadan 3 G (carbofuran), Temik 10 G (aldicarb) applied @ 3 g.a.i./plant, twice a year significantly reduced the populations of M. incognita and R. similis (Mohandas and Ramana 1987, Ramana et al. 1994). Addition of organic amendments like organic manures, crop residues, green manures, oilseed cakes, plant extracts etc improve the soil texture and also enhance the growth of useful microorganisms like VAM, Trichoderma sp., Paecilomyces lilacinus, Pasteuria penetrans, Bacillus pumilis, B. macerans, B. circulans, etc suppressive to nematodes. Certain oilseed cakes like neem, karanj (Pongamia glabra), mahua (Madhuca indica), castor (Ricinus communis), mustard (Brassica spp.) are commonly used in India (Mishra and Majumdar 1995). Application of neem cake @ 2 kg/vine twice a year along with fertilizers was found highly effective in suppressing the populations of M. incognita (Ramana et al. 1992) (14).

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Black pepper is one of the few spices having multiple uses in processed food industry, in kitchen, on dining table, in perfumery and allied industries, in traditional medicine and even in beauty care. Black pepper is a well-known culinary spice and condiment. Pepper is valued for its pungency and flavour, the former mainly due to the alkaloid piperine and the latter to the volatile oil present. The food processing industry makes use of the various ingredients (piperine and oil) either alone or in combination. The oleoresin extracted contains both pungency and flavour components and hence is used as such in processed food industry. 

Parts Used: The fruit is the most important useful part of Black Pepper.

Harvesting Practices: Harvesting is usually done in December-January, when one or two berries in few spikes turn orange or red. At higher altitudes, the fruit growth and maturity get delayed due to the low winter temperature. Care should be taken to harvest only mature spikes. Harvesting is done using a single pole ladder kept leant onto the support tree. The harvested spikes are collected in clean sacs or bags tied at the back of the worker. 

Post Harvest Handling:
Decorning (Threshing) - Harvested spikes are spread on a clean floor and then threshed manually by trampling with legs or by using mechanical thresher. Washing of Spikes - Mechanical washing of the harvested spikes or even simple dipping in a tank or big vessel with clean water will remove the adhering particles of dust and dirt. Blanching - Blanching of the threshed fruits is carried out by taking them in bamboo baskets or a perforated vessel and dipping in boiling water for one minute, which cleans the produce thoroughly from all the adhering dust particles, birds’ excreta and other extraneous particles and gives shining black colour to the produce and enhance the drying process. After blanching the produce can be dried to a uniform moisture level (8-10%) within three days in full sunlight. Blanching is known to activate the phenolase enzyme, which is responsible for producing the black colour. Sun Drying - After threshing fruits are dried in sun on bamboo mat, cement floor, polyethylene fabric or using solar drier or other mechanical driers (copra drier, convection drier, cascade type drier, etc). Garbling, Cleaning and Fractionation - Before packing dried pepper is cleaned to get rid of extraneous matters like dirt, grit, stones, pinheads, immature pepper, stalks, leaves etc using mechanical separators or hand picking and berries are graded according to size or density. Traditionally pepper is manually cleaned by winnowing and then packed in gunny bags(15).  


Produce and Products: The value added products may be classified into three major groups (Pruthi 1997): (i) Green pepper based products (ii) Black and white pepper based products (iii) Pepper by-products. 

A. Green pepper based products
Canned green pepper in brine, bottled green pepper in brine, bulk packed green pepper in brine, cured green pepper (without any covering tissue), frozen green pepper, freeze dried green pepper, sun dried or dehydrated green pepper, green pepper pickles in oil/vinegar/brine, green pepper mixed pickle in oil/vinegar/brine, green pepper flavoured products, green pepper paste.

B. Black and white pepper based products
Black pepper powder, white pepper powder, white pepper whole, pepper oleoresin, pepper oil,
C. Other pepper products
By-products from pepper wastes.


Chemistry: Chemistry of pepper can be classified broadly into (i) compounds contributing to its pungency and (ii) compounds imparting its characteristics. The aroma of pepper is contributed by the essential oil present in the fruits, while the pungency is due to the alkaloid piperine. The commercial product oleoresin, solvent extracted from pepper powder, contains both pungency and aroma contributing compounds (16).  

Pepper Essential Oil and other constituents 
Essential oil contains α-pinene, β-pinene, 1-α-phellandrene, dl-limonene, piperonal, dihydrocarvenol (m.p. - 1610C), β-caryophyllene, epoxydihydrocaryophyllene (piperidine complex), cryptone, possibly citronellol and an azulene (Hasselstrom 1951). About 135 compounds consisting of monoterpenoids, sesquiterpenoids, aliphatic, aromatic and miscellaneous nature were identified (Ikeda et al. 1962; Sharma et al. 1962; Nigam and Handa 1964; Wrolstadt and Jennings 1965; Chopra 1965; Muller and Jennings 1967; Muller et al. 1968; Russel and Else 1973; Richard and Jennings 1971; Debrauwere and Verzele 1975a,b 1976; Muller 1976; Lawrence 1979-80; Gopalakrishnan et al. 1993).

There 15 monoterpene hydrocarbons identified so far and they are camphene, δ3-carene, p-cymene, limonene, myrcene, cis-ocimene, α-phellandrene, β- phellandrene, α and β-pinenes, sabinene, α- and γ-terpinenes, terpinolene and α-thujene.

The other 24 sesquiterpene hydrocarbons identified are α-cis-bergamontene, α-trans-bergamontene, β-bisabolene, δ and γ-cadinenes, calamenene, α-copaene, α- and β-cubebenes, ar-curcumene, β- and δ-elemenes, β-farnesene, α-guainene, α- and γ-humulenes, isocaryophyllene, γ-muurolene, α-santalene, α- and β-selinenes, ledene, sesquisabinene and zingiberene. Miscellaneous compounds 
Eugenol, methyl eugenol, myristicin, safrole, benzaldehyde, trans-anethole, piperonal, m-methyl acetophone, p-methyl acetophone, n-butyrophenone, benzoic acid, phenyl acetic acid, cinnamic acid and piperonic acid are some of the aromatic compounds characterised in pepper oil. Methyl heptenone, pinol, butyric acid, 3-methyl butyric acid, hexanoic acid, 2-methyl pentanoic acid, methyl heptanoate, methyl octanoate, 2-undecanone, n-nonane, n-tridecane, n-nonadecane and piperidine are the other compounds identified.

Pungent Compounds of Pepper
Piperine, the most abundant alkaloid in pepper is a yellow crystalline substance and the structure was identified as the trans form of piperoyl piperidine. Later a pungent dark oily resin was obtained, which was named chavicine (mixture of piperine and several minor alkaloids). Five new minor alkaloids possessing a degree of pungency identified are piperettine, piperylin, piperolein A and B and piperanine. Three trace constituents viz peepuloidin, guineesine and pipericide showing insecticidal properties have recently been identified.

Piperine (C17H19O3N) has a melting point of 128-1300C and is a weak base which on hydrolysis with aqueous alkali or nitric acid yielded a volatile base, piperidine (C5H11N). 

Phenolics
The phenolics in black pepper are a mixture of phenolic acid glycosides and flavanol glycosides. On hydrolysis nine phenolic acids comprising hydroxy benzoic acids and hydroxy cinnamic acids along with significant quantities of quercetin and kaempferol were obtained (Bandyopadhyay 1990). Parmer et al. (1997) has listed the following flavones from pepper: isoquercetin, isorhamnetin, 3-0-β-D-rutinoside, kaempferol 3-0-arabinoside-7-rhamnoside, kaempferol 3-0-β-glucoside, quercetin 3-0-β-D-rutinoside. Pepper also contains sitosterol.

Blackening of Pepper
Lewis et al. (1976) reported that blackening of pepper is due to the enzymatic oxidation of polyphenolic substrates present in the skin of green pepper. 

Other Constituents of Black Pepper
Starch is a predominant constituent of black pepper ranging from 35-40 percent of its weight. Pepper contains fat ranging from 1.9-9.0 percent. The major fatty acids are palmitic acid (16-30%), oleic (18-29%), linoleic (25-35%) and linolenic acid (8-19%). Parmer et al. (1997) listed the following miscellaneous constituents from pepper: acetylcholine, caffeic acid, capric acid, choline, p-coumaric acid, p- and m-cresol, 2(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl) ethanol, henriacontane, henriacontane-16-ol, henriacontane-16-one, lauric acid, melvalic acid, 3,4-methyleredioxy cinnamaldehyde, myristic acid, oleic acid, palmitic acid, phenyl acetic acid, piperonal, stearic acid, sterculic acid and vernolic acid (16).

Processing Techniques:

Other uses: Whole black pepper in retail packs for table use, ground pepper in retail packs and dispensers. Flavoured ground pepper such as lemon pepper, garlic pepper, sauces and marinades that have pepper as the main component, pepper paste. Curry powders and spice blends for specific cuisines (such as five-spice powder, soup blends, etc). Pepper mayonnaise, pepper tofu, pepper cookies, pepper keropok (Prawn or fish crackers). Pepper candy, pepper perfume, Paper from pepper stalk, use of pepper stalk for growing mushrooms, etc.

Uses of Green Pepper Products: Immature or partly mature pepper fruits are used for the manufacture of green pepper in brine, oil/vinegar, desiccated green pepper, freeze dried green pepper, green pepper paste, etc. Tender spikes and fruits alone or in combination with tender cardamom pickled in vinegar and salt or sugar make delicious dishes (1).

Uses of black pepper products: Ground pepper - used in the dining table. It is also the only spice served along with food in flights and fast food restaurants. Flavoured pepper powder such as lemon pepper and garlic pepper - used as table condiment or in kitchen to flavour salads, sandwiches, chicken, fish etc. Pepper sauce - used as marinade for steaks, and other meat dishes and for stir frying. Pepper also is used in Worcestershire sauce, mayonnaise and various salad dressings in sauce, where pepper is used either in ground form or as oleoresin and oil for added flavour.

Spice mixes meant for a variety of dishes such as for various meat, fish, vegetable preparations are available in the modern supermarkets. Pepper flavoured vinegar and pepper salt are also excellent products for kitchen use. 

Pepper beverages: Pepper is used to flavour certain beverages such as pepper tea, coffee, pepper flavoured milk, pepper drinks such as Rasala, etc. Piperine and pepper oil are added to certain liquors such as some brands of brandy to impart a pungent taste and an exotic flavour.

Pepper oil, oleoresin and encapsulated flavours: The largest use of pepper oleoresin is in flavouring meat. The other end uses are preparing pickles, sauces, gravies, dressings, chutneys, soups and snacks. The encapsulated flavour (oleoresin) powder is used in dry beverage mixes, cake mixes, infant desserts, soup mixes, dusting on potato chip, nuts, etc.

Pepper perfumes: In perfumery the volatile oil from pepper can be used to impart a spicy, exclusive, oriental note. Some of the brands containing pepper oil are Revlon’s “Charlie”, Christian Dior’s “Poison” and the perfumes developed in Malaysia – “Sensai” and “Amila” (Ng 1993) 

White Pepper
White pepper is prepared from the fully ripe reddish berries which are decorticated and dried. The outer pericarp is removed before or after drying. After harvesting the ripe berries are tightly packed into gunny bags. The bags are then soaked in slow running water for 10-14 days. During this operation, microbial retting process occurs which loosens the pericarp from the core of the fruit. Then the fruits are washed again to remove the adhering pericarp and then dried in sun as usual. The white pepper can be sold in whole or ground form. This product is mainly used in food products in which dark particles are undesirable such as in light coloured sauces, salad dressings, mayonnaise and soups (1). White Pepper, Sterilized Black Pepper, Dehydrated Black Pepper, Black, White and Green Pepper Powders and Cracked Peppercorns are also produced and exported from India. 

Medicinal Uses: Pepper is one of the most important and unavoidable drugs in Ayurveda, Unani and Sidha, the Indian systems of Medicine. It is used as single drug or in combination with long pepper (Piper longum) and dry ginger (Zingiber officinale). It has the property of dispelling poison, pepper is pungent and acrid, hot, rubefacient, carminative, dry corrosive, alternative, antihelminthic and germicidal. It promotes salivation, increases the digestive power, gives relish for the food and cures cough, dyspnoea, cardiac diseases, colic, worms, diabetes, piles, epilepsy and almost all diseases caused by the disorders of vata and pitta. Pepper is prescribed in cholera, flatulence, diarrhoea and various gastric aliments. It is employed as antiperiodic in obstinate fevers, particularly malarial fever, as an alternative in paraplegia and arthritic diseases, as an aromatic stimulant in cholera, weakness following fevers, vertigo, coma and as a stomachic in dyspepsia. An infusion of pepper forms a useful stimulant gargle in relaxed sore throat, hoarseness, toothache and inflammation. Pepper is useful in diseases of the spleen, pain in liver and muscles, leucoderma, lumbago and paralysis. It is a local application for alopacia and some skin diseases. Pepper is esteemed as a digestive tonic and is believed to be diuretic, emmenagogue and a good stimulant in cases of bites of venomous reptiles. Administering pepper powder with butter removes oedema in children. According to Vagabhata, it is effective against night blindness; a paste made of black pepper and buttermilk is recommended for external application. Water boiled with pepper powder is a remedy for chronic dysentery. A combination of pepper, milk and oil applied externally is effective in curing rhinitis or coryza. It is effective against malaria, common cold, cough, elephantiasis, appendicitis and influenza. Pepper powder mixed with fresh ghee and taken internally cures itching due to skin diseases on fingers, knees, hips, etc (1). Also used in bladder stone, antidiarrhoeal, fever, aids digestion, hysteria, all women's diseases, disease of the liver, complaints of the stomach, plague, tetanus and contraceptive (18).

Botanical pesticides
Pepper has insecticidal and/or insect repellent and ovicidal activities to different insects and pests harmful to mankind. The major alkaloid piperine is more toxic than pyrethrine, a standard insecticide, to houseflies (Harvill et al. 1943). The volatile oil was found to cause mortality to Lasioderma serricorne (cigarette beetle) (Samuel et al. 1984). The alcoholic extract of pepper was effective against cotton ball weevil, rice weevil and Drosophila (Su 1977, Scot and Mikiben 1978, Barakat et al. 1985). At moderate to low doses acetone and petroleum ether extracts were lethal to storage insects like rice weevil (Sitophilus oryzae), pulse beetle (Callosobrunchus chinensis), cowpea weevil, lesser grain borer (Rhizoperpta dominica), ricemoth and repellent to flour beetle (Tribolium castaneum) (Gunathilagaraj and Kumaraswami 1979). This is also ovicidal for Tetranychus and toxic to eggs of rice armyworm (Spodoptera). The oleoresin, piperine and other amides inflict lethality to culex mosquito (Culex pipiens) and also to pulse weevil (Su 1977). Piperonal, piperine, piperoline, pellitorine, pepercide and dihydropipercide possess good insecticidal property towards pulse beetle, rice weevil, and Drosophila (Miya Kado et al. 1979, Masakazu et al. 1980 and Desmukh et al. 1982) (1).

Export Potential

Pepper is mainly cultivated in India for the export market. India tops the list of producing countries with 79,000 tons (1999-2000). Pepper export is 19250 tons at value of Rs. 32632.75 lakhs (Spices Board, Kochi: 2000-2001) to countries like Hungary, North Korea, Maldives, Trinidad, Yemen, Arab Republic and Yugoslavia has considerably reduced whereas new consumers have entered the scene. They include Chile, Kazhakistan, Tajikistan and Venezuela. Pepper is the major spice in international trade accounting for 30 to 35 percent in quantity and 20 to 25 percent in value (Peter 1996b) (1).

Miscellaneous Information

Planting
Planting of standards is to be taken up in April-May with the onset of pre-monsoon showers. Murukku (Erythrina indica) Karayam or Killingil (Garuga pinnata), Ailanthus sp., Subabul (Leucaenea leucocephal) etc., are suitable standards for growing pepper. In high altitude areas, dadaps (E. lithosperma) and silver oak (Grevilea robusta) are used as standards for pepper. The cuttings of standards are to be planted in narrow holes of 40-50 cm depth. The spacing recommended is 3 x 3 m on plain lands and 2 m between plants in rows across the slope and 4 m between rows on sloping lands. For planting pepper, prepare pits on the northern side of standards, 15 cm away from it. The pit size should be 50 x 50 x 50 cm. Fill the pits with a mixture of topsoil and compost or well rotten cattle manure @ 5 kg/pit. With the onset of South West monsoon in June-July, plant 2-3 rooted cuttings in the pits. The growing portions of the cuttings are to be trailed and tied to the standards. Provide shade to the plants if the land is exposed and if there is a break in the rainfall. When pepper is grown on coconut or arecanut trees, the pepper cuttings are to be planted 1-1.5 m away from the trunk of the trees. 

Management after planting: If the terrain of the land is sloppy or uneven, carry out contour bunding or terracing to prevent soil erosion. Digging around the standards and vines at a radius of about 1 m from the base is carried out twice during the year. 

Intercropping of pepper gardens with ginger, turmeric, colocasia and elephant foot yam is advantageous. Banana as an intercrop reduces pepper yields. Mulching the basins of pepper vines with saw dust, arecanut husk and dry leaves during summer months are highly advantageous. Prune and train the standards in March-April every year to remove excessive over-growth and to give them a proper shape (9).
Productive Life Span: The productive life span of pepper is around 10-15 years (1).
