Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

 Indigenous to Brazil and Guiana, grown in garden in India for its handsome foliage and showy flowers. 

Quassia amara is a small, 4-6 m tall, tropical tree which is distributed in Central America, Brazil and Surinam. A related shrub, Aeschrion excelsa, which grows in the West Indies, also contains insecticidal compounds. In India, farmers use Picrasma excelsa, which is either closely related to or identical with A. excelsa.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:
An erect shrub, 2-3 m. high, bark light colored; leaves unequally pinnate with broadly winged rachis; leaflets obovate; flowers purple in terminal racemes; drupes 1-5 purple black, 12-13 mm. Long; seeds globular.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Insecticidal, Larvicidal, Nematicidal, Quassia also acts systemically. Roots and leaves are used against Plutella xylosteaal, Leptinotarsa decemlineata, Diaphania hyalinata, Attagenus piceus, Bombyx mori, Hoplocampa spp. and  Phymatocera aterrima.

It is above all the wood which contains the insecticidal properties, but the roots, leaves and bark all contain quassin to a small degree. 

Parts Used:  Roots and Leaves


Harvesting Practices: Matured leaves and roots are harvested 


Produce and Products:


Chemistry: β –sitrosterone, β- sitosterol.

Processing Techniques: Spray preparations should be used immediately after making up. Since the insecticidal contents vary from place to place as well as methods of preparing the solution, it is advisable to experiment locally to discover optimum as well as preparation procedures.

Because Quassia works systemically, plants can be kept insect free by applying a Quassia in water solution to the ground. This is taken up by the roots and distributed throughout the plant.

Quassia sprays should not be used on plants with edible fruit or leaves. The solution is extremely bitter and is stable and long lasting.

Miscellaneous Information

Q. amara furnishes the bitter quassia wood known as Surinam Quassia in commerce. Surinam quassia resembles Jamaica Quassia obtained from Picrasma excelsa (q.v) in respect of its appearance, structure of wood, chemical constituents, medicinal and insecticidal properties and uses. The wood of Q. amara is heavier, harder and more deeply colored, light and soft, fine-textured, easily worked, finishing smoothly: the billers are usually thinner. More recently, B-sitrosterone and B-sitosterol were isolated from the wood.
