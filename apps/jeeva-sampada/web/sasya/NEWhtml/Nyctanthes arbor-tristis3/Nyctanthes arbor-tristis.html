 Other Biological Details
HABIT & HABITAT
A hardy large shrub or small tree, native to India, Thailand and Indonesia. Occurring wild in the sub-Himalayan region from Chenab to Nepal. In its natural habitat it grows gregariously on dry steep hillsides and rocky terrain and is often found in the under storey of dry deciduous forests, sometimes farming dense thickets. It is cultivated in gardens almost throughout India and in many other tropical countries for its flowers which are valued for making garlands and as offerings in temples.

The tree is very hardy and can withstand any type of soil.  However, heavy soils with impeded drainage is not suitable.


Availability :  
The tree grows all over India from Sub-Himalayas to Cape comerin; also in Central India, Burma and Ceylon.  

Status : 
These shrubs can be planted in gardens, Front yards of houses and in public parks for the perfume of flowers.  Commonly found in forests on hilly slopes.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

Grey or greenish-white rough bark. Branchlets quadrangular, strigose; leaves ovate, acuminate, entire or with  a few large distant  teeth, rough and scabrous above densely pubescent beneath; Flowers small 3-7 in each head, arranged in trichotomous cymes; Corolla fragrant, white 4-8 lobed with bright orange tube; Capsules suborbicular, compresed, chartaceous, separating into two flat 1-seeded carpels.
 The flowers open towards evening and fall to the ground on the following morning.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Bark, wood, leaves, flowers, seeds

Uses

The corolla tubes are orange-coloured and when severed from the hand give a beautiful fleeting orange or golden dye.  Corolla tube contain a colouring matter, nyctanthin, which is identical with crocetin from saffron.

  • The dye is mostly used for silk, sometimes in combination with turmeric.  Half a seer of the dried corolla tubes will dye 60yards of silk cloth. Flowers are rich in yellow colouring matter, which is readily soluble in water.  The infusion produces very bright yellow colour on mulberry silk when properly mordanted; and when the right procedure is used this dye-stuff appears to be eminently suited for dyeing tussar silk yellow.  It has not such a great affinity for wool. 
  • It is also used for colouring other kind of fabrics and for imparting colour to liquor.
  •   The whole flowers yield a fine buff or orange brown dye. It is much used in combination with other dyes.  
  • The inner bark afford a red dye when beaten with a little lime.
  • Bark  yields a  tanning material, expectorant. 

Harvesting Practices
The flowers which fall in the early morning, are usually collected.  

Produce and Products
Dye, Tannin

Processing Techniques
The flowers which fall in the early morning, are usually collected by women and children.  The white upper portion of the corolla, is picked off and the yellow tubes which remain are put out to dry in the sun and afterwards stored for future.  The colour is extracted by steeping the dye-stuff in boiling water and straining through a cloth.  The fabric to be dyed, generally silk, is first drenched with water and then steeped in the liquid, either when hot or after it has cooled.  It is dried in the shade.  The result is a bright orange colour, the shade of which can be deepened by further steeping.  The colour is very fleeting and no proper means of fixing it appear to be known.  In some parts of Bengal alum is employed for the same purpose and in Murshidabad, however a little lime juice is added to the liquid and this seems to render the colour, less transitory.

Other Uses
  1. Wood - Batten base for tile or grass thatch roofs
  2. Young branches used to make baskets
  3. Bark  yields a  tanning material, expectorant.  A decoction of bark, leaves, roots and flowers is used in Ayurvedic preparations for treating excessive diuresis and enlargement of the spleen.
  4. Leaves - Polishing wood and ivory, antibilious, expectorant and useful in fever and rheumatism, decoction (sciatica), cholagogue, laxative, diaphoretic, diuretic.
  5. Powered seeds - scurvy, affections of the scalp and baldness. Among the tribal inhabitants of Eastern Bihar, the seed paste mixed with honey  is applied for baldness The powered seeds are given in the treatment of malaria.
  6. Flowers are bitter, astringent, ophthalmic, stomachic, carminative and trichogenous and are useful in inflammations, ophalmopathy, flatulence, colic, dyspepsia, splenomegaly, grayness of hair and baldness.

 Miscellaneous Information

Flowers are valued for making garlands and as votive offerings in temples.  

The dye extracted from corolla tubes is used in colouring the robes of Buddhist priests and the dresses of school girls for offering worships to Goddess Saraswati.  

Tree is mentioned in many of Indian ancient scriptures.

The plant is supposed to have been brought from heaven by the God Krishna for his wife Satyabhama.

In Vishnupurana, it says that the shrub was a King's daughter, named parijataka.  She fell in love with the sun, who soon deserted her, on which she killed herself and was burnt.  This shrub arose from her ashes.  Hence it casts its flowers in the morning, as it cannot bear the sight of the sun.
