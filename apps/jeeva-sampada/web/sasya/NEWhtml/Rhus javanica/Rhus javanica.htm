DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Bark grey, rough, with deep, vertical furrows, exuding an acrid juice; leaves imparipinnatc, 25-45 cm. long : leaflets 5-13, very variable in shape, 5-10 cm. long; flowers numerous ; white or pale-yellowish green, in terminal tomentose panicles; drupes orbicular, 5-8 mm. in diam., compressed, red or red-brown, tomentose. The tree is important because of the galls (CHINESE or JAPANESE GALLS), caused on the leaves by some species of insects belonging to the genera Aphis, Melaphis and Eriophycs. The galls were esteemed for high-quality tannin and gallic acid and were exported from China to the European countries. Chinese galls have unusual hapes, and are covered with a dense, velvety fur. The Japanese galls of commerce are usually smaller than the Chinese galls.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used:
Leaves, Fruits, Leaf galls , Stem bark.

Uses

  1. The tannin content of dry galls is high, varying from 50 to 80 per cent. In addition, the galls contain small amounts of fat, resin and gum. Since the tannin occurs principally in the form of gallotannic acid, the galls are used for the manufacture of this acid. They are also used in the manufacture of inks. In leather tanning, the galls are reported to have the same action as oak-galls.

Produce and Products :

Processing Techniques
Initially the galls are globular or cone-shaped, but as they develop, they become branched or produce horn- like protuberances, in each of which is a cavity occupied by the insects. The galls are at first greenish yellow, but later turn dark green with patches of red or maroon. On reaching maturity on the tree, the galls split open and the insects escape. Commercial galls, however, are usually collected before they rupture. The insects are killed by immersing the galls in boiling water; the galls are then dried in the sun. Dry galls arc brittle and resin-like with a whitish inner surface. Big galls, which are formed by agglomeration of tiny galls, are usually 5-9 cm. long and 2-25-4-00 cm. broad, weighing 3-12 g.

Other Uses
  1. Apart from their use in tanning, the galls are used also for dyeing and for medicinal purposes. 
  2. Galls possess astringent and expectorant properties and are used in ointments and suppositories employed in the treatment of hemorrhoids, swellings and wounds. Some- times the galls are used for blackening teeth.
  3. Stem bark is reported to contain 10-15 per cent tannin.
  4. Fruits have a sharp acidic taste and are eaten. They are used for the treatment of colic and dysentery and their infusion is given to control diarrohea. Fruits contain tannin, gallic acid, and the potassium acid salts, together with small amounts of aluminium, calcium, magnesium and iron acid salts of malic, tartaric and citric acids. A vegetable wax (NEPAL-Omiu), similar to Japan-wax, is extracted from the fruits (wax content,7-8%), and used for candles. Fruits are also used as a substitute for rennet in the preparation of curds. 
  5. The wood is used as fuel.
