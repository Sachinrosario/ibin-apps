Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Cochorus olitorius is a herbaceous erect annual indigenous to India. Generally cultivated on highlands. Hooghly, Malda, Nadia, Jalpaiguri districts of Calcutta, Bihar, Assam, Orissa, Uttar Pradesh and Travancore, C. olitorius grows wild in many parts of India
Availability : Abundant


Conservation/ Threat Status : Both wild and cultivated.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

C. olitorius is usually a foot or two tall but several feet taller in India. The stem is smooth, cylindrical and more or less branched. The leaves are lively green, smooth, alternate, oval or ovolancedate with dentate margin; the two lower most dentitures terminate in a slender filament. The stipules are simple, oval-shaped and reddish at their base. However are generally in extra axillary cymes in groups of 2-5, sepals 5 or 6, petals 5 or 6, yellow. Fruit is a cylindrical pod. Seeds are numerous, grayish green or bluish black

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

C. olitorius flowers bear 30-60 stamens. Cup shaped nectary glands are present at the base of the petals. Fruit is a long, nearly cylindrical, 10 ribbed, 6-8 times longer than broad, 5 celled and 5 valved capsule which has 5 terminal points. Seeds have nearly perfect transverse partitions between them.

Breeding system and varietal improvement:

Breeding System : C. olitorius is highly self-pollinating . Several attempts have been made to cross the two jute yielding species namely C. olitorius and C. capsularis. Ganesan et al. reported that following hybridization between C. capsularis and C. olitorius a few celled embryo and a few free endosperm nuclei were formed but both degenerated. Islam and Rashid were able to induce full development o the hybrid embryo and endosperm by treatment with 3-indoleacetic acid.

Sulbha and Swaminathan grafted the two species reciprocally and used the grafted plants for interspecific hybridization. Grafting increased the percentage of fruit set but did not lead to any marked improvement in the growth of the hybrid embryo, when C. olitorius was used as the pistillate parent and marked increase in fruit set occurred when C. capsularis was used as the pistillate parent.


Phenology: C. olitorius flowers during the rainy season; fruits ripen during September-October


Pollinators: In C. olitorius the flowers are larger and remain open longer; the extent of natural crossing is therefore greater, average about 12% as compared to 2-3% in C. capsularis.

Floral and seed biology:

Seedling germination :

Broadcasting is the traditional method of planting the jute crop. Jute seeds are tiny unlike the seeds of other fiber crops; Olitorius seeds average about 500/g. For broadcasting the seeds are usually mixed with dry sand to obtain a more uniform stand; half the number of seeds is broadcast in north-south direction and the other half in east-west direction to obtain more uniform distribution. The seeding rate used in India is 6.0 kg/ha. The advantages of row cropping far outweighs the traditional method of broadcast seeding. Row planted jute is more uniform; it is easier to cultivate, thin, harvest, and ret; the yield is substantially greater; and the fiber from row planted jute is more uniform. In general, closer row width and plant spacing in the row gave higher yields.


MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
Agrotis ypsilon Rott. (the black cutworm) and Feltia subterranean (Fab.) (granulate cutworm) cut off jute plants near soil level. These pests can be controlled by preparation of land if weedy and application of toxophene.

Aphis gossypii (Glov.) stunts young plants; 1-2% parathion dusts give adequate control.

Estigmene acrasa Drury (salt marsh caterpillar) is another pest of tossa jute which can be controlled by parathion and toxaphene dust.

Prodenia eradiana (Cram.) acts as a defoliators. Good control is obtained with 10% toxaphene dust. Aphion spp. (small weevils) breed in stem tunneling half way round generally near leaf axils and causes stunting of the plants.

Cosmophila sabulifera (leaf eating semi looper) attacks leaves and leaf buds.

Laphyma exigna (Indigo caterpillar) feeds on young leaves. In worst cases they wholly defoliate the plants.

Diacrisia oblique (Hairy caterpillar), feeds on jute leaves .


Diseases:

Root rot and stem rot (both rots caused by Macrophomina phaseoli (Membl.) Ashby) occur at all stages of plant growth and damage leaves and stem. The infected plants will stand shed their leaves premature. Control measures include field sanitation practices together with balanced fertilizer application, spraying fungicide to prevent foliar infections, and use of resistant varieties. Powdery mildew caused by Oidium sp. produces powdery white ash on leaves which later brown and wither. Control measures include dusting with finely powdered sulphur and use of resistant varieties.

Anthracnose is caused by Colletotrichum corchorum. The pathogen infects stem and pods producing necrotic lesions.

Stem gall is caused by Physoderma corchori Lingappa. Stem is infected and lesions are formed. Other diseases of tossa jute plants are black band disease caused by Diplodia corchori Syd., sclerotial disease caused Pellicularia rolfsii, and leaf spot caused by Phyllosticta sp.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
fiber

Tossa jute is also used for making hessian, sackings, linoleum bacing, carpets (in conjunction with cotton yarns), twines, roofing fabrics, laminated jute (as a substitute for metal and plywood), for sandbags, tents, water holding stores, webbings, theatrical canvas and scrims, kit bags, tool bags and luggage cover. For clothing material, jute is blended with wool, or other fibers for blankets, carpets, rugs and antiseptic surgical dressings.

MEDICINAL
Tossa jute leaves are eaten as potherb; they are stomachic, laxative, carminative, stimulant, demulcent, toxic and diuretic

Parts Used:

Stem, leaves, fibers.


Harvesting Practices:
As the plants begin to bloom, usually during August-September, whole plants are cut close to soil surface.

Jute harvesting has not been mechanised because crop is grown by small holders in relatively small plots. However, cultivating the crop in larger plots would allow mechanisation of harvesting

 

Produce and Products:
Produce - Jute fiber
Products - Jute Reinforced Plastic (JRP) and several items of utility.

Chemistry:
An average sample of tossa contains 56-53% cellulose, 22-26%, hemicellulose, 11-12% lignin and minor components such as fats, waxes, gum and minerals

Processing Techniques:
Cut stems are dried to eliminate foliage, ten tied into bundles called jaks for submergence in pools or ditches for retting. It takes 1-4 weeks to disintegrate the soft tissues binding the fibers. Although in India a number of bacteria responsible for retting have been isolated and provenable to hydrolyze the pectins as short a period as 5 days. Jute bundles have been found to ret better when steeped at a depth of 6-9 inches in slow flowing, clear water. The top layer is covered with weeds or other refuse and is submerged by weighting it down with logs of wood, clods of earth or various other locally available materials. Banana stems, freshly cut log of mango and bel or clods of earth used for weighing down the jute 'jaks' are rich in tannin and discolour the finished fibers. This also happens when jute is retted in the same water consecutively, as jute also contains tannin, C. olitorius having more of it. By using acquatic plants as cover, the jaks may be weighted down by stones, bricks or dry logs. During the hotter months retting is quicker. The addition of small amounts of ammonium sulphate and bone dust hastens retting considerably, particularly during colder months.

Manual separation of the fibers from the stalk follows retting, usually accomplished while jute stalks stand in the pond; the fibers float on the surface and are swished to from fragments of bark.

Other uses:
JRP is used in manufacturing of boxes for packing tea, apples and other horticultural produce, ready made garments and preservation of forests.

NIRJAFT (National Institute of Research on Jute and Allied fiber Technology), West Bengal as developed disposable jute carry bags with a cellulosic lining, a jute substitute of wool, an alternate process for growth of mushroom on jute waste, a technology for using jute pulp for manufacture of paperboards. Jute carry bags would replace the non-biodegradable plastic bags. Non-woven jute is used for separation of gravel and subsoils. It also has been used in controlling river bank erosion, protection of plants and moisture control in agricultural activities .

Jute is also used in interior decoration, manufacturing of footwears, garments and ornaments.

Economic potential to the country

India is No. 1 in global producer of jute, accounting for about 40% of the world's total output. India is the second largest exporter of jute and jute products after Bangladesh. Jute is a Rs. 6000 crore industry in India with annual exports of Rs. 450-600 crores.

In recent years, the demand for jute and jute products has been falling both domestically and the world over. The domestic demand in India is being forcibly sustained by a government policy which has made it mandatory to pack commodities such as food grains and sugar and to some extent fertilizers and cement in jute bags only. The world demand for jute arose mainly from carpet backing cloth which has also started falling with replacement of jute by synthetic materials.

In 1999, globally the area under jute was 19.48 lakh ha and the yield was 1708 kg/ha (10), was 8.74 lakh ha in 1996-97. According to the Ministry of Agriculture in India the area under jute was 7.55 lakh ha (1997-98) and the production of jute during 1997-98 was ca 9.7 million bales.

 

Export potential:

India's export of raw jute during 1997-98 was 11,420 M and of the value of Rs. 833.17 lakhs and the export of jute goods was of the value Rs. 395.49 crores .
