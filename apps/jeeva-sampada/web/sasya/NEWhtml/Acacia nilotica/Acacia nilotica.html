DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:
Small evergreen trees, up to 10 m tall, bark with black longitudinally fissure bark; branchlets grey, hairy when young. Leaves alternate, 2-pinnate, 5-10 cm long; main leaf bearing axis downy, with several glands; stipular thron straight, 0.6-1.0 cm long, sharp, white; pinnae 4-8 pairs, 3.5-5.0 cm long; leaflets 18-25 pairs, overlapping, linear-oblong, 4-5 mm long, rounded at apex.
REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:   
Flowers yellow, usually in solitary globose head of 1 cm across; sometimes 2 or 3 together in an axillary clusters. Pods short stalked, bead-like, compressed, constricted at sutures between seeds. Seeds 10-12, grey, hairy, 8-16 x 1.2-1.5 cm, horned at apex.

Economic Potential and Uses
UTILIZATION:
Uses: The gum is used medicinally as an inferior substitute for true Gum-Arabic. The bark and the pods supply a valuable tanning material. The green pods, young shoots and leaves are used as fodder.
Chemistry:
  • Gum of A. nilotica has 4 aldobiouronic acids, 4-O- (4-O-methyl- [ a ]-glucopyranosyluronicacid) - galactose, 6-O- (4-O-methoxy-[ b ]- glucopyranosyluronic acid)-galactose, 6-O- ([ b ] glucopyranosyluronic acid) - galactose and 4-O- ([ a ]- glucopyranosyluronic acid-galactose. (Anderson and Karmalla 1966).
  • Polyphenolic phlobaphenes consisting mainly of catechol and pyrogallol from bark. (Ishak, 1974) C.A. 1977, 86, 167855v).  
  •  Stearic acid, kaempferol-3-glucoside, isoquercitrin, leucocyanidin and an unidentified phenolic compound from flowers. (Gupta and Bokadia,1975) C.A.1976, 84, 40784 z.
  • Gallic acid, protocatechuic acid and leucocyanidin isolated from pods. (Adewoye and Rao,1977)C.A. 1978, 89, 87172 s.
  • Hentriacontane, lapachol, n-hentria control, b-sitosterol and paulwnin isolated from roots; octacosanol, b-amyrin, b-sitosterol and betulin from root bark and b-sitosterol and b-amyrin from root heart wood. (Rastogi and Mehrotra 1980-1984).  
  •   Caparic (0.12), lauric (0.07), myristic (0.32), palmitic (0.51). stearic(16.12), oleic (34.78), linoleic (44.07), and arachidic (4.01%) acids detected in seed fatty oil by GLC. (Rathee and Kaushal, 1983) C.A. 1984, 101, 35913 f.
  • Chemical analysis of pods revealed presence of carbohydrate (47.75), protein (1.67) and lipid (1.41%); glucose, galactose, fructose, maltose, aspartic acid, glutamic acid, glycine, alnnine, proline, lecucine and threinine isolated from seeds (Dahot et al., 1989) 32, 405; C.A. 1990. 112, 18885b.
  • Linoleic (32.2) and oleic (32.8%) acids determined seed oil (5.2%) by G.C.-MS.; traces of epoxy and hydroxy fatty acids also present (Maity and Mandal 1990) C.A. 1990, 113, 96338s.
  • Ethyl gallate, methyl gallate, gallic acid, m-digallic acid, catechol and ellagic acid isolated (Wassel et al., 1990) C.A. 1991, 114, 139780v.
  • Four derivatives of (+) Catechin-5gallate (I, II,III & IV) (Fig. 1) isolated from the acetone extract of the bark of A. nilotica (Malan. 1991)
  • Determination of a -spinasterol in volatile oil of Libyan plant (Ahmed et al., 1992) C.A. 1994, 120, 265798u.
  • Nutritional and antinutritional characteristics and biological value of Acacia nilotica (L.) Del. Seeds were investigated. The mature seeds contained 234 g kg-1, crude protein 126 g kg-1 crude fiber, 66.6 g kg-1, crude fat, 39.7g kg-1 ash and 534 g kg-1 carbohydrates. Potassium phosphorus, magnesium, iron and manganese occurred in high concentration. Cysteine and Methionine were the first limiting amino acids. When compared with the globulin fraction, albumins appeared to be a richer source of cystine, methionine, threonine ,lysine and tryptophan. Oleic and linoleic acids constituted the predominant fatty acid (66.9%) Anti nutritional components reduced while dry heating and autoclaving. In vitro protein digestibilities of raw, dry heat treated and autoclaved seeds were 61.2%, 77.4% and 80.2% respiration .Biological value, true digestibility and net protein utilization were significantly richer in processed seeds than in raw seeds.   (Siddhuraju et al., 1996).
  • Chemical analysis of A. nilotica leaves revealed a crude protein, crude fibre, ash, and soluble tannin content of 12.1, 30.5, 13.2 and 34% respectively. (Sotohy et al., 1997), CA. 1997, 127, 330 758s.
  • Phytochemical analysis of A. nilotica indicated that most active phase contained terpenoids and tannins and was devoid of alkaloid and saponins. (El-Tahir et al., 1999).
  • Two new flavonal glycosides namely 5-hydroxy-7, 4 ¢ -dimethoxy-8-isoprenyl flavone-3-O-B-D-Xylopyranoside (Fig. 2.1) and 5,7,3 ¢ ,4 ¢ -tetrahydroxy-6-8-2 ¢ -trimethoxyflavon-3-O-A-L-rhamnosyl (1 ® 2)-O-B-D-glucopyranoside (Fig. 2.2) have been isolated from the seed of A. nilotica.   (Chauhan et al., 2000).
  • Leaves and leaf stalk of A. nilotica has high ytterbium precipitated phenolics (Mean = 298 g/kg DM) as estimated colorimetrically by folin- Ciocalteu methods. (Dube, et al., 2001).  

Pharmacology:

  • Seeds exhibited hypoglycaemic effect in normal rats but were ineffective in alloxan diabetic rats. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).
  • Acetone, alcohol and aqueous extract of the fruits and stem bark of A. nilotica shows molluscicidal activity against the two snail species i.e. Bulinus truncatus and Biom phalpfeifferi which transmit schistosomiasis. (Hussein, 1985).
  • Dried powder of the pods and stem bark of A. nilotica act as vegetable molluscicides. (Hussein, 1985).
  • Seeds of A. nilotica showed molluscicidal activity, which gave 100% kill against Biomphalaria pfeifferi. (Kloos, 1987).
  • A. nilotica has high thermal efficiency. Suspended particulate matter concentration for one-hour combustion of A. nilotica fuel ranged between 850 and 2360 m g/m3. (Kandpal and Madan, 1996).
  • Phytoconstituents such as flavanoids, polysaccharides and organic acid of A. nilotiaca were responsible for the anti-inflammatory activity. (Dafallah, 1996).
  • A. nilotica effects the digestion coefficient of nutrients and its antibacterial properties showed reduction in total bacterial percentage (Sotohy et al., 1997). C.A. 1997, 127, 330758s.
  • Extract of A. nilotica has antiplatelet aggregatory activity, which is mainly due to blocked of Ca2+ channels through membrane, although evidence also suggests the involvement of protein kinase C. (Shah et al., 1997).
  • Diffusates from various part of A. nilotica appeared to be potential anti-microbial agents, which could be used for the management of citrus canker disease. (Afzal, et al., 1997).
  • The methanolic extract of its pod caused a dose dependant fall in arterial blood pressure i.e. anti-hypertensive and has antispasmodic activity. (Gilani et al., 1999).
  • Aqueous extract of A. nilotica displayed calcium involved sustained dose related contractile activity (Amos et al., 1999).   
  • Anti-plasmodial activity of ethyl acetate extract of A. nilotica possessing IC (50) values less then 5 microg/ml on both Plasmodium falciparum strain 3D7 (Chloroquine sensitive) and Dd2 strains (Chlroquine Resistance and Pyrimethamine sensitive) (El-Tahir et al., 1999).
  • Methanolic extract of A. nilotica (Bark and Pods) and aqueous extract of its pod showed considerable inhibitory effects against HIV-1 PR. (Hussein et al., 1999).  
  • A. nilotica has low toxicity potential at 2% and 8% levels in rats for 2 to 4 weeks. Significant decrease in body weight, haemoglobin, and serum total protein and total cholesterol was observed. However, these effects were reversed after one-week treatment termination and showed no significant changes in serum parameters of hepatic renal function, fasting glucose, triglycerides were observed. Further no histopathological changes in liver sections and no death in treated animal. (Al-Mustafa, 2000).
  • Methanol extracts of different part of A. nilotica showed inhibition at 100 microg/ml on hepatitis C virus protease. (Hussein et al., 2000).
  • A. nilotica is used in the treatment of sexually transmitted disease. Methanol extract significantly inhibits both gram positive and gram-negative bacteria, acetone extract inhibit most of the species, while water extracts is less active than methanol and acetone extracts. (Kambizi and Afolayan, 2001).

Other uses:

 Edibles (Bark, Bhandari, 1974, Vartak, 1959, Datar and Vartak, 1975; Fruit, Harbhajan,             1945). Cordage (Fibre, Mittre, 1981).  

  • Symbolic in totems, worshiped (Agarwal, 1981; Sengupta, 1965).

  • Acacia nilotica is used on marginal lands for fuelweed production and improving soil condition. (Rawat et al.,1997).

  • Soda anthraquinone pulp from A. nilotical has the best pulping potentialities for papermaking and addition of guar gum as wet end/dry strungth improved both bonding and tear properties of A Q pulps. (Khristova and Karar, 1999)

  • Acacia nilotica possess high concentration of polyphenoles, which caused immobilozation of N in both leaves and manure. (Mafongoya et al., 2000).

  • Nutritive evaluation of leaves of A. nilotica    by chemical and sacco method showed its potential use of protein supplements for grass or crop residues in dry season. (Ramana et al., 2000).
