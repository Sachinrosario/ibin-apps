HABIT & HABITAT:

Shrub or small, much branched, evergreen tree, common in sub-Himalayan tract ascending sometimes to 1500 m altitude. Occurs throughout India.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

A small, much branched tree; branchlets, young leaves and inflorescence tawny- or rusty-pubescent. Leaves alternate, variable, ovate or ovate-lanceolate, acuminate, entire or slightly toothed, glabrous above, pubescent and with numerous orbicular red glands beneath, reticulately veined, base rounded or acute, strongly 3-nerved at the base and 4-7 pairs of nerves above the basal ones; petioles cylindric, fulvous-pubescent with two small sessile glands one on each side of the summit. Flowers dioecious, small; males clustered, sessile or very shortly pedicellate, in erect terminal spikes, usually several together and often longer than the leaves. Female sessile or nearly so, in short spikes.

Male flowers: Sepals 4 (rarely 5), lanceolate, acute. Stamens numerous. Bracts broadly ovate, acute. Buds globosely ovoid. Female flowers: Calyx divided nearly to the base; sepals 3 or 4, thicker than in the male, ovate, lanceolate. Ovary with red glands, 3-celled; styles 3, simple papillose. Capsules 3-lobed, loculicidally 3-valved, covered with a bright-red powder consisting of minute stellate hairy and fine grains of a red resinous substance soluble in alcohol and ether. Seeds subglobose, black.

Floral and seed biology:

Natural reproduction takes place by seeds which fall to the ground in the beginning of the hot season and germinate in the ensuing rainy season. The seeds are susceptible to drought and insect attack; where loose bare soil is available, seeds get covered during rains and germination is facilitated. Artificial propagation is done by sowing fresh seeds by about April. Germination is rather uncertain and it is advisable to sow seeds quite close, c. 5 cm apart, and to thin out during the first rainy season. The more vigorous seedlings are ready for transplanting during the first year; smaller ones may be kept in the field for another year in the nursery. Line sowing with field crops has proves successful. Regular weeding and loosening of soil should be carried out during first two years and as often as may be necessary afterwards. The tree also reproduces from root suckers. The growth is comparatively slow.

MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:

The tree is subject to attack by several rot-causing fungi, Fomes conchatus (Pers.) Gillet, F. rimosus Berk., F. caryophylli (Rao) Bres., Hexagonia discopoda Pat & Har., Poluporus adustus (Willd.) Fr., Polystictus hirsutus Fr., P. steinheilianus Berk., Stereum hirsutus (Willd.) Fr., Ganoderma applanatum (Pers.) Pat., G. leucopheus Mont. and Tramates personii Fr. The heartwood is damaged by the borer, Aphrodisium cantori Hope. Sapwood of felled and dead timber is liable to attack by Monochamus bimaculatus Gahan, Xylotrechus smei L. & G., Agrilis malloti Thery and Sinoxylon spp., the attack is secondary. Lyctus africans Les. and Stromatium barbatum F. attack dry wood.

Phyllachora malloticola Seshadri – tar spot

Fomes connatus (Weinm.) Gill.

Fomes robiniae – heart rot

Phyllosticta marmorata Cke. – leaf infection from Dehra Dun (U.P.)

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts Used: Bark, leaves and fruits.

Fuel: Wood used as fuelwood. Calorific value: sapwood - 4,835 cal, 8.704 Btu.

Wood suitable for rafters, tool handles, match boxes and small turnery articles. Yields light timber.


Harvesting Practices: Coppices vigorously and the growth of coppice shoots is more rapid than of seedling plants.

 

Produce and Products:

Dyes, Oils and Tannins.

Processing Techniques:

Difficult to saw, but can be worked.

Other uses:

Tree valued as the source of dyeing material, known as Kamala, used in dyeing of silk and wool.

Kamala powder also used as anthelmintic. It also has purgative properties. Used for external application in parasitic affections of the skin.

Recently, the oil extracted from the seeds has got attention as a substitute for tung oil in the formulation of quick-drying paints and varnishes.

Kamala may be employed as an anti-oxidant for ghee, vegetable oils and shortenings.

It has also been used for coloring food-stuffs and beverages.

Also used as sindhur or kumkum by Indian women.

Leaves used as fodder.Leaves and bark yield tannin.

All parts of tree used in external application for skin diseases.

Fruits - Yield a red dye of local importance.

Miscellaneous Information

Plant is medicinally important.

Wood: Whitish to light reddish grey, often with darker streaks, somewhat lustrous. Heartwood not distinct, fairly close- and straight-grained, medium fine and even textured, hard, moderately tough and heavy, 48 lb./ c ft., warps and shrinks badly.
