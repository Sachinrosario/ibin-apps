HABIT & HABITAT

A  sub-deciduous tree seldom leafless, constitutes about a major part of the total forest area. Found extensively in parts of  North-East and Central India.

Shorea robusta flourishes best in deep, well-drained, moist, slightly acid sandy to clayey soils.  It does not tolerate water logging.  The most favourable soil is a moist sandy loam with good subsoil drainage.  Availability of soil moisture is an important factor determining the occurrence of S. Robusta.  

In economic terms, S. robusta is the most important timber genus in the Asian humid tropics.

Sal is a gregarious species and it forms the dominant composition in the forests where it occurs.  It grows well in a well-drained, moist, sandy loam soil.  It is a moderate to slow growing species and can attain a height of about 30-35m and a girth of about 2 to 2.5m in about 100years under favourable conditions.

Of the 2 factors of habitat, climate and soil, the former decides the general distribution of S. robusta; among the climatic factors, rainfall is by far the most important. Annual precipitation normally comes with a dry season lasting 4-8 months (monsoon climate). At higher elevations, S. robusta can be damaged by frost.

S. robusta occurs in both deciduous dry and moist forests and in evergreen moist forest. It accounts for about 14% of the total forest area in India. For example, southwest Bengal harbours luxuriant S. robusta forests. Fire is normally responsible for its frequent occurrence in pure stands or as the dominant species of mixed stands, as S. robusta is better equipped to survive conflagrations than other tree species.

Availability :  

Sal trees are found from Burma in the East, to Assam, Bengal, Nepal, the Deccan Plateau, going up to the foothills of the Shivaliks on the left bank of the Yamuna.  In Haryana, Sal can be found in the Morni Hills and the Kalesar forest.  Sal grows well in low height plains to foothills viz. Shivaliks from 200 to 1200m above mean sea level.  But sal growing in Nepal and Singhbhum district of Bihar are considered to be the best variety.

Sal forests occur in the central Indian belt (accounting for 90 percent of the Sal forests) and at the foot of the Himalayas. 

Sal has two main regions of distribution, one on the north and the other on the south of the Gangetic plain. The extreme north western limit of the northern region is in the Kangra district of Himachal Pradesh, but the main and almost continuous stretch  of sal forests commences in Ambala and extends along the sub-Himalayan tract and outer Himalayan as far  east  as the Darrang district in Assam and to Tripura. It is also found in the Garo hills, Nowgog Kamrup and Jaintia hills of Assam.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

Shorea robusta is a large, deciduous tree up to 50 m tall and with a dbh of 5 m; these are exceptional sizes, and under normal conditions S. robusta trees attain a height of about 18-32 m and girths of 1.5-2 m; bole is clean, straight and cylindrical, but often bearing epicormic branches; crown is spreading and spherical. Bark dark brown and thick, with longitudinal fissures deep in poles, becoming shallow in mature trees; provides effective protection against fire. The tree develops a long taproot at a very young age.

Leaves simple, shiny, glabrous, about 10-25 cm long and broadly oval at the base, with the apex tapering into a long point; new leaves reddish, soon becoming delicate green.

Flowers yellowish-white, arranged in large terminal or axillary racemose panicles.

Fruit at full size about 1.3-1.5 cm long and 1 cm in diameter; it is surrounded by segments of the calyx enlarged into 5 rather unequal wings about 5-7.5 cm long.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY


Breeding System :
S. robusta is a hermaphroditic, self-incompatible species.  Heavy flowering of the tropical timber genus Shorea has is usually correlated with the previous drought period. Beginning at about age 15, S. robusta bears fruit regularly every 2 years or so, and a good seed-bearing year can be expected every 3-5 years. Major seed dispersal agents include wind and water.

Phenology :

Flowering : March- April

Fruiting: May-July.  In Central India trees remain leafless for a short period between February and the onsets of rain. Pollen vectors in its natural habitat are insects from the family Thysanoptera.

Seed Biology :

Storage behaviour is recalcitrant; viability is lost within 2 weeks in open storage at room temperature; 10% germination on desiccation at 33-36 deg. C to 9.6% mc. There are about 400-1000 seeds/kg.

They fruit during summer and the seeds ripen from June to July.  These tend to germinate even while on the tree and accordingly begin to fall soon after appearance.

Sal seeds are collected from the time ripe fruit start falling in May and continued till the onset of rains. Proximate analysis of Sal seeds are given below, Moisture-5.23%, Protein-6.16%, Ether extract-16.77%, Crude fibre-4.81%, N-free extract-63.25% and Ash-3.78%.

Seedling Germination :
Shorea robusta regenerates naturally through seed and coppice. Direct sowing is the cheapest and best method of artificial propagation, although stump plantings, planting out entire plants with balls of earth, and planting out container-grown seedlings are also employed. Air-layering is also physiologically possible. Artificial regeneration of S. robusta is practiced to a major extent primarily within its native habitat. The seed germinates soon after falling off the tree if moisture conditions are favourable. The germination percentage of fresh seeds is 90-100%, but their viability falls to zero after just a few weeks. Germination is hypogenous. Generally, a seed produces a single seedling, but cases of polyembryony have also been reported. The primary root of the seedling is long, thick, terete, tapering and woody, and the stem is erect, wiry, very finely pubescent or glabrescent. Most commonly the seeds are planted 10-20 cm apart in rows with a spacing of 3-7 m.

Cultivation:
Young plants grow quickly, attaining top heights of up to 6m after 6 years.  The 1st thinning is usually performed after 5 years, and thereafter the trees are thinned every 5-10 years.  Rotations of 30-40years are used when coppice regeneration is practiced, and 80-160 years for high forest regeneration.  A species that requires a lot light and it coppices well.  Both coppice regeneration and planting of seedlings are used in plantation systems.  It is also ideally used for growing under taungya systems.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests: 

  • Defoliators and borers. Among the defoliators Lymantria mathura Moore and Maurilia iconica Wlk.
  • Major damage to both sapwood and heartwood is done by the members of the family Cerambycidae, specially Hoplocerambyx spinicornis Newm. and Aeolesthes holosericea Fabr.
  •  Resin - Hoplocerambyx spinicornis
 Loranthaceous parasites, Dendrophthoe falcata and Macrosolen cochinchinensis


Diseases: 

Many types of rot, particularly species of Daldinia, Ganoderma, Lentinus, Lenzites, Polyporus, Polystictus, Stereum,Trametes etc.

Fungal infection:  Great damage is done by fungal attack.

Fomes lignosus Klot.and Trametes cubensis  Mont.(Brown cubical rot), Fomes lividus Kaichb. (white fibrous rot),  Polystictus stenheilianus Berk. & Lov. (white spongy rot), Lentinus subnudus berk. (white spongy sap and heart rot), Stereum hirsutum (Willd) Fr.   (white spongy slash rot) and Polyporus ostreoformis  Berk. (brown fibrous rot).

Fomes carryophylli (Racib.) Bres. and Hymenochaete rubiginosa (Schnad). Lev.

Hypoxylon mediterraneum de Not. Var. microspora Millar is frequently associated with dying and dead sal.

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Wood, bark, seeds


Uses 

  • Resin: It is known as sal dammar or Bengal dammar.   It is an almost opaque, brittle, light yellow substance that hardens in a stalactic form; it is aromatic and is used as an incense in Hindu homes during religious ceremonies and a disinfectant fumigant; it is used in inferior quality paints, varnishes, shoe-polishes, carbon papers, typewriter ribbons and for caulking boats; it has certain medicinal properties and is used in the preparation of skin ointments. It is also used for caulking boats and ships.  
  • Resin is used in the  indigenous system of medicine as an astringent, detergent and given in diarrhea and dysentery. Ointment for skin diseases and in ear troubles.   
  • Sal resin yields an essential oil, known as Chua oil  
  • The bark yields a tannin and is not being used in large scale because, it is not giving desired softness and colour to the leather.  Possible use with myrobalans.  Wattle bark has replaced avaram and Sal barks to a greater extent. 
  • The, oilcake, though rich in tannins(5-8%, has been used in proportions of up to 20% in concentrates for cattle without detrimental effects.  As the protein remains remains completely undigested, the oilcake yields energy only.  
  • Dye and Tan: Bark is used for the preparation of the red and a black dye.  The bark has long been used locally as a tan and it is to be feared that in dyeing it is more used as an auxiliary than as a dye yielding stuff.  As a tan it is much valued, being generally used along with Terminalia, Mimusops and Phyllanthus or with, in addition, the bark of Ficus religiosa, the babul and the mango. 

Harvesting Practices
Sal is often tapped for dammar - an oleo-resin best known as “ral” or “lal-dhuma’ used in India.   Ral is known to occur in lumps at the base of the tree or even in the ground beneath the tree.

This resin has been used in various parts of India since earliest times.  Cutting narrow strips of bark and in about 12 days the grooves get filled with resins. The tree is blazed and the resin exudes out.  It solidifies quickly on exposure.  Freshening is done from time to time by scrapping of the hardened resin from the blaze.  Each mature tree on an average will yield up to 5kg of resin each year.  Resin also exudes naturally from injuries caused by the Sal heartwood borer.  Studies have shown that the upper blazes yield more resin than that of lower ones; maximum yield is obtained from September to November and more yield is obtainable if the blazes are freshened after every 2 weeks rather than after every month. Three crops are obtained per year(July/Oct./Jan.). The first collection in July is the best quality.  

In some parts of the country, people used to ring the trees and collect the resin daily as it exuded, and in this way large extents of forest, chiefly in Central India, were ruined before the protective operations of forest department were brought into force.

About  5 kg of resin can be obtained annually from a good mature tree. Natural exudation also occurs from trees which are unhealthy or are injured by the heartwood borer.

The indigenous method of tapping consists in making deep gashes into the wood. 

 


Produce and Products
Resin, ( known commercially as Sal dammar or Bengal dammar), oils, tannin, Dye

Chemistry
The resin usually occurs in small rough pieces, nearly opaque and very brittle; but " in some parts of the Upper Tista forests, large blocks, often 30 to 40 cubic inches in size, are found in the ground at the foot of the trees.  The exudation ;has no taste or smell, a specific gravity of 1.097 - 1.123, is easily fusible, to a small extent soluble in alcohol, almost entirely so in ether and perfectly in oil of turpentine and the fixed oils.  Sulphuric acid dissolves it, imparting a red colour to the solution.

Processing Techniques

The extract is manufactured in a way similar to that practiced for Catechu-makers in the Gonda forests.  After choosing a good site, within easy reach of water and of the bark used for boiling down, the huts of the men and the furnaces are erected.  The furnace, built of clay with walls 6 ;inches thick, 10ft to 15ft long an inner breadth of 2 ft 6 inches and a similar height, has an arched top, pierced with holes on both sides along its length, to hold the earthen pots in which the bark is boiled and has fireholes at each end and at one side, for introducing fuel and raking out ashes.  When the furnace is finished, the work of collecting and chipping the bark is begun.  It is usually stripped off in pieces, 3 ft long and conveyed to the encampment where it is cut into smaller pieces 3 inches by 4 inches.  The earthen pots half-full of chips are filled with water till three-quarters full and then placed on the furnace to boil, after which they are allowed to simmer for one and half an hours, till the liquor is a very deep red colour.  It is then strained off into a fresh pot, the chips are thrown aside and when dry are used for the furnace as fuel.  Then the strained liquid is reduced to half its quantity by an hours boiling.  The liquor gradually becomes stiff and great care must be taken to keep it from burning and as soon as it reaches the consistency of treacle it is removed and poured into a trough.  When the trough is full the contents are allowed to cool during the  night and next morning are poured into a circular pit 3 ft wide and 3 ft deep and so on with each day's outturn till the pit is full and a new one has to be dug.

Other Uses
  1. Timber - constructional works, heartwood (durable), sleeper wood, overhead electric telegraph and telephone lines. Piles, mine work and pit-props, bridges, dug-out boats, carriage and wagon building, spokes, fellows and hubs of wheels, agricultural implements, tool handles, tent pegs, liquid storage vats. Fuel wood.
  2. Bark - Fuel, sal bark along with leaves and twigs is a promising tanning material. Tanneries, filler, cellulose from spent bark suitable for making wrapping paper.
  3. Seed and seed oil - seeds are eaten after roasting . Seed yields 19-20% fatty oil contained in cotyledons. Fat is obtained  by boiling husked seeds in water and skimming off the oil. Oil is used in cooking, lighting and adulterating ghee. Soap making and in chocolates.
  4. Sal oilcakes contains 10-12% protein, 50% starch and can be used as cattle and poultry feed. Sal seed cake can constitue upto to 10% of poultry and pig rations without changes in performance.  A kind of oil is also misused for adulterating ghee.  
  5. Leaves - foddar, pattal, donnas. In Ayurvedic, the leaves are considered anthelmintic and alexiteric. They are used to treat wounds and ulcers, itching, leprosy, cough, earache and headache.
  6. Sal flowers are good source of nectar for honey bees.
  7. The powered stem bark or bark paste is applied to stop bleeding and promote healing of cuts among the tribal inhabitants of Southern  Bihar and the Kondhs of South Western Orissa. It is reputed to be aphrodisiac and useful for the treatment of gonorrhoea.
  8. Further, during famine poor people grind the fruit into flour and eat it to ward off starvation. A paste made from the fruit is sometimes used to treat diarrhoea.
  9. In India, seeds are boiled into a porridge with flowers of Bassia latifolia and fruits of Dolichos biflorus.  In Madras, seeds are ground into a coarse flour used to make bread, and the plant is used as a famine food.
  10. Shorea robusta butter, used in cooking, is derived from the seeds.  A de-fatted kernel powder, popularly known as sal seed cake, contains about 50% starch, in addition to proteins, tannins and minerals.  The physico-chemical property of the starch can be exploited for preparing canned food products.
  11. In  India, S. robusta is lopped for fodder, but the leaf fodder is considered to be of medium to poor quality.  The oil cake, though rich in tannins(5-8%), has been used without detrimental effects in concentrates for cattle in proportion up to 20%.  As the protein remains completely undigested, the oil cake yields only energy.  Sal seed cake can also constitute up to 10% of poultry and pig rations without affecting the performance of these animals.  The leaves can be used as roughage for cattle and are fed to Antherae  mylitta, , a tasar silk worm.
  12. Shorea robusta leaves are widely used for making leaf plates and cups for both home use and sale to Sal plate factories.
  13. Food: In India seeds are boiled into a porridge with flowers of Bassia latifolia and fruits of Dolichos biflorus. In Madras, India, seeds are ground into a coarse flour used to make bread, and the plant is used as a famine food. The chemical composition of the seeds consists of 10.8% water, 8% protein, 62.7% carbohydrate, 14.8% oil, 1.4% fibre and 2.3% ash. S. robusta butter, used in cooking, is derived from the seeds. A de-fatted kernel powder, popularly known as sal seed cake, contains about 50% starch, in addition to proteins, tannins and minerals. The physico-chemical property of the starch can be exploited for preparing canned food products.
  14. Fodder: In India, S. robusta is lopped for fodder, but the leaf fodder is considered to be of medium to poor quality. The oil cake, though rich in tannins (5-8%), has been used without detrimental effects in concentrates for cattle in proportions of up to 20%. As the protein remains completely undigested, the oil cake yields only energy. Salseed cake can also constitute up to 10% of poultry and pig rations without affecting the performance of these animals. The leaves can be used as roughage for cattle and are fed to Antheraea mylitta, a tasar silk-producing worm.
  15. Lipids: S. robusta seed oil fat has become a significant foreign exchange earner for India.
  16. Timber: The dark, reddish brown, hard and heavy heartwood (specific gravity of 0.83-0.93 cm³) is very durable and highly resistant to termite attack; grain is strongly spiralled and rather coarsely structured; seasoning also presents problems. Wood is easy to saw, but because of its high resin content, it is difficult to plane and turn; it has a tendency to split when nails are driven into it.
  17. This important Indian hardwood is especially well suited for structures subject to heavy stress in house construction, hydraulic engineering, ships and railway cars. It is also used for poles, railway ties and posts, simple interior finishing such as window frames and floors, and many other applications. For making household or agricultural implements, S. robusta coppice shoots are used.
  18. Other products: S. robusta leaves are widely used for making leaf plates and cups for both home use and sale to sal plate factories.
  19. Intercropping: In Assam, India, artificial regeneration of S. robusta is practised in combination with crops such as upland rice, maize, sesame and mustard. Good results have also been achieved with mixed plantations in which S. robusta is cultivated together with Tectona grandis.

Export Potential 

In 1992 about 300 tones of Sal oil was exported to Japan for cocoa butter extender at a FOB price of US$ 1150/tones. The deoiled Sal seeds are used for cattle feed and its market price is US$ 60/tones and about 48000 tones which is about 70% of the total production was exported.

Miscellaneous Information

Religious aspects: Sal dammar is widely used as an incense, especially as an ingredient of Samagri  which is burnt in religious ceremonies and cremation rites; it emits copious white fumes.

The sal tree is an object of worship among Buddhists and Hindus in India and the adjoining countries. The legend has it that the famous Lumbini tract where Lord Buddha had sat for meditation and acquired salvation constituted a thick forest of Sal trees.

The small branches of  Sal are used by Indian villagers to detect witches;
