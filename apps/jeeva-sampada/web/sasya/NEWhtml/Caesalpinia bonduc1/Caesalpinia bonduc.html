HABIT & HABITAT:
 Scandent or climbing, armed shrub; stem and branches hairy, armed with hooked, yellow spines. It is found in Himachal Pradesh, Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, West Bengal, Sikkim, Assam, Tripura, Manipur, Orissa, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Laccadives, Andaman & Nicobar Islands.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:
Branchlets glossy, black, armed with recurved prickles at the base of pinnae and elsewhere pinnae 6-11 pairs leaflet stalked coriaceous, base rounded to acute, apex mucrovate, margins curved, upper surface shining, lower dull. Flowers: yellow, fragrant, in axillary and terminal racemes 30-60 cm. Long. Pods: somewhat swollen, dark brown to black, oblong, 1 or 2 seeded beaked, seeds orbicular or ovoid to reniform, black, polished hard.            


REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:
Inflorescence: Long peduncled, terminal, and supra-axillary racemes; Flower: pedicillate, bracteate, yellow, hermaphrodite; Calyx: fulvious-hairy, lobes obovate, obtuse, segments 5, imbricate; Corolla: Petals oblanceolate, yellow,imbricate, sub-equal ;Androecium: Stamens 10, free, filaments declinate, flattened at the base, clothed with white silky hairs; Gynoecium: Ovary sessile, ovules few, style filiform, sometime clavate at the apex, stigma terminal; Fruit:  Pods shortly stalked, oblong, with wiry prickles; Seed: 1-2, oblong, lead coloured . Flowering & Fruiting : July-September. Flowers are pollinated by insects. Seeds are dispersed by  wind, bird and animals.

Economic Potential and Uses
UTILIZATION:
Parts used and Uses:
Seeds-tonic, antirheumatic, autidiarrhoeal, leaf and bark-emmenagogue, anthelmintic; oil from seed – emolient, used as embrocation to remove freckles from the face, leaf, bark and seed febrifuge. Leaves are bitter and used as an emmenagague. Root is used in dysentery

NBPGR:
Seed: bitter substance phytosterinin, bonducin, saponin, fatty oil 20-24% starch, sucrose, 2-phytosterols; bitter amorphous glycoside bonducin isolated from the oil, bitter principle ineffective against bird malaria.

Fatty acid triglycerides have been identified as the macrofilaricidal principles from the seed kernel of Caesalpinia bonduc. Fatty acids comprising these triglycerides have been identified as palmitic, stearic, octadeca-4-enoic and octadeca-2,4-dienoic acids. (Rastogi et al, 1996). MAPA 1996, 9605-3076.

Four Cassane furanoditerpenes designated bonducellpins A (fig. 7a), B (fig. 7b), C (fig. 7c) and D (fig. 8) were isolated from the roots of Caesalpinia bonduc. The 1H and 13C-NMR spectra of all four compounds were completely assigned by using a combionation of 2DNMR experiments which includes Cosy, HM2C, HMBC and NOESY sequences. (Peter et al, 1997). C.A. 1997, 127, 343489x

A new diterpenoid named bondenolide was isolated from the ethylacetate soluble part of methanol extract of Caesalpinina bonduc seeds. The triterpenes a-amyrin, b-amyrin, lup-20(29)-en-3b-ol, and the sterol b-sitosterol and b-sitosteryl galactoside were also isolated. (Ahmad et al, 1997). C.A. 1997, 127, 15465v

A new rearranged cassane furanoditerpene caesalpinin B (fig. 9) was isolated from the roots of caesalpinina bonduc. Structures was established on the basis of stereoscopic data. (Lyder et al, 1998). C.A. 1998, 129, 242519v.

Four known triterpenoids a-amyrin, b-amyrin, lup-20(29)-en-3b-ol and b-sitosteryl galactoside were isolated from caesalpinia bonduc. (Ali et al, 1997).

Seven cassane diterpenes, including the known caesaldekarin A, were isolated from the roots of Caesalpinia bonduc, collected in Barbados. The 1H and 13C NMR spectra of all seven compounds were completely assigned by using a combination of 2D NMR experiments, which included 1H-1H COSY, HSQC, HMQC, and HMBC sequences. (Lyder et al, 1998).

Oil was extracted from Caesalpinia crista using Soxhlet techniques. The oil contained b-carotene plant sterols, glycolipids and phospholipids. Toxicological studies reveals the presence of some toxins gossypol, mytoxin, cyanogenic glucosides as well as unconventional fatty acids – cyclopropane, epoxy and hydroxy fatty acids. The seed contained important phytochemical tannins,saponins, glycosides and reducing sugars. Fiber and carbohydrate contents of the seed were equally high. The plant could find important and industrial applications, especially in cosmetic industry. (Njoku et al, 1999). C.A. 2000, 133, 271471s.

New cassane diterpenes named neocaesalpins C and D were isolated from the Philippine crude drug calumbibit botanically originating from the seeds of Caesalpinia bonduc (Fabaceae), and their structures were elucidated on the basis of the spectroscopic evidence. These compounds are characterized by the presence of the alpha, beta-butenolide moiety. (Kinoshita, 2000).


Pharmacology:
The calcium dependency and the cholinergic effect of the leaf extract of Caesalpinia bonduc Roxb. (Caesalpiniaceae) was studied in isolated pregnant rat myometrium preparations. Isometric contractions were recorded. The extract (Cebo) increased the contractile force in the isolated strips in a concentration-dependent manner. The effects were comparable to those obtained with acetylcholine. Contractions induced by Cebo or acetylcholine were inhibited in the presence of atropine. The stimulating action of Cebo on the contractile responses of isolated myometrium preparations inhibited by atropine may be mediated by cholinergic receptors. In calcium-free solution Cebo induced a tonic contraction (contracture) of the muscle. Moreover, in high-potassium calcium-free solution Cebo caused contracture of the uterine smooth muscle. Cebo was still able to elicit contractions in calcium-free solution containing EDTA or EGTA. These findings suggest the existence of cholinergic receptors sensitive to Cebo which could influence the influx of calcium (phasic contraction) and mobilization of calcium from cellular stores (tonic contraction), both of which are responsible for the increase of contractile activity and development of the contracture of uterine smooth muscle. (Date et al, 1998).

The oil was extracted fro Caesalpinia bonduc seeds using n-hexane as solvent. The oil content in the seeds was 35.7%. Some physicochemical characteristics were determined which showed antibacterial activity against he gram-negative bacteria Pseudomonas aeruginosa. (Raman and Muthumuniasamy, 2000). C.A. 2000, 133, 190471q.

Bondenolide from Caesalpinia bonduc showed antibacterial and antifungal activities. (Simin et al, 2001). C.A. 2001, 135, 369237m.

The antibacterial and antifungal activities, along with a phytotoxicity test of the newly isolated diterpene bondenolide, of a methanol extract, ethylacetate fraction and water soluble part of the methanol extract of Caesalpinia bonduc were assayed. (Simin et al., 2001).
