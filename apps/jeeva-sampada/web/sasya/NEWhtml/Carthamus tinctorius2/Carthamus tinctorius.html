Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Safflower is an errect growing annual herb presenting a thistle like appearance. The three centres of origin, as suggested by Vavilov for safflower are India, Afghanistan and Ethopia. The important safflower growing countries, besides India, are the USA, Mexico, Ethopia, Spain, USSR and Australia. In India, it occupies 590,000 hectares with a production of nearly 130,000 tonnes. Distributed throughout the country. Over 98% of the area is concentrated in the states of Maharashtra (64.4%), Karnataka (26.0%) and Andhra Pradesh (8.0%) (1).

It thrives in heavy clay soil with good waterholding capacity, but will grow satisfactorily in deep sandy or clay loams with good drainage, and needs soil moisture from planting to throughout flowering, Soils approaching neutral pH are best. Safflower is reported to tolerate annual precipitation of 2.0 to 13.7 dm., annual temperature of 6.3 to 27.5 deg.C., and pH of 5.4 to 8.2.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
The safflower plant is highly branched, herbaceous, thistle-like, varying in height from 30 to 160 cm. It has many branches each terminating in a flower. Taproot long and stout. Early growth is slow, young plants have rosette habit, later branching from near base. Branches stiff, cylindrical, whitish in colour. Leaves spirally arranged, sessile, oblong to ovate-lanceolate, dark green, glossy, 10-15 x 2-4 cm, with spines along margin and at tip (10). The inflorescence is broad, flat or slightly curved and densely bristled owing to the presence of numerous floral bracts (3). Plants are glabrous and pubescent leaves alternate, rigid with un-armed spinulose serrate margins. Each branch including main stem ends up in a flower head (4).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Capitulum terminal, 2.5-4 cm in diameter, with spreading outer leafy spiny bracts and inner triangular bracts, spine-tipped, forming a conical involucre, with small opening at tip, through which 30-90 florets protrude (10). Flowers are florets tubular varying from yellow to red in colour and hermaphrodite containing 20 individual florets, each of which produces a seed. Sometimes a few of the marginal florets are sterile. Bracts and bracteoles of each floret are modified into thin soft hairs. Calyx absent, corolla long tubular, arising on the inferior ovary. Corolla tube about 4 cm long with 5 pointed segments expanded above tube, staminal tube exserted, bright yellow, with 5 united anthers with introrse dehiscence, style pushes up through staminal tube, after which 2-lipped stigmas open. Stamens five and epipetalous, ovary bicarpellary, single celled, single ovuled. First the florets on the main branch open, flowering period varies from 3-4 weeks. In each head the florets at the margin open first and the flowering continues centripetally to the centre (4).

Breeding System :
Safflower is both self and cross-pollinated crop. The florets are protogynous, the stigma extrudes through the tubular corolla before the florets open, as soon as the florets open the anthers burst (4). The florets open in the morning hours between 8 a.m. to 10 a.m.

Phenology :
The crop is grown during Rabi primarily as a rainfed crop, but in some areas it is raised under irrigation. Duration of crop is around 120-130 days. Flowering starts 55 days after sowing.

Pollinators:
Cross-pollination occurs through wind and insects.

Back to Top

 

Seed Biology :
Safflower reproduces by seed. The seed rate depend on the nature of the crop (pure or mixed). Generally 5-12 kg of seeds are required for raising of the crop in one hactare with a spacing of 60 cm between rows and 30 cm between plants (2). Achenes white or pale grey, shining, 4-angled, about 8 mm long, with 30-50 per cent hull; pappus absent.

Seedling Germination :
Practically no germination occurs at 36°F. The average time for germination at 41°, 48°, and 600 F was 16,9, and 4 days respectively (10).

Cultivation:

Safflower is grown mostly as a mixture with wheat, barley, gram and rabi jowar, three rows of safflower being planted after every nine, 12 or more rows of the main crop. Sometimes, it is sown as border rows surrounding the crops of wheat, rabi jowar, etc., because being spiny, it protects the main crop against cattle trespass. As a pure crop, it is rotated with wheat, gram and rabi jowar. Occasionally, it is grown as a second crop after any quick-maturing kharif crop, e.g. green-gram (moong), black-gram (mash), groundnut, coriander, etc.

The number of ploughings should be as few as possible and be aimed at preserving the maximum amount of soil moisture. One or two ploughings with a country plough will be sufficient to remove the weeds and break the clods. Sowing is generally done in September and October. The seed-rate adopted varies from 5 to 12 kg per hectare, depending upon the soil fertility and the nature of the crop (pure or mixed). When grown in strips, a row spacing of 45 cm is adopted. Plants too close tend to have thinner stems or wear a superficial root system, with a reduced number of flowers. In general, the pure crop is given one or two weedings, combined with hoeing on the 2Oth and the 45th day after sowing. The application of 20-40 kg of N per hectare results in a substantial increase in yield. The mixed crop shares the preparatory tillage, manuring and cultivation given to the main crop. When the plants have developed the central flowering head, they are topped to promote branching, flowering and seed production (1, 11).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

1. Aphids (Dactynotus carthami)

Symptoms: Aphids suck the sap from the tender leaves and twigs, and stunt the plants

Control measures: Spray Fenithion 0.1%


2. Bud Fly (Acanthiophilus helianthi)

Symptoms: Maggots feed on the developing florets and on the thalamus. The flower buds fail to open. Retarded in growth and give a burnt-up appearance

Control measures: Spray 0.07% Endosulfan.


3. Leaf thrips (Frankliniella sulphurea)

Symptoms: The thrips suck the sap from the leaves and make them look crinkled.

Control measures: Spray 0.03% Dimethoate.


4. Safflower caterpillars (Perigaea capensis, Eublemma rivula)

Symptoms: Larvae eat leaves and also bore into the stem and flower buds

and feed on the contents

Control measures: Dust carbaryl 10% or Endosulfan.


Diseases:

1. Bacterial blight (Pseudomonas syringae)

Symptoms: Dark water soaked lesions appear on the stem and leaf petioles

Control measures: Spray the crop with Steptocycline 500 ppm two or three times


2. Fusarium wilt (Fusarium oxysporum)

Symptoms: The disease spread through the vascular tissue of the stem, branches and leaves. Plants wilt and die.

Control measures: Rotation with other crops


3. Root rot (Phytophthora drenchsleri)

Symptoms: Wilting plants become light coloured and dies

Control measures: Grow the crop in well-drained soil


4. Rust (Puccinia carthami)

Symptoms: Chestnut brown pustules appear on cotyledons, leaves and bracts

Control measures: Treat the seed with Captan or Thiram @ 3 g/kg of seed.


5.Verticillium wilt (Verticillium alboatrum)

Symptoms: Plants wilt and dry up earlier than normal.

Control measures: Rotation with other crops is effective (1).

Economic Potential and Uses

The crop is now cultivated primarily for its seeds which yield oil. The oil content varies from 24 to 36 per cent, depending on the variety, soil, climate and other conditions. The cold-pressed oil is golden yellow and is used for culinary purposes, or for making soap. The oil obtained by dry hot distillation is black and sticky and is used only for greasing well ropes and leather goods exposed to water (1). It contains a higher percentage of essential unsaturated fatty acids and a lower percentage of saturated fatty acids than other edible vegetable seed oils. The oil, light colored and easily clarified, is used in salad and cooking oils, margarines, liqueurs, candles, and as a drying oil. Safflower oil lowers blood cholesterol levels and is used to treat heart diseases (13, 14, 15).

Safflower oil has also good drying properties and is, therefore, used in the manufacture of paints, varnishes and linoleum and wax cloths. It can be mixed with white paint without any after-yellowing effects. The cake, particularly from decorticated seed, is used as a concentrated cattle feed, and that from undecorticated seed is sometimes used as a manure (1). The safflower oil is also used for manufacture of Roghan which is used for water proof cloth and preservation of leathers. A gelatinous material obtained by heating the oil for two hours to approximately 3000 C and then pouring hot oil into cold water, is used as glass cement and as a fixing material for ornamental stones and tiles.

Uses

The seeds are edible and are eaten after roasting. Young seedlings of safflower are useds as green vegetable in India. In India during ancient times, safflower was being cultivated for its dye, which was one of important cottage industry of the country. Tender shoots eaten as a salad and potherb. Seeds, both edible and nutritious, are eaten roasted or fried and used in chutney. Safflower dye despite the availability of synthetic dyes, is still used for colouring of ceremonial clothes, foods, toys. In recent years, again there is a revival of the safflower dye for colouring food in many countries as it is non-toxic as compared to several chemical colourings in food.

Safflower oil is nutritionally better than groundnut and mustard oil as it contains 55-80 percent linoleic acid. Besides, Vitamin A, D, K and E, presence of linoleic acid in the diet prevents coronary disease, bronchial asthma, kidney disorder and skin disease like eczema. The unsaturated fatty acid lower the serum cholesterol. So it is very useful for the patients suffering from heart disease (5).

Harvesting Practices
The crop matures in 4 or 5 months after sowing. The ripe plants are either cut or pulled out, stacked for a few days to dry, threshed by cutting with sticks, and the resulted material is winnowed to obtain clean seeds. The average yield of the pure crop is 400-500 kg of seed per hectare, whereas that of a mixed crop is about 100 kg.

Processing Techniques

Almost the entire produce of safflower seeds is processed to obtain edible oil in the developing world. In the developed nations including the United States, the extracted oil is mainly used to manufacture alkyd resins and various types of drying oils. In India, the oil is extracted mostly by traditional methods, while more efficient expeller pressing coupled with solvent extraction are employed in the United States. The seeds are often partially dehulled before oil extraction; however, crushing whole seeds without dehulling is also in practice.

The most popular method of extraction in India is by ghani, which is basically a large pestle and mortar. The mortar is an inverted cone, the pestle heavy baulk of timber seated at the bottom of the cone and inclined at an angle, so that meal is crushed against the mortar during rotation. The timber baulk is pulled against the side of the mortar by weight, and is rotated by a pole inserted through it at right angles. Power is applied by one or two bullocks yoked to the pole who walk blindfolded in a circle round the mortor. There are different types of ghani used in India, which include improved bullock-driven ghani, overhead-drive ghani and portable power-operated ghani (6, 13).

Other Uses

Seeds used for tumors, especially inflammatory tumors of the liver. Flowers considered diaphoretic, emmenagogue, laxative, sedative, stimulant, in large doses laxative and used as a substitute or adulterant for saffron in treating measles, scarlatina, and other exanthematous diseases. Charred safflower oil used for rheumatism and sores. Seeds, diuretic and tonic. In China, prescribed as uterine astringent in dysmenorrhea. In Iran, the oil is used as a salve for sprains and rheumatism (15).

Miscellaneous Information

The use of defatted safflower meal in human consumption is limited, primarily due to the presence of fibrous hull and two deleterious phenolic glucosides. The meal obtained from undecorticated seeds in expeller-press contains about 25 % fiber, the commercially defatted and partially decorticated meal contains about 15.4 % fiber while the defatted meal obtained complete dehulling contains about 5 to 6% fiber. All these meals are also known to contain two glucosides, namely 2-hydroxy arctiin and matairesinol monoglucoside, which exhibit, respectively, cathartic activity and a bitter taste. The phenolics can be effectively removed by extracting the meal with methanol, preparing protein isolates or treating the meal with beta glucoside. Both dialysis and water extraction at pH 5.0, although effective in removal of glucosides, may also remove significant proportions of dry matter of the meal. Methonal extraction removes these glucodides (6).
