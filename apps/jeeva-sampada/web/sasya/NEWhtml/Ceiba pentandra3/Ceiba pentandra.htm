HABIT & HABITAT:

Kapok is a medium-sized deciduous tree, it is found throughout the hotter parts of India, especially in Southern and Western India and the Andaman Islands. So far no effort has been made in India to cultivate it on a large scale. The plant is considered to be a native of America.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Kapok tree is 20-30 mt. In height with a clear level of 12 mt. and thick buttresses. Branches are horizontal, verticillate, armed with woody conical prickles when young. Bark is grayish brown.

Leave are digitately compound. Leaflets, 5-9, lanceolate-cuspidate, elliptic, acute, entire or serrate at apex, 1-10 cm long. Flowers white, yellowish or rose-coloured in axillary fascicles. Capsules oblong or cylindrical, pointed at both ends, upto 28 cm long. Seeds numerous, globose or obovoid, black, densely enclosed in long silk, unicellular hairs.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Flowers are in two-to-many flowered, axillary fasicles which are pedicelled and large. Differentiation of flower buds in Kapok takes place just before the trees lose their leaves at the beginning of the dry season. The flowers open about 15 minutes after sunset and remain open until the petals drop of late the next afternoon. The flowers are pentamerous; petals are shortly connate at the base; oblong-obovate, white, yellow or rose coloured. The stamens are pentadelphous, the phalanges bring connate at the base, white, 3-5 cm, long and rounded by one to three unfractuose anthers. The style is white, much censtricted and filiform at the base, suddenly dilated above the staminal tube, obscurely fine-lobed at the apex, and 2.5-3.5 cm long. The capsules are oblong or fusiform, narrowing towards, both ends, green, five-celled, many-seeded, 7.5 to 14 cm long and 3-4.2 cm. thick. When they are ripe, the capsules dehisce by fine values which separate upward from beneath. The valves are clothed with dense wood within. The septa are densely hairy, finally divaricate, and bearing seeds along the longitudinal axis. The seeds are obovoid, dark brown, glabrous and finely tuberculate, with a lateral air cavity within .

Fertilization is porogamous. Development of endosperm is of nuclear type. Seeds are large with massive seed coat.

Breeding system and varietal improvement:
Flowers are bisexual. As the flowers are wind-pollinated, usually early in the evening, a rain while they open is disastrous. The pollen grains germinate as soon as they reach the stigma of the flowers, and fertilization takes place within a few hours, provided the temperature does not fall below 20oC. At temperatures of 15oC, or lower, the growth of the pollen tubes is too slow for them to reach the ovules before the flower falls off the following day.

Propagation is by seeds or from cuttings . When cultivated on a plantation scale, Kapok is usually propagated by means of seed. Cuttings are made easily, even large branches used as fence posts generally root to form a hedge grow. For large plantations however, cuttings are impractical because of the difficulty entailed in obtaining sufficient plant material

Phenology: The tree flowers in December-January and fruits ripen in March-April


Pollinators: The flowers are wind pollinated

Floral and seed biology:
Seedling germination : Seed for sowing should, whenever possible, be specially obtained from the mature full sized pods of high yielding trees that are at least seven to eight years old, and not from seed which has been produced in the course of cleaning the floss. About 6lb of seeds are stated to be sufficient for plating 100 acres. The seeds should be planted 9 in. to 1 ft. apart in nursery beds about 4 ft. wide and raised about 6 in. The nursery must be carefully prepared, the soil being manured before hand if it is poor, and kept well weeded; in dry weather, watering may be necessary.

As soon as the seed has germinated, which takes only a few days, the seedlings should be shaded until they are about 5 or 6 in. high, when they should no longer be shaded but exposed to the sun. If the plants do not obtain plenty of sunshine they will grow tall and thin. The seedlings should, if necessary, be thinned out to 6 to 9 in. apart. When the seeds have been sown in hills, only one seedling should be allowed to each hill. The young plants should be taken to their permanent quarters when they are from eight to ten months old, and in lifting them great care should be taken to prevent damage to the roots-especially the tap-root. The crown is removed, leaving about 4 ft. of stem, which should be planted in the field without delay. This operation should be carried out, if possible, in rainy weather. In Indonesia the seedlings are planted out on the plantation at a distance of 16 to 18 ft. apart, but if other crops are to be grown in with the Kapok this distance is increased to about 24 ft.

As Kapok seeds germinate and grow rapidly, another method which can be used is to place three seeds in each hole, on land which has been properly cleared for planting, and where it is intended that the trees shall have their permanent site. About 2-3 months after the seeds have been planted out, the two less-promising of the seedlings from each hole are discarded, the best one being left to continue its growth. This method is stated to have its advantages in that there is no check to the plants when they are transplanted, and the cost of establishing a crop by this method is smaller. Once the seedlings have become established, they require very little attention, but the soil must be kept free from weeds

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
fiber -
Kapok fiber is light, brittle, elastic, lustrous and white or pale yellow in colour. Kapok finds use in bedding and upholstery industries, in the production of life saving equipment, and in the construction of thermally insulated and sound proof covers and walls. As a filling material it has the advantage overall the other stuffing materials in being light and reslient. It is used in filling mattresses, sleeping bags, pillows and cushions. As Kapok can be dry sterilized, it is used as a filling material for mattresses in hospital. It constitutes an excellent material for making padded surgical dressings.

On account of its buoyancy, freedom from water logging and weight bearing capacity, it is used in the manufacture of lifebuoys and belts, waist coats and other life saving appliances. Being impervious to water, it is no liable to fungus attack. Rotting of life belt covers, etc., stuffled with Kapok is very unusual.

During the war, Kapok was employed for insulating tanks, for lining aviation suits and for filling floats of army assault-bridges. It is also employed in the insulation of refrigerators, cold storage plants, offices, theatres, motor cars and aeroplanes and also for sound insulation in aeroplane cabins, broadcasting studios, hospitals, auditoriums and theatres as I is one of the best known sound absorbers.

Kapok was considered unsuitable for textile purposes; the fiber is brittle, smooth and slippery. Suitable machinery has been devised for carding, and kapok can now be spun either alone or in admixture with cotton into yarn suitable for the manufacture of plushes, lace and other articles. On account of its non-conducing character Kapok textiles find use as interlining in warm clothing. It has been used in sport and hunting jackets, yatching, fishing and aviation suits and for boxing gloves and bear protection. In U.S.A., base balls are filled with Kapok.

Seed Oil and Cake :

Refined Kapok seed oil is used for the same purposes as refined cotton seed oil. It is ine of the adulterants of peanut oil in China. The press cake and seed are used as feed for cattle. The cake is rich in constituents of manorial value . This oil is also used in making soaps.

Medicinal Uses :

The tree yields a dark-red, almost opaque-gum. It is astringent and is given in certain stages of bowel complaints. The root is diuretic. The young leaves are used as emollient.

 

Parts Used:

Pods, seeds, root, bark, leaves.


Harvesting Practices:
Kapok tree starts to produce fruits when it is about five to six years old, it does not reach its full bearing capacity until it is ten years old. The yield of floss, with seed, from one tree is between 10 and 40 lb. The fruits containing the floss can be picked within two or three months after flowering, so that the picking is continued until the beginning of June. The pods from which the floss is obtained are brought down, before they are ready to burst, by hooked knives attached to long holes. Owing to the fact that in India the trees are often in forest areas, which are not, easily accessible, the collectors tend to pick pods, which are at various stages of maturity, to avoid having to return to the area again within a short time. The fruits are dried in the sun and split open with mallets. The floss is taken out along with seeds, dried in the sun and the seeds separated by beating the dried material with sticks. In Java and in Phillippines machines are employed for cleaning the floss.

Produce and Products:
Kapok fiber - mattresses, pillows, cushions, life jackets, etc.
Kapok seed oil - soaps, edible oil.


Chemistry:
Kapok fiber contains 61-64% cellulose, the rest being lignin and other miscellaneous materials.

The percentages of fatty acids in the oil are : oleic, 43.0; linoleic, 31.3; palmitic, 9.77; stearic, 8.0; arachidic, 1.2; and lignoceric, 0.23.

Analysis of the seed cake gave the following values : moisture, 13.8; crude protein, 26.2; fat, 7.5; carbohydrate, 23.2; fiber, 23.2; and ash, 6.1%, nutrient ratio, 1; 1.5, food units,107.

Analysis of the wood gave the following values : moisture, 9.8; ash, 5.9; fats and waxes, 0.62; cellulose, 68.3; and lignone, 25.2%.

Processing Techniques : Where the floss is to be used locally and not for export, the seeds are removed from the floss by means of a 'charkhi'. This consists of a basket, earthenware jar, or some other suitable container, into which the Kapok is put, and a rod about 2 to 5 ft. long with sticks attached to it horizontally at one end. The rod is put into the basket, with the cross-sticks in the basket, and is made to revolve in alternate directions by rubbing it between the hands, or by causing it to revolve by means of a piece of string, so that the cross-sticks agitate the fiber and separate the seeds, which fall o the bottom of the basket and are subsequently removed. This method of cleaning, although sufficient for floss for local uses, always leaves some seed still attached to the material, and for this reason, where the Kapok is intended for export, power-driven machines are used for cleaning and ginning. For the best-quality Kapok, the fiber is usually gained two or three times.

The quality of Kapok is judged by its moisture content, colour, smell, luster and refraction. Refraction is the amounts of seeds, dirts, leaves, and any other extraneous matters mixed with it. The fault with Indian Kapok is that it is often adulterated with cotton waste and akund floss, and where this is so I cannot be used for life-saving apparatus. This adulteration is probably more harmful than bad grading, as manufacturers of life-saving apparatus cannot take risks, and will not use Kapok unless they can be assured of its purity.

For transport in India, Kapok is packed in jute bags; for export it is packed under hydraulic pressure into bales weighing 165 lb. each, and with a volume of about 13 cubic ft. It is important that the Kapok should be properly dry before shipment; otherwise it hardens during shipment and there is difficulty in opening up the bales

Other uses:
The wood is light and soft, and is suitable for making canoes and toys. It is used for matches. The pulp is suitable for making paper of ordinary quality. The root bark yields a fiber.

Very young fruits are eaten uncooked and the light brown, still rather soft seeds from half ripe fruits are eaten raw as a dainty
