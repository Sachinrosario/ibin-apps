Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Metroxylon sagu is a palm tree growing up to 15 m high with large pinnate leaves and stout creeping or ascending stems. It is often cultivated and grows well in freshwater swamps.

Distribution: Southeast Asia and nearby Pacific. This species is thought to have originated in Papua New Guinea where, without doubt, it is one of the most commonly seen palms in those regions. It has however spread from there to many regions in south east Asia because of its tremendous usefulness. They are occasionally grown in Indian gardens. (1,2,3,4,7 8,)

Status :Cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

M. sagu Rottb. is an unbranched palm, about 9-12 m. high. It has thick-set cylindrical trunk, often attaining a girth 1.25-1.50 m. (1)

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

The palm flowers once in its life time at the age of 9-15 years after which it dies. (1)

Breeding System :

Selection for high Starch yields is feasible, but germplasm resources need to be screened and selected forms must be propagated. Sago palm is propagated vegetatively, so superior selections can be readily maintained.

Induced- Mutation breeding of Sago and Micropropagation techniques have been developed at the Talau Research Station in Malaysia. (2)

Seedling Germination :

Metroxylon sagu is vegetatively propagated since seed propagation is difficult due to it's viability and development of off types. (1,2)

Cultivation:

The palm thrives in low marshy situations and propagated by suckers. It can also be grown from seeds, though good viable seeds are difficult to obtain; seedlings raised from them do not develop true to type. The Sago palm attains maturity and produces a inflorescence at the age of 9-15 years, during which the interior of the stem is filled with starch, when it is ready to be harvested. (2)

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used and Uses

Stem: Starch is obtained in the form of sagu from the stem of the palm. This starch is cooked into a gooey substance and eaten perhaps with freshly caught fish.

Leaves: Leaves of this palm are commonly used for thatching which, will provide at least 5 years of dry shelter. The leaves are also used for making mats and baskets

Trunk, stalks and fronds: Are used for building rafters, walls, mats and baskets. The outer layers of ,the trunk are used for flooring and also for making buckets. (1,2,4,11,12)


Harvesting Practices

The palm is cut just before the appearance of its terminal inflorescence and the stem cut into short lengths of 1.0 – 1.2 m. (1,2)


Produce and Products

Jelly: The sogo is boiled with sugar to make jelly. (12)

Sago meal: is produced from the trunk when the tree is about twelve years old. The trunk is cut into sections, which are split lengthwise, and the soft material in the center is scooped out. From this material starch is extracted by washing and straining. The starch is dried to yield sago meal or granulated to "pearl" sago. (1,2)

Confectionaries: Sago is also used in making steamed pudding, bread, cakes and pastries. (1,2)

Textiles: Sago starch is used in the textile industry for sizing and finishing cotton, warps. It gives a solution with a higher viscosity than maize starch solution of the same concentration, though not so high as that of tapioca starch; the viscosity is maintained over a wide range of temperature. (1,2,11)

Flour or " Rasp " : The sago can be used for feeding either as flour or as "rasp," obtained by mechanical rasping of the barked sago trunks. (1,2)

Animal feed : Sundried rasp gives a similar performance to that of sago flour when it is fed to cattle and older pigs. The meal is very digestible and can be fed to all classes of livestock. It has been included up to 50% in pig diets and up to 25% in poultry diets. High levels of sago meal in rations tend to decrease production and impair feed conversion. (7)

Paper Industry: Fronds are used in paper making. (6)

Chemistry  

The chemical composition and fiber morphology of sago fronds. The long fiber (1.69 to 3.59 mm) and high L/D ratio (142.61 to 284.14) are attractive features of the fronds for papermaking. The potential pulping property of this material is generally supported by the proximate chemical analysis which gives a considerably high cellulose content (72.54 to 73.26% and 65.82 to 66.92% of holo- and alpha-cellulose respectively). The moderately high alkali solubility (30.58 to 33.08%), however, may affect the yield in chemical pulping. (6)

Processing Techniques

Sago palm attains maturity and produces a inflorescence at the age of 9-15 years, when the interior of the stem is filled with starch. The palm is cut at this stage and the stem cut into short lengths of 1.0-1.2 m. and split. The pith is rasped into a coarse meal and kneaded with water over a strainer through which the starch passes into a trough leaving the fiber behind. The starch is allowed to settle, washed once or twice with water and dried. The quality of starch improves with washing. Starch of high quality is used for preparing sago pearls. For this purpose, the starch is kneaded into a paste and rubbed into grains through a sieve. The grains are roasted over fire in a pan smeared with vegetable oil to give gelatinized globules. A single palm is reported to yield 250-660 pounds of sago; on rare occasions, yield as high as 1,200 pounds is obtained. For local use it is pulverized, but for the market it is usually sieved and then heated to form granules. (1,2,11)

Other Uses

Medicinal uses: Sago Palm is used locally to treat Impotence and Viral shingles. Sago globules are used chiefly as an article of diet, especially during convalescence and also in bowel complaints and febrile disorders. It is non-irritating to the stomach lining and is readily digested. (5)

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

A single sago palm yields about 150-300 kg of sago. A normal freshwater swamp can produce fifty palms suitable for harvesting per hectare each year. The energy production per hectare is thus very high.

Sago will retain an important role in the local food production and it's importance increases as the crop thrives in marginal habitats. It grows well in swamps, an environment unsuitable for most crops. Sago palm is likely to become a more significant crop as some marginal areas of the tropics become crowded and as demand for quality starch continues to grow in international market.

Prospects for sago are particularly bright for some nontraditional uses. Several developing countries have expressed an interest in establishing industries for the bioconversion of sago starch to protein. In some countries sago palm has recently been used by local industries to produce ethanol, which can be added to gasoline to stretch petroleum supplies, or, with appropriately designed engines, can be used as a transportation fuel. (1,2,11)
