Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
The most probable primary center of origin of Maize is considered to be Central America particularly southern Mexico where marked genetic variability for morphological and physiological characters has been recorded (1). The genus consists of some four species Zea mays, Zea diploperennis, Zea luxurians and Zea perennis. Of the four species of Zea, only Zea mays is common and is cultivated throughout the world (2).

Maize is essentially a warm weather annual. Availability of very divergent types of maize has helped its cultivation over a wide range of climatic conditions, and it is now more widely distributed over the world than any other cereal. It is grown from a latitude of 58° N in Canada and the USSR in the north to a latitude 40° S in the southern hemisphere. It is cultivated from below sea level in the Caspian plain to an altitude of 3600 m. in the Peruvian Andes. Maize is extensively cultivated in India both in the plains and in the hills, especially in the areas with hot summer. Its acreage is next only to rice, jowar, wheat and bajra. The states of Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh and Punjab are the leading producers in India. Among the cereals, the average yield of Maize per hectare is highest in Karnataka. Maize grows best on deep fertile, well drained loamy soils that are neither too heavy nor too light. It is grown successfully over a range of soils with pH from 5-8. The yield is affected adversly when the soil pH is less than 5.5 (1).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A robust, monoecious, annual grass though generally tall, varies in height from 0.43 m. to sometimes over 6.0 m. in different varieties and regional types. Stems or stalks smooth, developing adventitious, fibrous roots from the lower nodes, leaves with overlapping sheaths, a short, ciliate ligule and broadly lanceolate blade, 10-125 cm. long, 2.2-12.0 cm. broad, hairy above, ciliate on the margins (1). Leaves borne alternatively on either side of the stem at the nodes, 8-21 in number. Sheath encloses internodes for varying distances, entire below, split above, glabrous or pubescent, usually with hairs along upper margins, blade linear-lanceolate, acuminate, 30-150 x 5-15 cm, stomata usually somewhat more numerous on lower surface (7).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Maize is monoecious, wherein male and female parts are present in the same plant at different positions. Male inflorescence is called Tassel and female inflorescence is called cob/silk found in the internodal regions of the plant. Since, maize is protandrous in nature, male parts always mature first (3). Male spikelets in long spike-like racemes, usually in large numbers and forming large, terminal panicles (commonly called tassels), spikelets arranged in pairs, one stalked and the other sessile, 2 flowered, awnless, with sub-equal, membranous glumes, female inflorescence in the axil of the leaves, with the spikelets arranged in 8-16 or even as many as 30 rows n a thickened, almost woody axis (cob), the whole enclosed in numerous, large, foliaceous bracts or spathes with the long, silky styles protruding from the summit as a mass of threads. Spikelets sessile, half sunk in the axis, conical, with a lower barren and an upper fertile floret, awnless, glumes broad, rounded or emarginate at the apex, lemma lembranous, palea shorter or absent (1). Emergence of silk will be around 8-10 days later than the complete emergence of tassel. After complete emergence of silk stigma becomes receptive in one or two days after and its receptivity lasts for 12-18 hours. The pollen tubes reaches the micropyle of the ovaries in 15-25 hours after pollination. The process of pollination and fertilization will be completed in 36 hours.

Breeding System :
Self-pollination and fertilization and cross-pollination and fertilization are usually possible and frequencies of each are usually determined by physical proximity and other physical influences on pollen transfer. A number of complicating factors, such as genetic sterility factors and differential growth rates of pollen tubes may also influence the frequencies of self-fertilization versus cross-fertilization (2).

Some exceptions in the usual process:

  • Pollen is produced entirely in the staminate inflorescences. Eggs are produced entirely in the pistillate inflorescences.

  • Self-pollination and fertilization and cross-pollination and fertilization are usually possible and frequencies of each are usually determined by physical proximity and other physical influences on pollen transfer. A number of complicating factors, such as genetic sterility factors and differential growth rates of pollen tubes may also influence the frequencies of self-fertilization versus cross-fertilization.

  • Corn styles and corn pollen tubes are the longest known in the plant kingdom. Shed pollen typically remains viable for 10 to 30 minutes, but may remain viable for much longer under refrigerated conditions (Coe et al., 1988).

  • The staminate and pistillate inflorescences do not develop at the same time. The pistillate inflorescence is precocious. However, there is the appearance of slight protandry because the elongating styles are delayed for about seven days in emergence from the bracts of the pistillate inflorescence, while the development of the later-developing staminate inflorescence is fully visible.

  • The genetics of corn is better known than that of any other crop plant (2).

Seed Biology :
Grains large, subglobose or dorsally more or less flattened, shining and hard when ripe, surrounded by the base of the glumes.

Seedling Germination :
Seeds starts germinating after 8-15 days of sowing. Seeds should be sown about 5 cm deep to ensure good seedling growth and vigour. It should be ensured that seeds have 95% germination. Seed lots with less than 80% germination are likely to impart poor seedling, vigour. Transplanting should be avoided, since gap-filled plants cannot cope up with the main stand.

Cultivation:

The date of sowing has to be suitably adjusted by making the best use of their natural precipitation and providing adequate time for land preparation for the following crop. In rainfed areas, crop is sown June-July in the northern plains or April-May in hills. A clean, smooth, deeply ploughed but firm seedbed is ideal. It is desirable to sow the crop on ridges to avoid damage due to waterlogging and to provide adequate moisture in the root zone. About 20 kg of seed would be needed to sow one hectare.

The precise quantities of fertilizers needed for various fields depend upon the fertility status of the soil, previous cropping history and the duration of the variety. A balanced application of 60-120 kg N, 40-60 kg P and 40 kg K/ha is recommended. Early-maturing varieties require smaller quantity of nitrogen than the full-season hybrids. One-fourth of the total quantity of nitrogen and the entire quantity of phosphorus, potash and zinc (if required) should be applied in banks 5-7 cm deep before sowing. The rest of the nitrogen should be applied in 2 unequal doses as side-dressing; half of the total nitrogen (60 kg N/ha when the total dose is 120 kg N/ha) should be applied when the crop is at knee-high stage (20-30 days after germination). While, the rest of the nitrogen (30 kg N/ha) should be applied after the emergence of flag-leaf, but before the emergence of tassels. The fertilizers should be applied 10-12 cm away from the base of the plan to avoid plant injury. Soon after application, the fertilizer on the soil surface should be covered to minimize losses.

A population of 65-70 thousand plants per hectare at harvest is necessary for realizing high grain yield. It will be necessary to attain about 10% higher stand at germination. For attaining the desired level of plant density, it is desirable to use a row-to-row and plant-to-plant spacing of 75 cm x 18 cm or 60 x 22 cm. Wider row spacing would be desirable for intercultivation. For ensuring high & stable yields, the available sources of irrigation should be tapped to provide 1 or 2 irrigations at the critical stages in years when rains fail.

Short-duration varieties of pulses (mungbean, urdbean, pigeonpea, cowpea, etc.), oilseeds (groundnut, soybean) and vegetables can be successfully intercropped in maize. The yield of maize under intercropping is not lower than that of the pure-sown maize. The intercrop would be a bonus.

Weeds having broad-leaves and most of the grasses can be conveniently controlled with a single pre-emergence application of Atrazine @ 1 kg/ha. In addition, 1 or 2 intercultivations are needed to keep weeds under check (2, 14, 15, 16).

Economic Potential and Uses

The corn oil may be used for frying since it does not tend to absorb flavors from any food and transmit them to others. . The high content of unsaturated fatty acids in corn oil makes it a good dietary agent for reducing blood cholesterol levels. The other larger use, about 30 % of corn oil is used in margarine. The acceptance of corn oil as a food oil is partly due to its flavour stability during storage and cooking. About 60 to 65 % of the corn oil in U.S.A. is consumed as salad or cooking oil. Its bland flavor and high, smoke point are mainly responsible for its popularity as deep-frying fat. A smaller proportion is used directly as liquid fat, or is sometimes blended with harder fats. Bakers use the oil for greasing tins or for brushing baked goods.

The proportion of corn oil utilized for other industrial purposes is very minor. These applications include pharmaceuticals and cosmetics, coatings for tablets and pills, vitamins, antibiotics, face and body powders. The hydrolyzed vegetable oil is the fatty acids recovered from crude oil during the refining step. The soap stock, a byproduct of corn oil refining, is used in the manufacture of soap and associated products. Smaller quantities of either crude or refined corn oil are also used in ammunition, chemicals and insecticides, paint and varnish substitutes, rust preventive compounds and textiles. Sulfonated corn oil has important applications in tanning and processing of leather. Oil is used for frying potato chips and doughnuts. Refined oil is used in pharmacy as a carrier for vitamins, it is used as a source of polyunsaturated acids in special diets. Linoleic acid, of which maize oil is a rich source, is effective in clearing dermatitis resulting from fat deficiency (4, 18, 19).

Uses

Maize is extensively used as food and is increasingly becoming important as fodder. It is the major source of starch and starch-based industrial products in many parts of the world. Corn is used as vegetable, as corn-on-the-cob, fresh, canned or frozen. Kernels may be cut from the cob and used in many ways, in succotash, custards, fritters, soups and powders. They are also used in mixed pickles and vegetable relishes. Corn meal, grits, and hominy are prepared forms of corn kernels. Corn is also converted into various substances which have a wide range of usage, as starch, syrup, cornstarch, dextrin and in the making of whiskey and other alcoholic products. These substances are used in the printing, confectionery, condensced milk, plastics etc. The defatted corn germ flour is presently used almost exclusively as an animal feed (5, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24).

Harvesting Practices
Maize cobs are harvested or removed before cutting the stalks at physiological maturity, at which the initial seed moisture content will be around 20-30 per cent depending upon weather conditions. At this stage seed colour in the cob turns to pearl yellow/white and cob sheath is dried and attains straw colour. Shelling of the cobs is carried out by using decarticator. Then the threshed produce is winnowed to obtain clean seed and further dried to reduce the initial seed moisture content to around 12 per cent (3).

Produce and Products

Maize is major raw-material for starch industry. Some of the important maize products are Dextrose, Maize starch syrup, Corn flakes, Corn starch, Corn meal, Paint and varnish, Paper products, Textiles etc. Some of the corn-derived ingredients for food applications are corn flakes, starch, maltodextrins, and sweetener product line, which includes corn syrups, high fructose corn syrups, dextrose, and fructose. These ingredients are used in a multitude of processed food applications, including beverage, baking, confectionery, dairy, jams and jellies, condiments, and more.
Candies: Corn syrup is used in hard candies to provide a body giving them chewiness and a desirable mouthfeel without excessive sweetness. Candies that are coated use a pyrodextrin corn starch for the coating.

Carbonated Beverages – Coke: High fructose corn syrup (HFCS) blended with sucrose in a 50/50 blend is sweeter than the same concentration of sucrose. The use of BFCS in carbonated beverages is common throughout Canada and the U.S.

Cookies: Corn starch, corn flour or dextrose may be found in cookies.

Beer: liquid adjuncts, namely corn syrups are used (19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24).

Processing Techniques

The dried germs recovered in the wet- milling or dry milling process contain up to 50 per cent of a semi-drying oil, known as Maize Oil or Corn Oil. The oil is extracted from the germs by crushing in expellers or by solvent extraction, refined by conventional chemical methods and chilled to remove any waxy matter present. Wet milled germ is preferably expelled from an oil content of 50-60% down to 20-25% and finally extracted with hexane to a residual oil content of 1-2% in the spent corn germ flakes. Most dry corn-mills recover the oil by tempering and flaking followed by solvent extraction of the germ (4).

Economic Potential To The Country

Globally, corn is the third most important cereal grain after wheat and rice. It has a great worldwide significance as human food, animal feed and as a source of a large number of industrial products. Among cereals, maize in India ranks 5th in total area, fourth in total production and third in yield per hectare. Corn is the only cereal, which can be used as a food at various stages of development of the plant and is not necessary to wait for the crop to reach maturity. Corn is used as baby corn as soon as the plant flowers. Green ears of corn are used on a large scale for roasting and boiling and consumed as food at dough stage (2).

The area under corn is nearly static at 6 million hectares since 1970 although production and productivity did show a modest growth rate of 1.9% per annum. The current production is approximately at 10 million tons. Maize does posses tremendous potential, about 55% of the corn produced in India is consumed directly as food, 30-35% as poultry, piggery and fish meal, 10-12% in wet milling industry (e.g. starch and oil) and about 3% in dry milling requirements. Export Potential

India has been able to attain self-sufficiency in maize. During 1970-71, a surplus of 300,000 tonnes was available for export. In the past, however, there were extremely limited exports of maize. This was due to the high cost of production and limited overall food supply situation. India has been importing maize for its expanding maize starch industry. However, there exists definite export potential for maize in India, by reducing the cost of inputs with the adoption of latest cultural techniques, as in other maize exporting countries. Japan, Italy and Spain are the leading maize importing countries (1).

Miscellaneous Information

Theories on the Origin of Maize: There have been four principal theories on the origin of maize.
1. The Pod Corn Theory: cultivated maize originated from pod corn, a form in which the individual kernels are enclosed in floral bracts as they are in other cereals and in the majority of grasses.
2. The Teosinte Theory: maize originated from its closest relative teosinte, by direct selection, by mutation or by the hybridization of teosinte with an unknown grass now extinct.
3. The Theory of a Common Ancestry: maize, teosinte and Tripsacum have descended along independent lines directly from a common ancestor
4. The Tripartite Theory: Proposed by Mangelsdorf and Reeves that:
  • cultivated maize originated from pod corn,
  • teosinte is a derivate of the hybrid of maize and Tripsacum,

the majority of the corn varieties are the product of the admixture with teosinte or Tripsacum or both.

The minor theories on the origin of maize, ‘minor’ in the sense that they have had little effect on the thinking and experimentation concerned with the origin of corn are:
  • The Papyrescent ‘Semivestidos’ Theory: cultivated corn originated from papyrescent corn, a type of superficially resembling a weak form of pod corn
  • The Corn-Grass Theory: corn originated from ‘corn-grass’ a mutant form with numerous tillers

Corn is an allopolyploid hybrid originating in south-east Asia by the hybridization of two ten-chromosome species such as Coix and Sorghum (2).
