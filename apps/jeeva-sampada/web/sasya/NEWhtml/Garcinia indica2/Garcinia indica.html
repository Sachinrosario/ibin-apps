Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Garcinia indica is a large evergreen tree distributed in tropical Asia, Africa and Polynesia. About 30 species occur in India. In India, tree is found in tropical rain forests of western ghats in Karnataka, Kerala, as well in parts of West Bengal, Assam and Gujarath. It is often planted in southern districts of Maharashtra. It is reported to flourish well on the lower slopes of Nilgiri hills. It grows on wide range of soils ranging from marginal to deep alluvial soils, which are well drained. Soils having good moisture retention capacity are favourable. The most favourable regions for Kokum cultivation are warm and moderate humid zones. The temperature range from 20°C to 35°C, 60 to 80 % humidity, well distributed annual rainfall ranging from 2500 to 4000 mm seem to be ideal for this crop. (1, 2, 3).

Availability : 

Status : Wild

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Kokum is approximately 15 to 20 m in height, slender, pyramid shaped, evergreen tree with drooping branches, has dark-brown or nearly black, flaking bark, the inner bark containing much yellow, gummy, bitter latex. Leaves ovate or oblong, lanceolate, 2.5-3.5 inches long and 1-1.5 inches broad, dark green above and pale beneath. Fruits globose or spherical (2.5-3.5 cm dia), dark purple when ripe enclosing 5-8 large seeds (1, 3, 4, 5).

Flowers, 1.5  to 2 inches (4-5 cm) wide and fleshy, may be male or hermaphrodite on the same tree. The former are in clusters of 3-9 at the branch tips; there are 4 sepals and 4 ovate, thick, fleshy petals, green with red spots on the outside, yellowish-red inside, and many stamens though the aborted anthers bear no pollen. The hermaphrodite are borne singly or in pairs at the tips of young branchlets; their petals may be yellowish-green edged with red or mostly red, and are quickly shed (1, 3, 4).

Fruits glabose or spherical 1-1.5 inches in diameter. Dark purple when ripe enclosing 5-8 large seeds. Ovoid-oblong, somewhat flattened, 1 in (2.5 cm) long and 5/8 in (1.6 cm) wide, that cling to the flesh (1).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Back to Top

Breeding System :
Kokum is dioceous in nature and reproduces through cross pollination.

Phenology :
Kokum tree starts flowering during the month of November- December and fruits are harvested in April-May.

Pollinators:
Pollination occurs through insects. The male plants serve as pollinizers and hence it is necessary to keep 10 per cent of the male plants well scattered in the orchard.

Back to Top

Seed Biology :

Seedling Germination :
Percentage of germination is directly related to the weight of the seed. Fully developed seeds should be chosen for planting. Even these will lose viability in 5 days after removal from the fruit, though they are viable for 3 to 5 weeks in the fruit. Soaking in water for 24 hours enhances the rate of germination. Generally, sprouting occurs in 20 to 22 days and is complete in 43 days (4).

Back to Top

 

Cultivation:

Propagation of Kokum by seed is the prevailing practice. For raising of seedlings, bigger sized fresh seeds from high yielding and regular bearing plants should be selected. The seeds should be cleaned with water and then sown in polythene bags (12.5 x 20.0 cm) containing potting mixture. Two days prior to sowing, seeds should be soaked in water which gives early and better germination (upto 90 %). One year old seedlings should be used for planting in the field.

Vegetative propagation: Vegetative propagation has got many advantages over seedling propagation. To ensure female trees, uniformity in yield and quality of fruits, vegetative propagation should be followed in kokum. Different grafting techniques like inarching, side grafting, softwood grafting have been tried with considerable success.

Inarch grafting: Inarching seems to be the most successful method of vegetative propagation in kokum. It is reported that inarch grafting was successful on 10 to 18 months old, vigorous seedlings when done in the month of December-January. For grafting purpose, about 3-4 months old upright growing shoots from scion plants should be selected. If horizontal growing scion shoots are used, the grafts have straggling habit with top heaviness on one side. About 90 per cent success is observed in this method.

Soft wood grafting: The soft wood grafting technique consists of grafting by wedge method on terminal new growth of the stock, For soft wood grafting, more than 22 weeks old seedlings and 5-6 months old scion sticks are suitable. Graft union occurs within 2 months if the take is perfect. April-May was found to be the best period for grafting and about 80 per cent success is reported in this method.

Side grafting: This seems to be the best method of propagation for kokum since it is ideal for establishing in situ plantations. Beside, this technique can be successfully utilized for preparing plants anywhere since the scion sticks can be transported to any place for grafting. Also this is an ideal method for converting old and inferior seedling trees into productive ones of a desired type through top working.

Planting: Prior to planting, field should be cleaned by removing bushes and levelled. Pits of 60 x 60 x 60 cm should be dug at 6 x 6 m distance and filled with the top soil mixed with 20 kg FYM, 1 kg superphosphate. Pits should be prepared a fortnight before the onset of rains so that the pits are ready for transplanting of seedlings or grafted plants during the rainy season. At the time of planting, root ball should be kept intact. When graft is used, care is taken to keep the graft union above the soil surface. Soil around the seedling/graft is firmly pressed and support of bamboo stick is provided.

Care of plants: In the first year, for protecting the young plants from scorching sun, overhead shade is provided in summer months. Weeding should be done to keep the basins weed free.

Intercropping: The kokum requires a long time to occupy fully the land provided during planting for future mature trees. It is desirable that the interspace should not be left unutilized. Suitable crops should be grown as intercrops till the trees reach bearing stage. In general crops like banana, cocoa, coconut, arecanut and rainfed crops like sweet potato, vegetables and flowering annuals have been found feasible for growing.

Irrigation: During the initial two years, plants should be watered during winter and summer months. For conservation of soil moisture over a longer period, the soil around the plants in early stages of growth be mulched with dry grass.

Manuring and fertilization: It is recommended that in the first year, each plant should be applied with 2 kg FYM, 50 g N, 25 g P2O5 and 25 g K2O. This dose is increased every year and from 10th year onwards, each plant may be given 20 kg FYM, 500 g N, 250 g P2O5 and 250 g K2O. Manures and fertilizers are applied in ring method under the tree canopy during the month of August after the heavy rains are over (1, 3, 4, 5, 6).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

Few pests have been reported. A leaf-eating caterpillar Orgyra sp. of the tussock moth family, Lymantridae. A small ant, Myrnelachista ramulorum colonizes the tree, tunnels into the trunk and branches, and damages the new growth. Mites sometimes deface the fruits with small bites and scratches. Fully ripe fruits are attacked by monkeys, bats and rats (4).


Diseases:

Thread blight caused by the fungus, Pellicularia koleroga, is often seen on branchlets, foliage and fruits of trees in shaded, humid areas. The fruits may become coated with webbing and ruined. Zignoella garcineae, gives rise to "canker"–tuberous growths on the branches, causing a fatal dying-back of foliage, branches and eventually the entire tree. Breakdown in storage is caused by the fungi Diplodia gossypina, Pestalotia sp., Phomopsis sp., Gleosporium sp., and Rhizopus nigricans. A major physiological problem called "gamboge" is evidenced by the oozing of latex onto the outer surface of the fruits and on the branches during periods of heavy and continuous rains.

Economic Potential and Uses

The kernel in the seed contains about 33-44 per cent oil which is popularly known as Kokum butter. It is white in colour with yellowish tinge. It remains solid at room temperature. The oil is used for burns and injuries as Vaseline. It is also used as a substitute for ghee and in soap manufacture. Fat from the seed is used as confectionary butter. It also suitable for candle manufacture. The kokum butter is considered nutritive demulcent, astringent and emollient. It is suitable for ointments, suppositories, cosmetics and other pharmaceutical purposes. It is used as a local application to ulcerations and fissures of lips, chopped hands and feets etc. Due to high contents of disaturated and monosaturated glycerides, it is in demand as a substitute for cocoa and extender in chocolate and confectionery products. Stearic acid produced from the fat is used for the sizing of cotton yarn. Kokum oil seed cake is used as a substitute feed stock for biogas production. The feedstock substituted cowdung to the extent of 33% (dry weight basis), and produced about 30% more methane yields than cowdung slurry alone. The oil cake is also used as manure. (1, 3, 7, 8, 13).

Uses

Fruit has a agreeable flavour and a sweetish acid taste. It is used in Konkan chiefly in the form of kokum prepared by drying the outer rind, soaking it repeatedly in the juice of the pulp and sun drying. It is used as a garnish to give an acid flavour to curries and also for preparing cooling syrups during hot months. Kokum has the same souring qualities as tamarind, especially enhancing coconut-based curries or vegetable dishes like potatoes, okra or lentils. Kokum is especially used with fish curries, three or four skins being enough to season an average dish. It is also included in chutneys and pickles. The skins are not usually chopped but are added whole to the dish. In some places wood is used to make handles for spears, rice pounders and is employed in construction and cabinetwork (1, 3, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10).

Harvesting Practices
Ripeness is gauged by the full development of colour and slight softening. Picking may be done when the fruits are slightly under ripe but they must be fully mature (developed) or they will not ripen after picking. The fruits must be harvested by hand from ladders or by means of a cutting pole and not be allowed to fall. Yield varies from tree to tree and from season to season. The yield steadily increases with age, individual trees between the ages of 20 and 45 years have borne 2,000 to 3,000 fruits. Average yield of a full-grown tree is about 200 fruits (1, 3, 5).

Produce and Products

Kokum syrup (Amrit kokum): This product is prepared from the fresh ripe kokum rind by adding cane sugar at the rate of 1:2 proportion. The mixture is kept as such preferably in sunlight, after packing in glass container for 8 days occasionally striring. The mixture is strained through muslin cloth and the syrup is stored in the food grade plastic container or in glass bottles. This product is required to be diluted 5 to 6 time with water before consumptlon as sharabat. A pinch of common salt and cumin powder are added for taste.

Dried ripe kokum rind: The ripe kokum rind is dried under sun or in a cabinet drier at 60°C temperature. It is treated with brined kokum juice and again dried; this is done for five minutes. The finally dried product is used in curry in place of tamarind.

Kokum agal (Brined kokum juice): This product is prepared either from ripe kokum fruit rind, unripe kokum fruit rind and pulp or only from pulp. This product is used for making 'Skolkadhi' a refreshing drink after lunch.

Fruits are eaten and used in the preparation of dishes. Oil obtained from the seeds is used in soap manufacture and is employed for variety of other purposes. Fruit has many medicinal properties (5, 10).

Processing Techniques

The fruit contains three to eight seeds, which contain about 33-44% oil. The seeds are decorticated by beating with a rod, and the oil is extracted mostly on cottage industry basis by crushing the kernels, by boiling the pulp in water and skimming off the fat from the top (1, 2).

Other Uses
The root is astringent. Kokum has refrigerant properties. It is useful as an infusion, or by direct application, in skin ailments such as rashes caused by allergies. Kokum butter is an emollient helpful in the treatment of burns, scalds and chaffed skin. Fruit is anthaelmintic and cardiotonic and useful in piles, dysentery, tumours, pains and heart complaints. The fruits are steeped in sugar syrup to make amrut kokum which is drunk to relieve bilious affections and sunstroke (6, 9, 10).

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

Kokam is one of the most important tree oil seed crops found cultivated in the west coast. It appears to be a promising raw material for commercial exploitation in view of its interesting chemical constituents. Exact statistics regarding the area and production of this fruit in India is not available because it is seldom planted in the form of a regular orchard. Generally, scattered trees are found in back yards, waste land, on road side, forests and also in coconut and arecanut plantations. However one report suggests that the total availability of kokum seed in India is about 2000 tons and its oil is about 800 tons (3).
