HABIT & HABITAT
A glabrous much branched shrub, found throughout India, chiefly in drier parts; commercially cultivated in  Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Tamil Nadu.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:
 A glabrous much branched shrub, lateral branches 4 gonous, often ending in a spinous point. Leaves 1.3-3.2 by 0.6-1.6 cm., elliptic or broadly lanceolate acute or obtuse, often mucronulate, base tapring; petioles very short or absent.  flourers numerous, less than 1.3 cm. across, fragrant, white or rose-coloured, in large terminal pyramidal panicled cymes; pedicels short, slender.  Calyx 3-5 mm. Long, broadly campanulate; lobes 2.5-3 mm. long, ovate, acute. Petal 3 mm long  as broad as long, suborbicular or subreniform, undulate; stamens 8, insested in pairs on the calyx tube. Capsule 6 mm.diam; globose, slightly veined outside, supported by the persistant calyx and tipped with the style. Seeds trigono-pyramidal about 2.5 mm. long externally subtuberculate.

Economic Potential and Uses
UTILIZATION:
Uses:
 The roots are used to cure dipsia, leprosy, skin diseases, strangury, amenorrhoea, dysmenorrhoea and premature greying of hair. Leaves are anticephalalgic and used in sore throat, burning sensation in feet. The seeds are antipyretic, intellect promoting and constipating, and are useful in intermittent fevers, in sanity, amentia, diarrhoea, dysentery and gastropathy.


Chemistry:
NBPGR:  The leaves contain luteolin, luteolin-7-o-glucoside acacetin–7-o- glucoside, β-sitosterol glucoside, laxanthones I & II and lawsone. The back gave n-triacontyl-n-tridecanoate, n-triacontanol, β-sitosterol, lupeol, 30-norlupan-3β -ol-20-ane, betulin, betulinic acid and pentacyclic triterpenes-hennadiol and a dihydroxylupane. The plant also contained a coumarin-lacoumarin identified as 5-allyloxy-7hydroxy coumarin and three xanthones-laxanthones-I, II and III.
Two new xanthones - laxanthone I (fig. 1a) and II (fig. 1b) isolated and characterized as 1,3-dihydroxy-6,7-dimethoxyxanthone and 1-hydroxy-3,6-diacetoxy-7-methoxyxanthone respectively. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979)b.
Laxanthone III (fig. 2) isolated and characterized as 1-hydroxy-3,7-dimethoxy-6-acetoxyxanthone. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979)c.
Gallic acid, 2-hydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone, 1,4-dihydroxynaphthalene and 1,4-naphthoquinone isolated from leaves. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984)a.
Lutealin, its -7-O-glucoside, acacetin- 7-O-glucoside, laxanthone I, laxanthane II, lawsone and b-sitosterol glucoside isolated. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984)b.
Apigenin-7-glucoside, apigenin-4'-glucoside, lutealin-7-glucoside and luteolin-3'-glucoside isolated from leaves. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984)c.
Fraxetin, scopoletin and esculetin isolated from leaves. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984)d.
Two pentacyclic triterpenes - 3b,30-dihydroxylup-20(29)-ene (hennadiol) and (20S)3b,30-dihydroxylupane -isolated from bark and characterized. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984)e.
b-sitosterol, stigmasterol and 1,2- dihydroxy-4-glucosyloxynaphthalene isolated from leaves. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984)f.
lawsone, laxanthone II, luteolin, b-sitosterol and its 3-O-glucoside isolated from leaves. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989)a.
Mannitol (0.7%) present in stems; flowers and roots contained lower amounts. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989)b.
Isolation of new phenolic glucosides - lawsoniaside (fig. 3) and lalioside (fig. 4) and their characterization. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989)c.
A new aliphatic hydrocarbon isolated from stem bark and characterized as 3-methylnonacosan-1-ol. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994)a.
Isolation of a new sterol - lawsaritol (fig. 5) from roots and its structure elucidation as 24b-ethylcholest-4-en-3b-ol. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994)b.
Seed oil contained oleic, palmitic and linoleic acids as major components along with stearic, lauric and myuristic acids. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994)c.
A new napthaquinone - isoplumbagin (fig. 7) isolated from stem bark and its structure determined. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994)d.
Isolation and structure elucidation of a new dihydroxysterol - lawsaritol A (fig. 6) from roots. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994)e.
Bioassay guided fractionation of the methanolic extract of the defatted Lawsonia inermis seeds has led to the isolation of two new triterpenoids lawnermis acid and its Methyl ester. Their structures have been established on the basis of chem. and spectral evidence. (Handa et al., 1997) CA. 1998, 127:78516.
Two pentacyclic triterpenoids have been isolated from the aerial parts of Lawsonia alba. The structures of the new constituents, named as lawsonic acid (I) and lawsonin (II) have been elucidated as 3b-E-ferulyloxy-lup-20(29)-en-28-oic acid and 3b-E-ferulyloxy-urs-11-en-13b-ol respectively through spectroscopic studies. (Siddiqui and Kardar, 2002) CA. 2002, 136:229402.

Pharmacology: 

1,2-Dihydroxy-4-glucosyloxynaphthalene was active against Bacillus subtilis and Saccharomyces pastorianus. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984)g.
Luteolin, lawsone, laxanthone II, b-sitosterol and its 3-O-glucoside exhibited anti-inflammatory activity; b-sitosterol (20.0 mg/kg) was more potent than phenylbutazone (15.0 mg/kg) whereas luteolin and lawsone (20.0 mg/kg) were equipotent to phenylbutazone (15.0 mg/kg) and b-sitosterol-3-O-glucoside (20.0 mg/kg) was equipotent to hydrocortisone acetate (5.0 mg/kg). (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989)d.
Isoplumbagin was isolated from stem bark exhibited antitumour activity. ( Ali and Grever, 1998). MAPA. 1998, 9804: 2125.
Lawsone have been studied in vitro against bacteria, fungi and yeast. The inhibition zone was <7 mm at a drug concentration of 30 mg for bacteria (Esherichia coli, Klebsiella, Pneumoniae, Campylobacter jejuni, Staphylococcus sp., Bacillus sp., Mycobacterium sp., and Corynebacterium diphtheriae), fungi (Aspergillus sp. and Helminthosporum sp.), and the yeast Candida albicans. (Dama et al., 1998) CA. 1999, 131:85344.
Bioassay guided fractionation of the methanolic ext. of the defatted Lawsonia inermis seeds has led to the isolation of two new triterpenoids lawnermis acid and its Methyl ester. Their structures have been established on the basis of chem. and spectral evidence. Which shows significant anti-complementary activity in vitro tests. It inhibits the immunohemolysis of antibody coated sheep erythrocytes by pooled guinea pig serum. (Handa et al., 1997) CA. 1998, 127:78516.
The ethanolic extract of Lawsonia inermis leaves and lawsone tested for trypsin inhibitory activity showed an IC(50) value of 64.87 and 48.6 microg/ml, respectively. (Yogisha et al., 2002).
Modulatory effect of henna leaf (Lawsonia inermis) on drug metabolising phase I and phase II enzymes, antioxidant enzymes, lipid peroxidation and chemically induced skin and forestomach papillomagenesis in mice. (Dasgupta et al., 2003).
Pharmacognostic studies of leaf, petiole and leaf powder. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979)a.


Ethnobotany: 

Medicine for antifertility (Seed, Sharma, 1981); Burning sensation (Leaf, Nagendra Prasad and Abraham, 1984); Headache, pain in muscle (Part not mentioned, Jain and Tarafder, 1970); Jaundice (Root, Hemadri and Rao, 1984); Sprain (Leaf, Jain and De, 1966); Weakness (Seed, Gupta, 1981); Ornamental to dye hands, feet (Leaf paste, Agarwal, 1981); Garden hedge (Whole plant, Mehra et al, 1975)

Pharmaceutical and Toxicology:

Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficient newborns with haemolysis linked to henna application had delayed age at presentation and a higher reticulocytic count and hyperbilirubinaemia compared with non-henna-induced haemolysis (p < 0.05). Percutaneous henna absorption is well recognized and clinical findings support the harmful effect of henna on G6PD-deficient red blood cells. A local health education programme has been established to prevent the use of henna dye in infancy. (Kandil et al., 1996).
