HABIT & HABITAT
Herbs or undershrubs, evergreen, glabrous. It is found to occur in  Uttar Pardesh, Bihar, West Bengal, Sikkim, Assam, Meghalaya, Orissa, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka, Maharashtra. Sri lanka, Indonesia, Malaya, Deccan Peninsula, Tenasserim.


Availability :  Plenty, Throughout India

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:
 Leaves terete, elliptic, lanceolate or obovate or oblanceolate, 6.0-16.5 x 3-5 cm, acute or acuminate at apex, entire at margin, tapering gradually into petiole, birhgt green above, pale beneath; petioles 5-7 mm long.
REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:   
Floral and seed biology:
Inflorescence terminal or sometimes axillary, usually in dense many flowered cymes forming a hemispheric head at the end of peduncle axis, light green in colour; Flower pure white or white in touch of pink on the outside of corolla but mature inflorescence red at maturity, bracts minute,, subulate from triangular bracts., bisexual, hypogynous, rotate with distinct tube or limb ; Calyx lobes squamulose, almost free ,deltoid to lanceolate,1.3-3 mm long, acute at the apex ,often with one or two minute teeth on the margin near the base ; Corolla petals 5,salvareshaped,connate with twisted aestivation, tube pinkish, cylindrical and slender, slightly swollen at the location of anthers ; Androecium stamens 5, inserted in the corolla tube and adnate to its filaments, very short disc of 2 large glands; Gynoecium: bicarpellary with free ovaries, but one stigma and one style annulated, disk subtending and partly clocking ovary, cylindric, truncate the rounded or slightly concave at the apex, narrowed to a stipe like base, glabrous, carpels united at base to the length of disk the ovule subtented, two in each cell, style long 8mm long, membranaous, tunicate at the base fringe at apex, stigma of minute apiculi ; Fruit drupe ,double united at the half way, oval, slightly flattened ; Seed single in each carpel,oval,flattened endocarp copious, and soft.
It flowers between November - September.
Mode of pollination and seed dispersal:

Plants are pollinated by insects and seeds are dispersed through winds.
Genetic variability:
The plant is diploid with chromosome number  2n=22. Tetraploid plants with chromosome number 2n = 44 have also been reported.

AGRO TECHNOLOGY:

Propagation: Plant is propagated   either by seeds or vegetatively by stem cuttings and root cuttings.

Soil type: Plant grows best in sandy, loam and loam soils. 

Nutrient requirements: At the time of plantation 25 kg/ha of nitrogen, 22.5 kg/ha of phosphorous and potash will be applied.

Water management: Plant are benefited by monsoon rains.

Harvesting period / Method: Roots are harvested during winter months and care should be taken that root bark remains intact as it contains high alkaloid contents.

 Root is referred as antidote to snake-bite, for the treatment of high blood pressure, abortifacient, anthelmintic, fever, insanity, insomnia, intestinal troubles, malaria, nerval diseases and stomach troubles.

The selectivity of these adsorbents for some alkaloids has been demonstrated, and results of optimization of ajmaline isolation technique have been presented. This method of isolation and purification of ajmaline from plant material allows to obtain 17-17.5 mg of homogeneous alkaloid per gram of dry tissue. (Smirnova et al., 1996)

A five step procedure using anion exchange chromatography, chromatography on hydroxylapatite, gel filtration and FPLC-chromatography on Mono Q and Mono P delivered in a yield of 0.9% approximately 1200-fold enriched glucosidase. (Warzecha et al., 1999)

·        An efficient system for the analysis of indole alkaloids by HPLC on a reversed-phase column using an ion pair technique is described. The optimised chromatographic conditions allowed the successful separation of 22 standard monoterpenoid indole alkaloids (including some isomers) and tryptamine. The described HPLC system was applied to the analysis of alkaloids in intergeneric somatic hybrid cell cultures of Rauvolfia serpentina x Rhazya stricta. (Gerasimenko et al., 2001a).

·        Careful reexamination of the published 1H and 13C NMR spectral data of ajmalimine , an indole alkaloid from the roots of Rauvolfia serpentina, indicates that, in reality, the compound is (+)-17R-O-(3',4',5'-trimethoxybenzoyl)ajmaline. (Hanhinen and Lounasmaa, 2001)

·        A new monoterpenoid indole alkaloid, 3-oxo-rhazinilam , was isolated from intergeneric somatic hybrid cell cultures of Rauvolfia serpentina and Rhazya stricta, and the structure was determined by detailed 1D and 2D NMR analysis. It was also proved that 3-oxo-rhazinilam is a natural constituent of the hybrid cells. (Gerasimenko et al., 2001b).

·        Two biologically active compounds, the enzyme superoxide dismutase (EC 1.15.1.1) and the antiarrhythmic indole alkaloid ajmaline, were isolated from a callus culture of Rauwolfia serpentina Benth. Sequential isolation of biologically active compounds by metal-chelate affinity chromatography followed by azoadsorbent affinity chromatography allowed us to obtain highly purified products. The yields of superoxide dismutase and ajmaline were 180 mg/kg biomass and 16.5 g/kg dry weight, respectively. (Kirillova et al., 2001)

·        Five anhydronium bases were isolated by preparative HPLC from a methanolic extract of Rauvolfia serpentina roots. For the first time 3,4,5,6- tetradehydroyohimbine, 3,4,5,6-tetradehydro-(Z)-geissoschizol, 3,4,5,6-tetradehydrogeissoschizol and 3,4,5,6-tetradehydrogeissoschizine-17-O-beta-D-glucopyranoside were isolated from a natural source. In addition, the well-known anhydronium base serpentine was isolated. (Wachsmuth and Matusch, 2002)

·        Three new monoterpenoid indole alkaloids, 19(S),20(R)-dihydroperaksine (1), 19(S),20(R)-dihydroperaksine-17-al (2), and 10-hydroxy-19(S),20(R)-dihydroperaksine (3), along with 16 known alkaloids 4-19 were isolated from hairy root culture of Rauvolfia serpentina, and their structures were elucidated by 1D and 2D NMR analyses. (Sheludko et al., 2002)

(Sheludko et al., 2002)


Pharmacology:

·        Ajmaline was evaluated in cardiac arrhythmia model in dog and was found more effective in arrhythmias of ectopic focus type than in circus movement model. It decreased the excitability and increased the functional refractory period of all heart tissues. It also showed moderate degree of anticholinergic activity. It showed hypotensive activity in dogs and human patients. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Efficacy of ajmaline in human patients suffering form digitoxic arrhythmias was found to be the same (85.1%) as by treatment with oral potassium chloride. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Hypotensive effect of ajmaline was apparently mediated through its ganglion blocking activity. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Reserpinine depressed general behaviour of mice, prolonged the action of soporifics, decreased body temperature in doses of 50 mg/kg and had hypotensive action at 1 mg/kg. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        In isolated perfused rabbit heart, reserpine (1mg/ml) produced partial failure of impulse transmission due to decrease in noradrenaline released by sympathetic nerve stimulation. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Reserpine (2.5 mg/kg, i.p.) decreased levels of glutamic acid, alanine and glycine in sensory ganglia, glutamic acid level in sympathetic ganglia and levels of glutamic and aspartic acids in spinal cord in cats. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Reduced or abolished sympathetically-induced responses of stomach to perivascular nerve stimulation after4-5 hr in anaesthetized dogs pretreated with either atropine or atropine plus phenoxybenzamine. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Reserpine (10 mg/kg) raised serum gastrin concentration of fasting unoperated rats for a period of 6 to 10 hr after injection. It also reduced histidine decarboxylase activity in rat stomach. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Reserpine (1 mg/kg, i. v.) in dog produced slight fall in blood pressure , but same dose given after pretreatment with various sympathomimetic amines caused marked rise in blood pressure; maximum pressor response was seen after mephentermine and amphetamine pretreatment and minimum after tyramine and methoxamine pretreatment. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Reserpine (0.25, 0.5 and 1 mg/kg, i.p.) decreased mean blood pressure and heart rate of anaestheitsed rabbits; pretreatment with reserpine produced supersensitivity in these animals to hypotensive and positive chronotropic effect of isoprenaline. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Reserpine (2.5 mg/kg, i.p.) administered to rats immediately before exposure to hypoxic condition, prevented hypothermia, which was probably due to endogenous amines released by reserpine. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Reserpine treated atria had greater resting and maximum rates of beat than untreated atria. Atria pretreated with reserpine (1 to 3 mg/kg) were supersensitive to inotropic effect of norepincphrine. Reserpine at 3, 1 and 4 mg/kg dose increased content of sodium, potassium and magnesium respectively in atria and at 4 mg/kg decreased calcium content. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Reserpine (5mg/kg) decreased brain calcium level in rabbits, rats and mice after fifteen minutes. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Reserpine (1 mg/kg, s.c.) was administered to pregnant rats at different periods of gestation; rats born to mothers who received drug upto sixth day before delivery showed early postnatal adrenal catecholamine depletion attributable to a direct action of the drug. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Reserpine decreased calcium content in brain, smooth and skeletal muscles and liver of rabbits, rats and mice 24 hr. after treatment. Pretreatment with antidepressants inhibited calcium-depleting effect of reserpine in brain and aortic tissue. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·         A review of absorption, distribution, metalblism and excretion of reserpine. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        A review on chemistry, metabolism and pharmacology of reserpine and related alkaloids. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Male guinea pigs injected s.c. with different doses of reserpine (50,200 mg and 2mg) for 7 or 14 days showed increased levels of lipids and glycogen in heart. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Prolactin was not detectable in young sheep fetuses after administration of reserpine (2.5 mg, i.v.); same dose of reserpine did not raise plasma prolactin level in older fetuses, the only cardiovascular effect was slight  bradycardia; however, maternal plasma prolactin rose markedly after short delay in all animals. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Reserpine (5 mg, i. m.) caused rapid (within 30 min.) and sustained (at least 24 hr.) elevation of serum prolactin in dairy cows; peak level (283 ng/ml) was observed at 3 hr. post-injection; serum glucocorticoid and growth hormone concentrations were unaffected. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Reserpine (0.01-1mg/ml) competitively inhibited contractile response to calcium of isolated vas deferens from normal or reserpine-pretreated rats; direct anticontractile action of reserpine may be due to metabolic block making calcium less available at sites of muscle contraction. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Reserpine administered to Indian koel (0.25 mg/bird) twice on alternate days caused decrease in weight of ovary and oviduct and hypotrophy of intrafollicular masses of ovary, with disintegration of interstitial cell mass. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·         Reserpine decreased tachycardia (31%) in hyperthyroid guinea pigs, 24 hr. after injection; a slight increase in heart rate was observed due to catecholamine release. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Toxicity of reserpine in pigs was increased by hyperthyroidism induced by thyroxine. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Intracerebroventricular administration of reserpine (5-10 mg) initially enhanced urine outflow for 5-30 minutes in about 60% of rats, followed by inhibition. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Reserpine (25 mg/kg/day for 3 days, i. p.) administered to frogs, darkened their skin colour and decreased dopamine level by about 50% in heart and brain. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Reserpine (100 mg/day fro 6-18 days, s. c.) decreased rat uterine glycogen. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·        Ajmaline increased diuresis  and excretion of Na+ in rats (10 mg/kg, s. c.) and in dogs (3 mg/kg, i.v.)by increasing glomerular filtration rate and renal blood flow. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Hydrochloric acid content as well as volume of gastric juice and Na, K Mg, Ca and CI was increased in humans who performed physical exercise after i..m. administration of reserpine (2.5 mg) in contrast to normal cases where these parameters decreased. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Reserpine (1 mg/kg, i. p.) injected into rats during gestation decreased survival rate of small-for-date fetuses after birth and caused various visceral or skeletal effects. It decreased ATO content in placenta and other organs and depleted catecholamines in brain of mothers. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Reserpine administered to mice pretreated with a-methylp-tyrosine caused significant sedation within 30 min which was partially reversed by 1-dopa, suggesting decrease in adrenergic tone being responsible for its sedative action. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Administration of reserpine (0.005 mg/kg, p.o. for 14 days ) inhibited but did not completely block, development of neuropsychogenic hypertension in dogs subjected to irregular reinforcement of conditioned stimuli. Increase in plasma catecholamines associated with hypertension was also less in reserpinetreated dogs. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Reserpine (0.0001M) caused dose-dependent biphasic inhibition of monoamine uptake in rat striatal synaptosomes. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Reserpine (0.5 mg/kg, i.m. for 30 days) given to rabbits prevented increase in intraocular pressure normally produced by daily electric stimulation of ventromedial nucleus of hypothalamus. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Reserpine found to be more potent releaser of prolactin than haloperidol in man. Since there in high correlation. Between prolactin response and clinical potency of neuroleptics, reserpine could be a potent antipsychotic. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Noradrenaline depleting and Ca-depleting effects of reserpine on heart in rat may be due to its symkpathomimetic effects; the periods of greatest noradrenaline and Ca-depletion being coincident. Tyramine pretreatment diminished effects of reserpine on Ca in rat heart, but its administration 1 hr. after reserpine enhanced loss of Ca. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Reserpine (1 mg) administered to dogs and after 24 hr femoral artery was removed. Relaxant action of glyceryl trinitrate, but not that of adenosine, on these arterystrips which were contracted in vitro woth noradrenaline, was enhanced due to reserpine pretreatment. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Reserpine (2.5 mg/kg/day for 2days) pretreatment of guinea pigs increased Ca2+ concentration of 0.7-5.0 mM; higher doses depressed uptake. Apparently, reserpine pretreatment in a dose which produced inotropic supersensitivity to Ca2+ also markedly increased SR/Ca2+ uptake. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        In rats, i.v. injection of reserpine produced a small but significant hyperthermic effect followed by a persistent decrease in temperature. Hyperthermic effect was significant by potentiated in animals pretreated with amphetamine and monoamine oxidase inhibitors. However, in anaesthetized rats, hypothermic response was delayed and was not as marked as in conscious animals. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Yohimbine induced a dose-dependent antidiuretic response without modification of daily water uptake in normal animals but produced no response in dogs with diabetes insipidus; these findings suggested that it stimulated vasopressin secretion. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Chronic treatment of rats withpyhimbine stimulated spermatogenesis which appeared to be due to increase in mitotic activity in spermatogonia. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Effect of yohimbine on response tonociceptive stimulation mediated by supraspinal structures was studied. Low yohimbine doses (0.35-2 mg/kg,s.c.) dose-dependently facilitated nociception whereas high doses (8-25 mg/kg) induced antinociception . (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Small dose of yohimbine (0.1 mg/kg, i.v.) increased systolic blood pressure, heart rate and cardiac performance in anaesthetized dogs whereas larger dose (1.0 mg/kg, i.v.) influenced cardiovascular system in opposite manner. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Extract exhibited activity against vescular stomatitis virus in monkey cell culture. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989).

·        Yohimbine (4 mg/kg, s.c.) caused time-related increase in serum rennin activity and heart rate in conscious rats. although, arterial pressure was not decreased significantly at 30min post-injection, serum rennin activity remained elevated for upto 2 hr. with 9-fold increase occurring at 30 min. Highest dose (10 mg/kg) significantly lowered blood prerssure. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Serpentine (400 mg/day) showed activity against mammary cancer MS 301 in mice. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·        Cytological effects of yohimbine and ajmalicine studied on cells of Allium cepa roots; both compounds showed reversible mitodepressant effect and possessed differing degrees of clastogenic properties. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989).

·        Yohimbine (5.0-10.0 mg/kg, i.p.) elevated seizure threshold in normal mice in a concentration dependent manner but showed no significant effect on seizure threshold in audiogenic  seizure-prone (ASP) mice. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989).

·        Yohimbine showed anticonvulsant activity against maximal electroconvulsions in mice and rats with peak effect at 15-30 min, to decreased ED50 of several anticonvulsants including phenobarbital. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989).

·        Yohimbine when administered i.p. once a day for 8 days, increased serum prolactin concentration but when given orally in feed for 7 days, it decreased the concentration. These effects were dependent on dosage and route of administration. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989).

·        Yohimbine produced a reversible and concentration-dependent decrease in maximum upstroke velocity and a slight increase in action potential duration in isolated canine ventricular myocyten. It (0.1 mM) reduced upstroke velocity by >90.0%. Studies suggested that yohimbine can produce local anaesthetic effect, primarily due to inhibition of sodium channels. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989).

·        Yohimbine reduced morphine tolerance in longitudinal muscle-myenteric plexus strips of guinea pig ileum. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989).

·        Effects of yohimbine in capsaicin – induced depressor reflex in rat determined; yohimbine (5.0 mg, intrathecal) given before clonidine abolished inhibitory effect of clonidine on blood pressure response to capsaicin and when given intracisternally in 20.0 mg) did not change mean blood pressure but showed dual effect on depressor reflex in response to capsaicin injection, a short period of enhanced reflex response followed by a long – lasting inhibition of response. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989).

·        Yohimbine (10.0 mg/kg, p.o.) caused increase in plasma nor – adrenaline and almost no change in plasma glucose and adrenaline in pentobarbital anaesthetized rats. It potentiated urethane induced elevation in plasma nor – adrenaline but partially inhibited urethane – induced increase in plasma adrenaline and glucose; thus yohimbine inhibited urethane-induced hyperglycaemia via its inhibitory action on adrenaline secretion and also through its blocking action on peripheral a-adrenoceptors. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989).

·        Yohimbine (5.0 mg/kg, i.v.) increased both resting and parasympathetic and sympathetic stimulation – induced submaxillary salivary secretion for a period of 45 min in anaesthetized dogs. Studies indicated that yohimbine increased submaxillary secretion by inhibition of presynaptic a2-adrenoceptors located on chorda tympani nerve which inhibited cholinergic transmission. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989).

·        Effect of yohimbine and caffeine investigated in social interaction, elevated plus –maze and punished – drinking tests of anxiety in rats. Yohimbine (2.5 mg/kg, i.p.) was anxiogenic – like effects in social interaction and plus-maze tests. However, caffeine and yohimbine antagonized each other in social interaction and elevated plus – maze tests. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989).

·        Administration of reserpine (1.0 mg/kg/day) to rats for 7 days resulted in a marked decrease in a corticosterone – sensitive component of extraneuronal accumulation of (3H) – isoprenaline into their atria. Reserpine in vitro did not inhibit extraneuronal uptake. Studies indicated that reserpine – induced change in accumulation of [3H] – isoprenaline was dependent upon functional adrenergic innervation. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989).

·        Effect of reserpine on the contraction of isolated saphenous artery of rabbit by stimulation of sympathetic nerves studied. In vessels exposed to reserpine, substantial contraction was recorded despite a 95.7% reduction in noradrenaline content of tissue. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989).

·         Raubasine inhibited platelet biological activity in patients with marked predisposition to complications of atherosclerosis. Platelet aggregation was inhibited in 85.7% of patients, plasma fibrinogen was reduced in 71.4% and platelet count was increased in most of the patients. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989).

·        A single injection of reserpine produced bradycardia reaching maximum effect in 8 hr. Sensitivity of heart to acetylcholine was increased at 24 hr. after reserpine injection and effect of reserpine was related to increase in concentration of acetylcholine in heart (20.8% in atria and 113.3% in ventricals). Maximum bradycardiac effect of reserpine was associated with decreased in acetylcholinesterase activity whereas increased sensitivity to acetylcholine was associated with increase in choline acetyltransferase. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).

·        Acetylcholine accumulated in heart of rats in early period following reserpine administration (within 8hr) because of inhibition of hydrolysis of mediator. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).

·        Reserpine (1.0 mg/kg, i.p.) not only reduced cAMP levels in both cerebral  cortex and hippocampus in WCI mice but also potentiated degree of seizures elicited by CL (coriaria lactone). Reserpine injection could shorten latency of CL-seizures and decrease its threshold. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).

·        Level of tele - methylhistamine, a major metabolite of histamine (HA), was decreased by reserpine (1.0 – 5.0 mg/kg, s.c.) in dose – dependent manner in all regions of brain in mice, whereas  HA level was unaffected. Results suggest that there may be both reserpine – resistant and reserpine – sensitive HA pools in histaminergic nerve endings and most of neuronal HA in brain may be located in former pool. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).

·        Study of effect of reserpine on vascular and intestinal smooth muscles showed that it inhibits L – type Ca2+ channels to inhibit smooth muscle contraction. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).

·        Yohimbine hydrochloride (5.0, 10.0 mg/0.01 ml/rat) or vehicle injected i.c.v. in 52 weeks old male Wistar rats. Each male was then given opportunity to mate with receptive female for 30 min. Yohimbine produced decrease in latency to initial mounting and increase in number of mountings. However, there was no ejaculation inyohimbine and vehicle – treated males. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).

·        Yohimbin (2.0 mg/kg) known to facilitate coupling behaviour in male rats, caused decrease in locomotor activity. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).

·        Yohimbine reduced neuropathology (formation of vacuoles in large cortical neurons and neuropil) induced by ketamine/zylazine anaesthesia, speeding recovery after surgical procedure. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).

·        Pre-treatment of dogs with yohimbine prevented xylazine – induced retrograde flow of yohimbine (0.5 mg/kg, i.v.) to anaesthetized dogs. Sectioning of chorda tympani nerve abolished yohimbine – induced increase in resting salivary secretion. Results showed that yohimbine increases submaxillary secreation by inhibition of presynaptic a - adrenoceptors located on chorda tympani which inhibit cholinergic transmission. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).

·        Effect of yohimbine on ATP – sensitive K+ channels studied in normal mouse b-cells. In presence of 3.0 mM glucose which opened ATP – sensitive K+ channels, yohimbine (20.0 to 10.0 mM) inhibited  86Rb efflux from islets. However, in presence of  6.0 mM when most of the channels are closed, this efflux was unaffected by yohimbine (200.0mM). (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).

·        Yohimbine (1.0 mg/kg/hr, i.v.) caused slight traffic. It also enhanced and total body spillover of norepinephrine at any given firing rate of renal sympathetic nerves. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).

·        Rauvolscine elicited sustained caontraction in dog mesenteric artery precontracted with 20.0 mM KCI or 1.) nM endothelin 1; results suggest that rauvolscine is a partial agonist with lower intrinsic acitivity than 5 – HT and acts at the same 5 – HT1 – like receptors. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).  

·        Role of Rauwolfia serpentina root extract in the regulation of hyperthyroidism in mice. In L-thyroxine  (50   mug/100 g for 30 days)-treated mice, an increase in serum  concentrations of   both thyroid hormones (thyroxine and triiodothyronine) and in hepatic glucose-6  -phosphatase activity was observed. Daily administration of the plant extract  (2.5 mg kg-1) either alone or with thyroxine for 30 days decreased  concentrations of both thyroid hormones, indicating the possible regulation of   hyperthyroidism by the plant extract. The plant extract also decreased hepatic   lipid peroxidation and increased superoxide dismutase and catalase  activity in  hyperthyroid mice without hepatotoxic effects. R. serpentina root extract might   be a potentially effective treatment for hyperthyroidism. (Panda and Kar, 2000)

·        The disc diffusion method confirmed the antimicrobial activity of Rauwolfia   serpentina at concentrations ranging from 50-1000 mg against 7.0X109 cells of S. aureus, Y. enterocolitica, S. typhi, S. typhimurium, E. coli and C.   albicans. The minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) of R. serpentina crude   extract was 250 mg/ml as determined by tube dilution method against S. aureus and Y. enterocolitica. It was found that the MIC in case of E. coli, S. typhi, S. typhimurium and C. albicans was 500 mug/ml indicating that R.  serpentina was   more effective against S. aureus and Y. enterocolitica than other pathogens  tested. But the zone of inhibition formed at 1000 mug concentration suggests that this herbal extract was much more effective against Y. enterocolitica (29mm). These results indicate that R. serpentina has a potent broad spectral antimicrobial activity. (Elizabeth, 2001)

·        Effect of Rauwolfia radix on heat shock   protein (HSP) 70 expression and cytotoxicity against tumor cells in activated   human T cells. When activated T cells were cultured with Rauwolfia radix for 18   h, HSP70 expression after heat shock was remarkably increased, and cytotoxicity   against T98G tumor cells was augmented. Moreover, Rauwolfia radix also enhanced   the cytotoxicity of heat shocked activated T cells against Molt-4 and T98G   tumor cells. Secretions of interferon-gamma (IFN-gamma) and tumor necrosis alpha (TNF-alpha), due to Concanavalin A (Con A) stimulation, were increased by   Rauwolfia radix in activated T cells. To investigate the antitumor effect in   vivo, EL-4 tumor-bearing mice were administered with Rauwolfia radix in   drinking water. The survival period of the Rauwolfia radix treatment group was   significantly prolonged compared with that of the control group.  Reserpine, the   major active ingredient of Rauwolfia radix, also enhanced the cytotoxicity of activated T cells against Molt-4 and T98G tumor cells, and prolonged the survival period of EL-4 tumor-bearing mice. Taken together, our results suggest that Rauwolfia radix can enhance the activity of immune cells against tumor cells. (Jin et al., 2002)
