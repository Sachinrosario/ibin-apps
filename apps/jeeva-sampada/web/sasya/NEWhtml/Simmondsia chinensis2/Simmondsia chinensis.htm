Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Simmondsia chinensis (Jojoba) is a woody evergreen shrub or small multi-stemmed tree native to the Sonoran Desert in southern Arizona, California and Baja California in Mexico. Throughout its natural range located between 23° and 34° north latitude and between 110deg. and 118deg. west longitude, jojoba grows from sea level to 1500 meters in altitude. Jojoba can be grown on an array of soil types from sands to loams, but the soil must be well drained. It grows best where the annual rainfall exceeds 30 cm, but does exist where less than 12.5 cm occurs. It tolerates full sun and temperatures ranging from 0° to 47°C. Mature shrubs tolerate temperatures as low as -10°C, but seedlings are sensitive to light frosts just below freezing. In India the crop is distributed in Rajasthan, Pune in Maharashtra, Gujarat and Tamil Nadu (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Simmondsia chinensis is a multibranched tree that typically grows to a height of 10 to 15 ft. Leaves thick, leathery, bluish-green, oblong, opposite, oval or lanceolate, 2.5–3.5 cm long, entire and are xerophytic with a thick cuticle to reduce moisture loss, sunken stomata and the plant is long-lived (100–200 years). The plant develops one or a few long tap roots (up to 40 ft) that can supply water and minerals from far below the soil surface. The fruit is a green capsule encloses upto three seeds (3, 4, 6, 7, 9, 10, 11).

Jojoba is usually dioecious (male and female flowers are borne on separate plants). Flowers apetalous, the female flowers are small, pale green and commonly solitary or in clusters at the nodes. Male flowers are yellow, larger, and occur in clusters with 10–12 stamens per flower; female flowers are small with 5 pale greenish sepals, soft and hairy. fruits ovoid, usually dehiscent, with 1 to 3 peanut-sized, brown seeds each, the endosperm scanty or absent (3, 4, 6, 7, 9, 12).

The fruit is a green capsule which encloses up to three dark brown seeds that normally ranges in their dry weight between 0.5-1.1 g and contain 44-56% wax. When ripe (3 to 6 months after fertilization) the capsule splits and reveals the seed, which is brown, wrinkled and about the size of a small olive seeds weighing about 750 to 5,150 per kg (3, 6, 7, 9).

Breeding System :
Jojoba is a monotypic genus with a dioecious flowering habit and is a cross-pollinated crop. Anthesis occurs in the spring when the soil and air temperature rise to above 15°C. Severe water stress prevents opening of flowers.

Genetic variability in morphology, anatomy and physiology within the species is very large and enable selection of clones for high yield and other agricultural attributes. Flower buds (and later fruit) are typically produced at every other node on new growth near branch tips of jojoba, Simmondsia chinensis (Link) Schneid. Because of this, the number of nodes produced puts an upper limit on the number of flowers, fruit, and seed that a plant can produce. Casual observation of many plants and clones suggested that, in general, highly branched plants have more flowers and are more productive, and indicated that this could be a useful trait to select for in a breeding program. To begin to test this, variability of this trait must be quantified to obtain an estimate of its heritability (7, 12, 13).

Phenology :
Flower buds form in the axiles of leaves solely on the new vegetative growth occurring during the warm season under favorable temperatures and water regime. New flower buds are dormant and will open only after a cool season with enough cold units for the fulfillment of their chilling requirements. The staminate and pistillate flower buds usually develop in the spring and late summer on new growth and remain dormant until warm weather in late winter or early spring. Fruit maturation in Arizona occurs about five months after pollination during the summer months of July and August. Fruits ripens during the spring and early summer and seeds fall to the ground in late summer (3, 5, 9).

Pollinators:
Pollination occurs through wind or insects.

Seed Biology :

Seedling Germination :
Germination is hypogeal. Jojoba seeds retain nearly 99% germination capacity after 6 months and 38% after 11 years stored in an open shed. Germination is good in alkaline sands at temperatures of 27°–38°C. Jojoba seeds lose viability relatively rapidly in laboratory storage at room temperature (from 100% to <60% after 2 years), but they are apparently still orthodox in storage behavior. When stored at low moisture content and temperature, seedlots have retained high viability for 10 to 12 years. Under natural conditions, jojoba seeds do not form a persistent seedbank; all seeds either germinate, lose viability, or are consumed within a year of production.

Jojoba seeds require no pretreatment and are usually readily germinable immediately after harvest. It takes 3-7 days for the first emergence of the radicle. Seedlots of large seeds germinated more quickly and to higher percentages than did lots of small seeds, suggesting that seed size is associated with germination polymorphism (3, 6, 7, 9).

Cultivation:

Climate: Natural stands of jojoba occur in areas that receive 3 to 18 inch. of precipitation annually. Irrigation has produced more luxuriant vegetative growth, but it is not known whether this increased growth results in higher seed yield. Jojoba requires the most water during late winter and early spring.

Jojoba is propagated by direct seeding at depths of 2-5 cm in wet, light or medium textured soils with good drainage and good water infiltration. Planting on heavy soil results in later blooming, slower growth and more problems with fungal diseases.

Seed bed Preparation: Jojoba plantations are established by clearing and leveling a site prior to seeding.

Seeding Date: Jojoba can be seeded or transplanted to the field when the soil temperature reaches 70°F. Low soil temperature may delay emergence by as much as 2 to 3 months.

Method and Seed rate: Seeds are planted 1 inch. deep, and emergence usually occurs within 20 days. The soil should be kept moist but not wet through emergence.

Spacing: Individual seeds or seedlings are planted 12 to 18 inch. apart in rows. With hand harvesting and cultivation, rows can be as close as 10 ft. To obtain the proper female: male ratio (6:1), it is advisable to over-plant (7 to 9 lb/acre of seeds) and rogue out excess males later. As male plants flower, they should be thinned out to 1 male every 40 ft on the row. As female plants flower, usually in the third year, any slow-growing or unproductive plants are thinned out, leaving 1 female plant in every 2 to 3 ft on the row.

Fertility and Lime Requirements: Fertilizer is applied with the irrigation water, mainly nitrogen phosphate and potassium. Jojoba grows wild on soils of marginal fertility with soil pH ranging from 5 to 8. Apply enough dolomitic lime according to soil test recommendations to raise soil pH to 6.0

Jojoba can be propagated from softwood stem cuttings taken in the spring or summer. Preparation of rooted cuttings of jojoba is done in a greenhouse with substrate temperatures of 30°C and mist applied for 10 seconds every 8-10 min. Three-node young branches are held for 48 hours in a refrigerator, dipped in rooting powder, and placed in peat containers. Rooting occurs within three to five weeks. The rooted cuttings are transferred to final pots after about four to five weeks and gradually hardened by transferring to unheated tables, and then to a net house (3, 4, 6, 7, 12).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

More than 100 species of insects have been identified on jojoba, but few cause known economic damage. A scale insect that inhabits the leaves is not detrimental. Harmful pest is micro lepidopoterous insect, that destroys a large part of the wild crop by consuming the very young ovules. One spraying at the proper time might eliminate this damage. The scale Situlaspis yuccae and the unique mealybug Puto simmondsia have been reported. Infestations by spider mites, grasshoppers, and thrips may result in yield losses. Fences may be necessary to eliminate browsing by wild animals who find the plant very palatable (4, 6, 7).

Diseases:

On poorly drained soil, jojoba is susceptible to fungal wilts, including Verticillium, Fusarium, Pithium and Phytopthora. It has been found that during the propagation of cuttings the dominant phytopathological problems in the nursery are caused by two species of Fusarium, F. solani and F. oxysporum. The symptoms of fusarium disease may include wilt and defoliation of leaves, which develop into desiccation and death of the plant. The infection may lie dormant and the disease can take hold in quite well-developed plants three and four years after the infection.

In addition, a number of other pathogens were observed: Alternaria spp., Pythium spp., Phytophthora sp., Rhizoctonia solani, Cylindrocladium sp., Diplodia sp., Colletotrichum sp., Phoma sp. and Erwinia spp (4, 6, 7).

Economic Potential and Uses

Seeds of jojoba contain from 40-60% of a chemically unique oil, which is more accurately characterized as a long straight-chain liquid wax of non-glyceride esters. Its chemical structure is very similar to, and can substitute readily for sperm whale oil. Simmondsia is unique among plants in that its seeds contain an oil which is a liquid wax. It is light yellow, unsaturated, of unusual stability, remarkably pure. The oil does not become rancid, is not damaged by repeated heating to temperatures over 295°C or by heating to 370°C for four days; the color is dispelled by heating for a short time at 285°C, does not change in viscosity appreciably at high temperatures, and requires little refining to obtain maximum purity.

  • Oils and Waxes of Jojoba are useful as chemical feedstocks for a wide array of industrial products including high pressure and other specialized lubricants, antifoaming agents, detergents, disinfectants, driers, emulsifiers, fibers, plasticizers, protective coatings, resins, and surfactants. The purity, lack of odour, and resistance to rancidity, which are useful characteristics in a base for creams and ointments, have stimulated the initial use of jojoba oil in the cosmetic industry.

  • The wax and its derivatives have a wide range of industrial uses, mainly in cosmetics in which it is incorporated in formulations for skin care preparations such as lotions, moisturizers, massage oils, and soothing creams. It is also widely used in hair care products, such as shampoos, gels and mousses, and is a very good base for lipstick, makeup and nail products. There are many potential uses in pharmaceuticals, and in industry (as extenders for plastics, printer's ink, gear-oil additives, various lubricants etc.).

  • The liquid ester is chemically almost identical to spermaceti oil which is also a pure ester found in the head of the Sperm whale. Jojoba is the ideal substitute for the oil of the Sperm whale which was used for high temperature lubrication, cosmetics and as a lubricant for automatic transmissions. Jojoba has played a major role in conservation of whales.

  • Jojoba "oil" is a natural mimic of the oil secreted by human skin so it may be used to protect and lubricate skin and hair. It is soothing, stops multitudinous skin problems and protects against premature aging and wrinkling of the skin caused by exposure to ultra violet radiation.

  • Jojoba oil can be easily hydrogenated into a hard white wax, with a melting point of about 73°–74°C, and is second in hardness only to carnauba wax. The oil is a potential source of both saturated and unsaturated long-chain fatty acids and alcohols. It is also suitable for sulfurization to produce lubricating oil and a rubber-like material (factice) suitable for use in printing ink and linoleum. The residual meal from expression or extraction contains 30–35% protein and is acceptable as a livestock food (1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22).

Uses

Seeds were said to be palatable and were eaten raw or parched by Indians. They may also be boiled to make a well-flavoured drink similar to coffee, hence the name coffeberry. Plants are extremely tolerant of drought and their foliage is a source of nutritious forage for sheep, goats, and cattle, as well as for wild ungulates and smaller browsers such as rabbits (6, 7, 9).

Harvesting Practices
In the wild, the only method for harvesting has been hand collecting from under the plants, since mature seeds fall from the bush. Under cultivation, seeds may be collected manually or by machines. In hedge-row or orchard-like plantations, without undergrowth, seeds could be raked from under the bushes and then picked up by suction. Pruning the lower branches might be advantageous if this method be used. Several machines are available: one is based on vacuum and the other is a pickup that collects the seed from a central wind-row into which it has been concentrated by means of a blower and a sweeper Harvesting efficiency can be improved by using a shaker that causes the seeds that still adhere to the plant to fall onto the ground and a blower to push the seeds out from under the bushes. All seeds on a jojoba shrub do not mature at the same time, and more than one harvest may be necessary. A device could be designed to pick the seeds from the bush prior to the time of falling. Cost of harvesting would depend on the method (4, 6, 7).

Produce and Products

Jojoba oil- contains straight- chained C20 and C22 fatty acids and alcohols and two unsaturated bonds, which make the oil susceptible to many different types of chemical manipulations. The extracted oil is relatively pure, non-toxic, biodegradable, and resistant to rancidity. Jojoba wax (called oil)- makes up 50% of the seed's dry weight. Jojoba seed contains a light-gold colored liquid wax ester which is the primary storage lipid of the plant. This is unlike conventional oilseed crops, such as soybean, corn, olive, or peanut which produce oils as the primary storage lipid.

The seed meal- contains up to 30% protein, but toxic compounds (simmondsins) make it currently hazardous as an animal feed after detoxification.

Chemistry

Jojoba oil or liquid wax is made up of straight-chain esters of mono-unsaturated long chain fatty acids and fatty alcohols and has an average total carbon chain length of 42 carbons. At room temperature, it is a light yellow liquid and does not become rancid or damaged by prolonged exposure to high temperatures. The wax can be isomerized, hydrogenated, sulphurized, chlorinated or transesterified. These waxes are esters of long chain (mostly; C20, C22, and C24), monounsaturated, fatty acids and alcohols.

Processing Techniques

The nuts contain 50 – 60 % oil, which can be extracted almost completely from the deshelled, granulated fruit. Around 35 – 40 % of a particular high-value oil is obtained by cold pressing (screw pressing) and a further 15 – 20 % of a lower-value oil is extraction of the pressed cake. The oil is purified by simple filtration. Hydrogenation converts jojoba oil into a very useful white crystalline wax (4, 6, 7, 16, 18, 19).

Other Uses
The fruit is used as food and the oil as a medicine for cancer and kidney disorders. Indians in Mexico use the oil as a hair restorer. Indigenous Amerinds in the Sonora Baja California and Seri Indians used jojoba seed and oil for cooking and for many medical problems. Jojoba is used as a folk remedy for emetic, cancer, colds, dysuria, eyes, head, obesity, parturition, poison ivy, sores, sore throat, warts, and wounds. They drank jojoba-ade for colds and to facilitate parturition (4, 7).

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

At present Jojoba is being grown in an estimated area of about 335 hectares. Of this, 90 per cent is in Rajasthan. The rest is Pune in Maharashtra, Gujarat and Tamil Nadu. The cost of production of Jajoba depending on land availablity, irrigation facility including tube well has varied from Rs 15,000 to Rs one lakh for a period of first four years. The cost of production of one kg of Jajoba in India is comparatively less as compared to production in Israel and the United States. The cost of production of one kg of Jajoba in Israel is Rs 32 and in US it is Rs 68. This is a cutting edge for India to venture in huge commercial production. A kilogram of Jajoba oil commands a price tag of US $ 90, while the price for bulk supplies range between US $ 13 to 40 in the international market.

An extraordinarily high market demand exits for a sperm oil substitute, which can be satisfied to great extent by Jojoba oil. The value of jojoba oil as an alternative industrial oil with multiple applications and as a replacement for non-renewable fossil petroleum has been demonstrated. This demand is a result of the world wide prohibition of whaling, even though the latter is not obeyed by all countries (7, 8).

Export Potential

Jojoba oil is available in insufficient quantities at a very high price. Its use is therefore limited to cosmetics and pharmaceuticals. As soon as reliable quantities can be supplied at acceptable prices, the demand may increase. This oil can then be used as a raw material in high-performance lubricants and in modified form in plasticizers and stabilizers. Oil seems to have to have lot of industrial significance and the plants suits well to the dry areas. As such there are no available records of seed oil exports from India (7, 8).

Miscellaneous Information

Rancidity: The non rancidity of the jojoba oil is due to : The "oil" from the seeds is unusual in that it is not an oil but a pure liquid ester. The difference between an oil and an ester is small and yet large in terms of properties. Vegetable oils have several alcohol groups on the molecule and some have forked molecules and will eventually oxidize and become rancid. Jojoba has only one alcohol group and is a straight chain molecule; therefore it is not subject to oxidizing and in fact is an anti-oxidant and will never become rancid. However, the liquid ester does have the ability to self polymerize in the presence of sunlight, so it is best to keep it in brown glass bottles, in the dark, or in closed metal cans. The physical properties of jojoba oil are: high viscosity, high flash and fire point, high dielectric constant, high stability and low volatility. Its composition is little affected by temperatures up to 570°F (300°C).

Toxicity:

The plant contains a group of glycosides, known as food intake inhibitors with simmondsin [2-(cyano-methylene)-3-hydroxy-4,5-dimethoxycyclohexyl-b-glucoside] being the most important toxic factor. These glycosides are found in leaves, stems, roots as well as in jojoba meal after oil extraction. Seeds contain 2.25–2.34%, seed hulls, 0.19%. Core wood, 0.45; leaves, 0.19–0.23%; twigs, 0.63–0.75%; an inflorescence, 0.22%; simmondsin, a demonstrated appetite depressant and toxicant (23).

Bio synthesis of wax in the plant:

As in other plants, jojoba fatty acids up to 18 carbons in length are synthesized in plastids. Oleic acid (18:1) is exported from plastids, and its CoA esters serve as substrates for microsomal membrane enzymes involved in wax formation. Three enzyme activities are involved in the formation of waxes:

  1. Fatty acid elongase (FAE) is a complex of four enzymes that converts 18:1 to eicosenoic acid (20:1), erucic acid (22:1), and nervonic acid (24:1).

  2. An acyl-CoA reductase catalyzes the formation of fatty alcohols from 20:1-, 22:1-, and 24:1-CoAs.

Wax synthase is an acyltransferase that forms wax esters from fatty alcohol and acyl-CoA substrates (13, 22).
