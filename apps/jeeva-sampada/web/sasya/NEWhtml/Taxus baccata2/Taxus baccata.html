 Other Biological Details
HABIT & HABITAT
An evergreen tree, found in the temperate Himalayas at an altitude between 1800 and 3,000 m and in the hills of Meghalaya and Manipur. Yew-bearing forests are in Talle Valley area falling in Lower Subansiri district of Arunachal Pradesh.

Age Very long lived - Maybe even 2000 years.  Often occurs in the dense shade of Oak woods. Used for hedging and topiary.

The common yew is a large evergreen tree, native throughout Europe, and very commonly planted as an ornamental tree in church yards, cemeteries, etc., especially in England.  It is of very slow growth, and has a hard, close-grained wood that is much used by cabinet-makers. The branches are long and horizontal, the lowest ones proceeding from the trunk at only a few feet from the ground. The bark is of a dark-brown color, and does not split longitudinally, like the bark of most trees, but scales off in thin plates.

It needs full sun or partial shade and it can grow on any fertile, well-drained soil.

Availability : 
Found throughout Britain and Ireland. Native to most of Europe, Asia Minor and North Africa.
Yew used to be more widely spread in Europe than it is today and is the only member of its family to grow wild.  It avoids areas with marked continental climates and occurs locally in southern Scotland, England, Ireland and Wales. It is also indigenous to central and southern Europe, Algeria, and northern Spain. Yew woods are not common and never have been , but can achieve local dominance in some situations, doing particularly well in mild oceanic climates . In Britain it extends northwards to Perth and Argyle, and its limit is set by the tree’s intolerance to winter cold, which also relates to its late appearance in the historical record of the British Isles. The distribution of yew is primarily based on edaphic conditions.

Status : Used for hedging and topiary.
Yew has an extraordinarily high resistance to urban air pollution. Most of the species are used as ornamentals.

Taxus baccata is, within the temperate zone, among the commonest ornamental conifers.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Medium sized evergreen tree usually 6-8 m in height and 1.5-1.8 m in girth. Bark reddish brown, thin and scaly. Leaves distichous, linear with recurved margin, shining above, pale-yellowish brown or rusty red below.  Flowers usually dioecious; male strobili stalked, globose, arising from the axil of the leaves on the underside of the branchlets of the previous year, each consisting of 6-14 stamens with short filaments; female strobili  solitary, axillary;  seeds borne in a scarlet, fleshy cup (called arils). Single seed in each aril. Chromosome no: x=12.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Breeding System :


Flowers unisexual, with the sexes invariably on different trees, produced in spring from the leaf axils of the preceding summer's twigs. Male, a globose cluster of stamens; female, an ovule surrounded by small bracts, the so-called fruit bright red, sometimes yellow, juicy and encloses the seed.
Phenology :

Fl.: March-April

Fr.: Aug-Sep.

Pollinators:


Pollination is by wind. 

Seed Biology :


Seed dispersal is primarily by birds, which eat the seeds in the aril and subsequently excrete viable seed.

Single seed in each aril. 10,000 seeds per kg.

Seedling Germination :
Propagation is through seeds, which do not germinate until the second year, and from cuttings. Young plants require shelter and deep shade; does not thrive in areas where forests have been cleared.

Deeply dormant seed needing treatment from collection in autumn to the spring of the following season. 

Also propagated from cuttings and this may prove to be easiest method.

Cultivation:
Trim or renovate in summer or early autumn.  Rather unusually for conifer, yews respond to renovatory pruning.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Wood, young shoots, leaves, aril

Uses

  • The leaves and small branches of Taxus baccata produce resin, which can be used as an anticarcinogen. . 

  • At present the resin is processed by Dabur only up to three stages. Taxus baccata extraction is used in preparing medicine for some forms of breast cancer. And it is high in the list of plants cultivated in high altitudes of Nepal under Dabur’s scheme.


Harvesting Practices
Up to 72 kg of fresh leaves per day can be harvested, equivalent to 36 kg of dried leaves

Produce and Products
Timber, resin, Zarnab-unani medicine

Chemistry
The foliage and seeds contain several alkaloids, in particular taxine, very poisonous, which alters to hydrotaxine by hydrolysis. Also one glucoside, taxicatine is present. The wood, bark, foliage and seeds are toxic. The foliage is the principal source of taxine.

The poisonous principle of yew resides in an alkaloid taxine, first isolated from the leaves by H. Lucas (Archiv der Pharm., 1856, Vol. CXXXV, p. 145), and later from the leaves and fruits by Marmé (Amer. Jour. Pharm., 1876, p. 353), and by A. Hilger and Fr. Brande (ibid., 1890, p. 297). According to Marmé, it is a white crystalline powder, little soluble in water, soluble in ether, alcohol, chloroform, etc.; insoluble in petroleum-ether. It melts at 80° C. (176° F.), and produces a red color with concentrated sulphuric acid. Amato and Caparelli (ibid 1881 p. 56) obtained from the leaves a probably similar alkaloid, volatile oil, and a non-nitrogenous, crystalline substance, which they called milouin.

Other Uses
  1. Timber: Brown very durable wood & takes exceptional fine and lasting polish. Used in cabinet work, candle-sticks and other fancy articles, such as handles of knives, backs of combs, wood carving. Furniture, veneers, parquet-flooring and panelling, gates, fences, carrying poles, in retaining walls for ploughs, waterwheel-cogs, cheap-grade pencils and for turnery. Wood is also burnt as incense,
  2. Young shoots - medicinal tincture made from the young  shoots for the treatment of headache, giddiness, feeble and falling pulse, coldness of the extremities, diarrhoea and severe biliousness.
  3. Leaves are credited with emmenagogue and antispasmodic properties. Treatment of hysteria, epilepsy and nervousness, as a lithic in calculus complaints.
  4. T. baccata – source of the drug ZARNAB – prescribed in the Unani system of Medicine.
  5. The red aril surrounding the seed can be eaten just as it is like delicacy, not chewing the seed. It is sweet and very mucilaginous. The arils removed from the seeds, have diuretic and laxative effects . It is credited with carminative , expectorant and stomachic properties
  6. Extract of T. baccata  can be added in cosmetics, such as hair-lotions, rinses,  and shaving creams and dentifrices.
  7. It is the source of the anti-cancer alkaloid "Taxol".  It is also used in treatment of asthma, haemoptysis and epilepsy.

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country
The yew plant (Taxus baccata L.) has acquired importance because it yields taxol, which is effective in the treatment of cancer the world over. There is great demand for Taxus from pharmaceutical companies and the species is being extracted heavily from its natural habitats throughout the world. 

According to the Department of Forests the collection of leaves has been increasing (227 tons in 1997/1997, 253 tons in 1997/1998).

 Miscellaneous Information

The plant is exceptionally toxic, with one mouthful able to kill a horse or cow within 5 minutes. Toxicity is compounded by the apparent palatability of yew. Many animals are poisoned accidentally when yew trimmings are thrown into the pasture or when yew is planted as an ornamental within browsing reach. Infrequent reports of dogs chewing the leaves resulted in gastroenteritis, seizures, and aggressive behavior.

Excepting the pulp of the fruit, all parts of the yew tree are poisonous. Pliny, Dioscorides, and other ancient writers, mention the poisonous properties of the leaves and seed, and it has been recorded that wine, preserved in casks made of its wood, has occasioned the death of those who drank it. Strabon states that the Gauls used the juice of the leaves as a poison for their arrows. More recent observations have confirmed the statements as to its toxic character and we frequently read of animals and birds that have been poisoned by the leaves and berries. It is likewise stated that the exhalation emanating from the tree may occasion vertigo, lethargy, and a kind of drunkenness, and that even death may ensue to those who carelessly sleep beneath its branches

The leaves, seeds and twigs of the yew are deadly toxic to all livestock. The ingested leaves, bark or seeds contain a toxin known as taxine. Taxine can cause collapse and sudden death. A small quantity of the plant is all that is needed to kill a horse (0.05% of body weight or 0.5 lbs. for a 1000 lb. horse). Poisonings occur commonly from the ingestion of fresh leaves as well as from accidental ingestion when horses consume shrub trimmings.

The toxin is a mixture of alkaloids, that slow down cardiac conduction. As little as 0.1 to 0.5% of the fresh plant per body weight is lethal. Death is due to cardiac and/or respiratory collapse.

No tree is more associated with the history and legends of Great Britain than the Yew. Before Christianity was introduced it was a sacred tree favoured by the Druids, who built their temples near these trees - a custom followed by the early Christians. The association of the tree with places of worship still prevails.
