Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Deciduous, middle sized, balsamiferous tree occurs commonly on dry hills throughout greater part of India, in dry forests from Punjab to West Bengal, and in peninsular India. The tree is common at the foot of the western Himalayas, in Rajasthan, Gujrat, Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Bihar, Orissa, Andhra Pradesh and further south in the peninsula.

Tree prefers the dry, hot exposures of rocky hills, especially the volcanic traps. On poor and shallow soil or where the ground is ferruginous, it is found pure or nearly so. In North India, it is found on the southern slopes of the Sivalik hills, flanking the Himalayas, on the dry sandstone ridges associated with Acacia catechu, Anogeissus latifolia, Buchanania lanzan Spreng., Lannea coromandelica (Houtt.) Merrill, Nyctanthus arbor-tristis Linn., Zizyphus xylopyra Willd., etc. In Rajsthan, it occupies the drier ridges of hills on metamorphic rocks, chiefly gneiss and schists, and occurs either pure or mixed with Anogeissus latifolia, lannea coromandelica, Sterculia urens and sometimes Hardwickia binata Roxb. and Tectona grandis Linn.f.

The tree is able to thrive and reach fair dimensions even where other species remain stunted. In its natural habitat, the absolute maximum shade temperature varies from 38 to 49 and the absolute minimum fero -1 to 7 and the normal rainfall ranges from 50 to 125 cm.

The tree is usually gregarious and forms comparatively open forests. It is a strong light demander. It withstands fire which is frequent in the dry tracts. It is, however, frost tender.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Bark greenish grey, yellow or reddish grey, fairly thick, smooth, exfoliating in thin, papery falkes, resinous inside. Young shoots and leaves pubescent. Leaves 8-15" long, leaflets opposite, sessile, 8-15 pairs and an odd one (the pair at the base of the leaf often much smaller than the others), variable in shape, ovate or ovate-lanceolate, usually inequilateral and obtuse, crenate-serrate, more or less pubescent, base acute, rounded, or somewhat truncate.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

lowers in axillary racemes, shorter than the leaves. Calyx pubescent outside, lobes broadly triangular-ovate. Petals ovate, pubescent outside, tips inflexed. Stamens inserted at the base of an annular crenate disc, anthers slightly pubescent. Ovary surrounded by the disk; style grooved.

Drupe, trigonous; pyrenes heart shaped, cotyledons trifid.

Phenology: Flowers February – April when the tree is almost leafless.

Floral and seed biology:

Natural regeneration from seed and root suckers is usually good, even on the poorest soils.

Artificial regeneration is done through large cuttings, over 10 cm in diameter. They should be planted in the rainy season when the soil is damp. Even in the drought the cuttings are reported to root well.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Fuel: Wood as fuel. But considered to be bad fuel.

Wood: Sapwood distinct from heartwood, wide, white to dirty greyish-white, resinous and very susceptible to fungal sap-stain. Heartwood small, greyish-brown to pale yellowish or greenish-brown with darker brown wavy lines. Wood is dull with a rough feel, faintly scented. Wood moderately hard, moderately heavy, average weight 575 kg/m3 (36 lb/ft3) at 12 per cent moisture content; straight to somewhat interlocked-grained and coarse-textured.

A diffuse-porous wood. Growth rings indistinct. Pores moderately large to small, visible to the eye, distinct under hand lens, moderately few to moderately numerous, evenly distributed, solitary or in short radial multiples, filled with tyloses or brownish-yellow deposits in the heartwood; vessel lines distinct on longitudinal surfaces. Soft tissues indistinct even under the lens. Rays moderately broad, visible to the eye, distinct under the lens, rather few and somewhat widely-spaced. Radial canal present, usually visible under the lens on the tangential surface as black dots in some of the rays.

Yields valuable timber. It is suitable for manufacturing plywood, veneers, building boards (hardboards) with requisite strength and water resistance, and compreg of good quality. It is also used for inferior planking, cheap furniture, cooperage, cheap boxes and packing cases, well construction, waterpipes, hookah-stems, sheaths for knives and swords, toys and carving and boat masts. It has been used for match splints, but its liability to fungal attack is a serious disadvantage. Ammunition boxes made from this wood are reported to be better than those from teak.

Timber is commercially exploited for paper pulp.

Harvesting Practices:

It is best to separate the sapwood and heartwood before seasoning. Air seasoning of the timber was found to be unsatisfactory as the wood is very liable to to rapid decay, mould and discolouration if left in the log, and also subject to white ant and other insect attack. Therefore, it is best to convert the logs directly in the dry season after they are felled. The sawn stock should be open stacked under cover to avoid mould and stain. It can be kiln-seasoned satisfactorily. The seasoned timber is fairly durable under cover.

Other uses:

Timber

Oleo-gum-resin - The tree, on injury, exudes an oleo-gum-resin known as Indian Olibanum, Indian Frankincense or salai guggul. This is only non-coniferous source of turpentine and rosin in India. Medicinally valuable. Used in incense.

Flowers and seeds are eaten. Medicinally useful.

Silviculturally the species is most useful for clothing dry, hot and barren hills.
