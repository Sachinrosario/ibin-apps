Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Small tree found in tropical medium rainfall areas of Indo-Gangetic Plains and the Peninsula (moist tropical), dry areas of Rajasthan and Peninsular India (dry tropical). Found throughout the drier parts of India. It is indigenous to the plains of Andhra Pradesh and Maharashtra and is cultivated or found self sown throughout the drier parts of India, ascending to an altitude of 900 m. It is essentially a tree of the plains, occurring on flat or undulating ground and ravine country, seldom extending into hilly regions. Suitable for field boundaries, fields, marginal lands and village commons. In the plains of Punjab and Uttar Pradesh it is found scattered and is frequently grown along canal banks. It is occasionally found in the drier parts of Bihar, especially in Chota Nagpur, on embankments and wastelands. In central India it occurs chiefly as scattered trees in fields and wet lands and along roadside bunds and railway embankments. In southern India also, it is found in greater or less abundance throughout the plains in the drier parts of Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, but not in the moist regions of the West coast. It has also been observed in the "terai" areas of Kumaun region. Some of the common associates in babul bans are Acacia leucophloea, Prosopis cineraria Druce., Azadirachta indica A. Juss., Ziziphus jujuba Mill and Cassia auriculata Linn.

Tree is gregarious and forms pure strands or is dominant. It is a strong light demander and the crop comes up in even-aged groups or patches of varying extent. Flowering irregular. Wood hard and tough wood, resistant to termites, impervious to water.

It is characteristic of the dry regions, but does not thrive without irrigation if the climate is too arid. In the regions where it is indigenous or has become naturalized, the absolute maximum shade temperature varies from 400 to 500 and the absolute minimum from -10 to 150, and it is usually tender, but is drought resistant so long as the sub-soil moisture holds out, it is also fire resistant.

Thrives best on riverine alluvium subject to inundation and on black cotton soil. It is also found on alluvial loam in the plains of northern India, on loamy soil in the peninsula and in tank beds in Tamil Nadu. It survives even on usar (saline) soils.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Tree almost evergreen with flattish dark brown or black longitudinally fissured bark, branchlets slender, terete, pubescent when young. Leaves bipinnate, 2-4" long, main rachis downy, often with glands, stipular spines variable, sharp, straight, often absent, pinnae 4- 9 pairs. Leaflets subsessile 10- 25 pairs, linear-oblong, subobtuse, glabrous or nearly so.

Flowers yellow fragrant, in globose heads, peduncle axillary, in fascicles of 2-6, terete, pubescent. Bracteoles 2 above the middle of the peduncle, broadly ovate, acute, pubescent. Calyx companulate, teeth very short. Corolla lobes short, triangular.

Pods stalked, moniliform, compressed, constricted at the sutures between the seeds, densely and persistently Grey downy.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Breeding system and varietal improvement:

Phenology:

Leaf shedding commences before May and continues up to the end of May; the new leaves appear during March-May. The trees generally flower during the rainy season, but flowering may extend to December - January. Fruiting is usually from April to June. Trees commence to bear fruit at an early age and seed annually.

Floral and seed biology:

Seedling Germination: Grown by direct sowing. Under natural conditions germination commences early in the rainy season and continues for sometimes. For vigorous natural reproduction, complete sunlight, abundant moisture, loose soil and absence of grass and weed are essential.

Direct sowing, either in lines or on ridges, has proved successful for afforestation. Choice of a suitable site, preparation of seed, weeding and thinning at regular intervals are essential for obtaining good results.

Seeds collected from the litter of goats germinate easily. Before sowing the seed collected in April is coated with cow dung and kept as such until July, then it is sown.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Fuel: Wood popular fuel on the Indian subcontinent and large quantities are consumed as firewood and charcoal wood. It has also been used extensively to fuel locomotives and river steamers and it powers the boilers of some small industries as well. Calorific value for sapwood 4800 kcal/kg and of heartwood 4950 kcal/kg.

The exhausted tanstuff consisting of the exhausted bark and possibly pods also, is discarded as spent tan from the tanneries which consume huge amounts of babul bark blended with myrobalans. This spent tan usually goes as cheap fuel.

Wood: Sapwood distinct from heartwood, pale yellowish-white. Heartwood light red or pinkish-brown, with darker streaks, the colour often turning deeper on exposure. Wood hard, heavy, average weight about 785 kg/m3 (49 lb/ft3) at 12 per cent moisture content; somewhat coarse textured and interlocked-grained.

A diffuse porous wood. Growth rings distinct to indistinct. Pores medium-sized to large, fairly distinct to the eye, moderately numerous, mostly solitary or in short radial multiples of 2-3, often filled with dark reddish-brown gummy deposits; vessel lines distinct on longitudinal surfaces. Soft tissues predominantly vasicentric, in thin light-coloured sheaths round the pores, occasionally forming eyelets. Rays moderately broad, distinct to the eye and rather prominent under the lens, fairly widely-spaced.

Timber - It is one of the favourite timbers for native wheelwright work, being used for felloes, spokes, naves and axles for the bodies of carts and also shafts and yokes. It is used for agricultural implements such as ploughs, harrows, clod crushers and Persian wheels. It is also used to make well curbs, tent pegs, boat handles, knees for boat-building, oars, sugar- and oil-press, bedsteads, railway wagon buffers, hookah-stems, tops, dies for cloth stamping, walking sticks and for carving and turnery. It is suitable for hammer handles and is used by the railways for anvil blocks and railway keys.

Wood used for carving, boat building and turnery. Favourite wood for sugar and oil presses, Persian wheels, tent pegs and hammer handles.

Harvesting Practices:

Produce and Products:

Tannin - Bark and pods used in leather industry for tannin. Most important tanning material of northern India and is used in village tanneries from Haryana to West Bengal. A large quantity is consumed by tanning industry in Kanpur.

Leather made from babul bark is firm and durable, though harsh and dark coloured. This tannin is good for heavier leather.

Gum - although called gum arabic, is not the true gum arabic. Gum exudes from the wounds in the bark, mostly during March-May. Though some trees yield a maximum of 1kg per year, the average is only a few grammes. The yield lessens with the increase in age of the trees and it is believed that tapping accelerates the flow, but is not practised often.

Other uses:

Spines used for fishing hooks and pins.

Gum - Good quality gum is used in calico printing and dyeing, as a sizing material for silk and cotton and in the manufacture of paper. Generally it finds application in all industries where a mucilage or adhesive is required. It is used as a substitute for true gum arabic as an adhesive, though its adhesive strength is somewhat lower. It is fried in ghee and used in the preparation of sweetmeats. It was tried as a possible ice-cream stabilizer.

Food & Fodder - Seeds are eaten roasted or raw in times of acute scarcity. The ground bark mixed with seeds of sesamum indicum Linn. has been used for food. Leaves and pods used as fodder.

A decoction of the bark is used as a substitute for soap.

Thorny boughs of babul are used to fence folds.

Unripe pods are used to make ink.

Tree considered valuable for reclamation of wastelands and alkali soils.

Large quanitites of lac are collected from babul trees

.Bark - Bark yields tannin. Also yield fibre.

Miscellaneous Information

 The soluble gum is known locally as amrad, amrawatti, brown barbary gum.


Plant shows mycorrhizal association of VAM type. Glomus deserticola and Glomus occultum are found to be associated with the plant. Under water stress conditions, there is decrease in root-shoot ratio and increase in the seed yield due to VAM.


Tree is a nitrogen fixer.

The plants are micropropagated.
