Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
A genus of Deciduous trees mainly distributed in the Tropics and subtropics of the World. Bombax ceiba grows almost throughout the country except the arid tracts of Rajasthan and the temperate regions in the Himalayas. In the sub Himalayan tract, it ascends up to about 1000 m elevation and occasionally even up to 1500 m. It is commonly found on flat alluvial ground along the streams and also on grassy savannah lands where it often tends to be gregarious. It is a characteristic tree species of southern moist mixed deciduous forest, west Gangetic moist mixed deciduous forest, and low alluvial savannah woodland. Bombax ceiba grows in regions experiencing wide range of temperature and rainfall, but it thrives best in a comparatively moist tropical climate. In its natural habitat (excluding the outer ranges of Himalayas) the absolute maximum shade temperature varies from about 350 to 49°C, the absolute minimum temperature from about -4°C to 18°C and the normal annual rainfall from about 750 to 4,500 mm. The tree prefers deep, sandy loam soils. It grows well on well drained hillslopes, provided soil is moderately deep (5, 6, 7, 8).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Bombax ceiba is a large sized deciduous tree, that grows to an height of 40m and 6m or more girth, with an clear bole of 24-30m. Leaves large, spreding, digitate and glabrous. Leaflets 3-9, entire, lanceolate or oval, cuspidate and tip is acute. Petiole is long (up to 20 cm), petioles 1,2-2.5 cm long, and stipules small and caducous. The stem is straight and cylindrical, large trees are almost buttressed at base. The buttresses may extend upto 3-5m height. The bark is grey, thick and smooth in young trees and rough with longitudinal cracks in old trees. Young stems and branches are covered with large straight stout spines (5, 6, 7, 8).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Flowers solitary or clustered, large, 10-13cm in diameter, axillary or sub-terminal, fascicles at or near the ends of the branches, when the tree is bare of leaves. The flowers bisexual and protandrous in nature, rarely unisexual flowers also occurs. Calyx is cup-shaped usually 3 lobed. Corolla red or white, petals 5, oblong, recurved, fleshy, tomentose on the out side and sparingly pubescent inner. Staminal tube is short. Stamens numerous in 2 concentric rings with only the bases fused (like a comb). The stamens are loosely clustered in 5 groups in each ring. Ovary conical, glabrous, stigma 5, capsule ovoid, 5 valued dehiscing by 5 leathery, woody valves and lined with white silky hairs (5, 6, 8, 9).

Breeding System :
Red Kapok is a Cross pollinated crop. Sometimes seeds may be set by self-pollination when the stigmas and stamens of adjacent flowers come into contact.

Phenology :
The tree commences to shed leaves in the beginning of December, becoming leafless by the end of the month. The new leaves appear in March-April. The flowers appear during January-Febraury and sometimes continue upto March (5, 7).

Pollinators:
The flowers are pollinated by birds, bats and bees, when they visit the flowers mainly for nectar.

Seed Biology :
Seeds are numerous, long, ovoid, black or gray in colour and packed in white cotton. One hundred capsules (dry) weigh about 2 kg and yield about 570 g of floss and 450 g of seed. About 214-385 clean seeds weigh one gram. Fresh seeds shows high fertility and gives an average germination percentage of 82% (7).

Seedling Germination :
The seed germinates with the onset of mansoon rains under natural conditions. Germination starts in about a week and takes about four weeks to complete. Germination is epigeous. The growth of seedlings is somewhat slow during the first season. It picks up from the second season and thereafter it is fairly fast. The development of root is much faster than shoot during the first few years (6, 7).

Cultivation:

Bombax ceiba can be raised either by direct sowing or by planting out nursery raised seedlings. Planting of stumps prepared out of 1-2 year old plants is better than the above two methods. Branch cuttings also root and rooted cuttings can be used for propagation on a limited scale, but not on a field scale. In somewhat dry areas planting out of nursery raised seedlings and stump planting were found to be successful while direct sowing and planting of branch cuttings failed. Grafting is employed to raise seed orchard, and air layering and budding can both serve as alternatives to the standard cleft grafting technique.

Direct sowing is done either in lines or in patches. Soil along the lines is worked in about 30 cm wide strips while patches of about 45 X 45 cm are prepared. Spacing between the lines may be about 3.5-4.0 m and that for patch sowing is normally 3.5 X 3.5 m. Sowing is done in July with the commencement of monsoon rains. The seed should be covered only lightly. Germination percentage of about 20-60 can be expected. The lines or patches should be kept weeded and bushes likely to shade the plants are cut. For raising nursery stock, sowing in irrigated nurseries is done in May and in rain-fed nurseries in July. The seed is sown in lines about 15 cm apart if transplanting is to be done and about 25 cm apart if transplanting is not to be done. If transplanting is not to be done, the seedlings should be spaced about 20 cm in lines when these are about two months old. In nurseries where transplanting is done, about two month old seedlings are transplanted at a spacing of about 25x20 cm. The seedlings are planted out in July-August. Planting is done in 30 cm3 pits dug at a spacing of about 3.5 X 3.5 m. In good areas where fast growth rate is expected, a spacing of about 6 X 6 m is adopted.

In case of stump planting, stumps are made out of one or two year old seedlings. Stump diameter at the collar is generally 8-20 mm and planting of bigger stumps of 25 mm diameter and 60 cm length are reported to have given better results. Stumps are planted in July either in crow bar boles or in 30 cm3 pits. Planting of stumps should be done as soon as possible after their preparation. During storage and transport, the stumps should kept wrapped in moist gunny bag cloth and under shade.

This species has been raised successfully in taungya plantations also where it is sown in lines spaced about 7 m apart and agricultural crops are grown in between. The lines should be kept clear of agricultural crops for about 60 cm width to avoid suppression of the seedlings. Under jhuming cultivation, sowing is done in patches spaced about 8 x 8 m and five seeds are sown in each patch. After germination and seedling establishment, only one seedling is left per patch and the remaining are planted out in failed patches (5, 6, 7, 10).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

Tonica niviferana Walker a shoot-borer, bores through the young shoots, often killing the tree. A granular insecticide, Sevidol (6 g/plant) has also been recommended to control this shoot-borer. Batocera rufomaculata also bores the heart wood, sometimes killing the tree. Diapromorpha melanopus, Desmidophorus hebes, Hypomeces squamosa, Myllocerus lineaticollis, Lagria sp, Adoretus bimarginatus, Apogonia ferruginea, Hyposidra successaria are some of the common defoliators. The beetle Alcides affaber bores the top shoots which die above the wound; it lays eggs in these holes and the larvae tunnel down the pith for several centimetres. The larvae of Petasobathra sirina bore the shoot, the larvae of Aleurolobus simulus and Tenaphalara elongata feed upon the sap of leaves (5).

Diseases:

The silk cotton tree is subject to fungal diseases, such as root-rot caused by Ganoderma lucidum. Cladotrichum folicola and Phyllosticta bombacis are reported to cause leaf-spot, and a new species of Dicellomyces causes curling, wrinkling, necrosis and premature dropping of leaves. Species of Ceratostomella and Graphium, mould and stain fungi cause blue stain on the freshly cut logs and planks. Quick seasoning after conversion and treating with 2 per cent sodium pentachlorophenate can prevent the incidence of these diseases. Several wood-destroying fungi such as Trametes corrngata, T. meyenii, T persoonii, Lentinus sajorcaju and Polyporus friabilis, causing white spongy rot. Polystictus hirsutus causing spongy sap-rot. Schizophyllum commune causing mottled sap-rot and Pleurotus flabellatus causing white sap-rot have also been reported on silkcotton (5).

Economic Potential and Uses

Seeds yield 20-25% semi-drying oil. The unsaturation in kapok seed oil makes it ideal as edible oil and margarine for health conscious consumers and as semi-drying oil it is used in paint and varnish industries. Oil is used same way as that of cottonseed oil. The low freezing point of kapok seed oil (0oC) makes it suitable for use in salad oil. Kapok seed oil is readily saponifiable and used widely for lubrication and soap making. In addition, kapok seed oil is also polymerizable and this can be made into factice which is an additive in rubber compounding. Oil used in the manufacture of margarine and for protective coverings. To lesser extent it is used in the packing of fish and cured meats. The cake left after oil expression is a protein rich feed for ruminent livestock (5, 10).

Uses

The floss is suitable for stuffing life belts and other life saving appliances besides mattresses, cushions and pillows, upholstery and quilts. As kapok can be dry sterilised, it is used as a filling material for mattresses in hospitals. It constitutes an excellent material for making padded surgical dressings. It is now employed in the insulation of refrigerators, cold storage plants, offices, theatres, motor cars and aeroplanes and also for sound insulation in aeroplane cabins, broadcasting studios, hospitals, auditoriums. It is also employed as a packing for water pipes exposed to frost. Because of the silky smooth surface and lack of axial twist, the floss was considered unsuitable for ordinary textiles, but with suitably modified carding machines, it can now be spun easily into yarn, which is used for the manufacture of plushes, laces and few other special articles. The seeds are fed to cattle. The tender leaves are eaten, young twigs and leaves are lopped for fodder. Tender bark is used as famine food. Wood is one of the most useful product in India, as it is cheap and freely and readily available. The timber is most widely used in the match industry. It is suitable for the manufacture of cheap grade, light plywood for tea-chests, packing cases, for canoes, shingles, toys, scabbards, musical instruments, pencils, penholders, frames, light cooperage, coffins, well-curbs, brush-handles. The wood pulp admixed with other pulps is suitable for manufacturing paper for wrapping, writing and printing. The tree is an important source of honey and also suitable for soil erosion control and watershed protection. In agroforestry it is grown with coffee, cocao (5, 7, 10).

Harvesting Practices
When the fruits have turned dark brown, they can be collected from the ground or cut from the tree using hooked knives. 600-900 fruits per tree has been reported. The seeds are removed by beating with sticks or by machine (5).

Produce and Products

Seeds: Oil is extracted from the seed which is used as

-Salad and cooking oil

-Shortenings and margarine

-Packing of fish and cured meat

-Manufacture of soaps

Oil cake is used as livestock feed. Kapok of commerce is used as filling material in bedding and upholstery industries.

Other Uses
The young fruits are reported to be employed as expectorant, stimulant and diuretic and are considered beneficial in calculous affections, chronic inflammation and ulceration of the bladder and kidneys. Seeds are reported to be employed in the treatment of gonorrhoea, chronic cystitis and other catarrhal affections. The hot, aqueous extract of the seeds possesses moderate oxytocic activity. The flowers are astringent and cooling. The paste of flowers and also that of leaves is employed as an application in cutaneous troubles. The bark is mucilaginous and its infusion is given as demulcent, emetic and its aqueous extract mixed with curd is used to check blood dysentery. Externally it is used as a styptic and also for formenting wounds. Paste of the bark is applied to skin eruptions. The bark exudes a gum, from natural wounds caused probably by decay or by insects. The gum is edible. The gum and roots are regarded as astringent, tonic, and demulcent properties and is used for dysentery, haemoptysis in pulmonary tuberculosis, influenza and menorrhagia (5, 8).

Miscellaneous Information

Principal Constituents of kapok tree: Preliminary tests show the presence of glycosides and tannins from root, stem and leaf. In the stem some alkaloids and in root proteins are identified. The stem bark contains lupeol and b-sitostrol The root bark has 3 naphthalene derivatives related to gossypol (toxic principle of cotton seed) and called as 'semigossypol'. Flowers contain b-sitosterol, traces of essential oil, kaempherol and quercetin. On hydrolysis gum yield arabinose, galactose, galacturonic acid and rhamnose (8).
