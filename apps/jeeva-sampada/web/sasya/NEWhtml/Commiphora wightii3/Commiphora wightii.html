 Other Biological Details
HABIT & HABITAT
Slow growing, highly branched perennial shrub or a small tree. Grows wild in the arid, semi-arid rocky tracts of Rajasthan, Gujarat, M. P. and Karnataka, also in Sindh and Baluchistan states of Pakistan. Occurs in the arid rocky tracts of Rajputana, Khandesh, Berar.  

It is not grown on a commercial plantation scale anywhere.  But the plants are found growing naturally in arid tracts of Western India over sandy to silt-loam soils, which are poor in organic matter but rich in several other minerals.  Faster growth of the plant is observed in soils which have moisture-retaining capacity.  An average soil is suitable for its cultivation.

The plant has a wide adaptability and is found growing in arid regions under varying conditions.  Its small, leathery leaves and thick bark, covered with a white waxy coat over the stem, helps it to withstand desert conditions.  It prefers a warm, dry climate for a good yield of ole-gum-resin.

The mean annual rainfall in its zone of occurrence is 225 to 500mm and it is very drought resistant.  The mean annual temperature is above 27°C.  An extremely hardy tree of rocky and sandy desert fringe areas, resistant to drought and mild frost.

Availability :  
The genus has its origin in Africa and Asia and is widely distributed in the tropical regions of Africa, Madagascar, Asia, Australia, Pacific Islands, India, Bangladesh and Pakistan.  

Globally this species is reported to be distributed from Arabic Countries to India.  In India the available literature records its presence mainly in Gujarat and Rajasthan and to a small extent in Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra and Karnataka.  

Wild population: Grows wild in the arid, rocky tracts of Rajasthan, Gujarat, M. P. and Karnataka (also in Sindh and Baluchistan states of Pakistan).

Cultivated populations: Central Arid Zone Research Institute, Jodhpur, Rajasthan; Guggul Herbal Farm, Ajmir, Rajasthan

 
Status : 
The species is over-exploited and threatened.  It  is included in the Red data book (IUCN). Owing to the enormous demand for the gum-resin, the plant is subjected to crude and destructive tapping procedures. There is an important need to conserve the germplasm and ensure a sustained supply of the gum-resin. 

Initially the Red List Status of this species, in South India, was assessed as " Vulnerable (Regionally)" based on old literature records of its presence in Karnataka state.  However, based on recent studies this has been changed to N.E. ie., " Not Evaluated" for Southern India on account of absence of any wild population in this region.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

It is a small tree or shrub with spinescent branches. The tree is with papery bark and grayish soft wood. Plants are predominantly female, both in natural habitats and in cultivated plantations. Green stem, gland-dotted with silvery and thin bark that peels off in flakes. Branches are crooked and very frequently end in spines. Leaves alternate, impari-pinnate, leaflets opposite. Flowers are either solitary or borne in fascicles of 3-5 and are red to brownish red. Fruit is a typical non-dehiscent drupe, deep red to purple at ripening.

The flowers are mostly unisexual. The male flowers bear stamens arranged in two whorls of four each and a centrally located minute pistillode. Pollen grains are isopolar, trizonocolporate with tectate exine showing reticulate ornamentation and contain lipids as the reserve material. Fresh pollen grains show 80% viability and a small proportion of pollen retain viability even after 8 days of storage under laboratory conditions.

The female flowers bear a superior ovary raised on a nectary disc with staminodes arranged in two whorls of four. The stigma is bilobed, papillate and wet. The style is solid with two separate strands of transmitting tissue. There is a marked decrease in the area occupied by the transmitting tissue. Unlike in other species, the cells of the transmitting tissue do not form longitudinal files of elongated cells. Also, unlike in other species the intercellular matrix of the transmitting tissue does not respond to stains specific for proteins.

The bisexual flowers, observed in only one  out of 1200 plants screened have fertile stamens as well as fully developed pistil.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Breeding System :
Reproductive biology has been worked out on natural populations as well as cultivated plantations. Sketchy embryological data are available. Most of the plants both in nature and in plantations are females. Out of the four populations studied, two male and one andro-monoecious plants were located in only one population ; the rest were all female plants. Female plants set seeds irrespective of the presence or absence of male plants in the population. Detailed studies have shown that this species is an obligate, non-psudogamous apomict with nucellar  polyembryony and  autonomous endosperm formation.

Although thrips visit flowers for pollen, there was no pollination of female flowers even in the population which contained male and andromonoecious plants. Hand pollinated pistils showed germination of a limited number of pollen grains on the stigma. However, pollen tubes ceased growth beyond the region of the stigma and upper region of the style; pollen tubes never entered the ovary.

In the ovules, although Polygonum type of embryo sac differentiates, the egg apparatus and antipodal cells degenerate; the two polar nuclei remain intact, fuse and give rise to the endosperm without fertilization.  Several embryos (2-11) differentiate from the micropylar part of the nucellus and  get embedded in the endosperm tissue. Pollination stimulus in not required for the initiation and development of adventive embryos. During seed development the nucellus and endosperm tissues are consumed and the embryos fill up the entire seed. The cotyledons in mature seeds are massive and contorted.  Mature seeds generally contain twin embryos.


Phenology :
Flowering has two peaks - April-May and October-November.

Flowering continue on some older female plants throughout the year.

Fruit mature by the end of June-July and January-February. 

Pollinators:
There is no natural pollination. Although thrips have been observed in flowers, screening of a large number of  stigmas even in the population containing male plants did not show any pollen.

Seed Biology :
Seeds are enclosed in indehiscent drupes, oval in shape, smooth light to dark brown, each having multiple embryos.


Seedling Germination :

The plant can be propagated by seeds and vegetatively through stem-cuttings.  Air-layering has also been successful in this plant.  In literature, the protocol for meristem culture is also available.

However, propagation from seed is not common because the seed germination is slow and is also very poor(5%) due to the presence of the hard seed coat.  To achieve good germination, seeds are mechanically scarified with sandpaper and are kept under running water for 24hrs.  The seedlings may be raised in polythene bags during the kharif season and after hardening, may be planted in the main field.

About 15-20cm long and 10mm thick, semi-hardwood cuttings are taken and the cut end is treated with growth regulator solutions and planted in a well prepared and manured nursery beds.  The cuttings sprout in 10-15days, they grow well and are ready for planting in main field after 10-12 months, during next rainy season.  The percentage rooting in stem-cuttings is around 80-94%.

Air layering is successful in all seasons except winter and gives 100 percent success.  Twigs of at least 1.75cm diameter should be used.  The grafts are ready for planting after one year.  Watering and weeding should be done to the nursery beds for a year before transplanting is taken up.

To produce more gum and to save the species from total loss extinction, various propagation techniques were studied. Mechanical scarification of the stones and keeping seeds under running water for 24 hrs for good seed germination.

Cultivation:
Direct planting of cuttings entails risk of damage by termites.  Hence, 100gm of de-oiled neem cake powder per pit can be added to keep out termites and seedlings/cuttings/grafts may be planted in the field at a spacing of 3m x 3m.  Sprouted cuttings are planted out after a year.  Cuttings and grafts are to be planted in small pits at a depth of 15cm.  Seedlings should be transplanted in Kharif season (May to July).  It should be ensured that the tap root remains intact.  Weeding and irrigation is necessary for 2 to 3 years after planting.  The plantation can be irrigated thrice a year during the first three years; once after the monsoon recedes and twice during summer.  Termites attack the root of young plants in drier months and root rot is frequently observed-especially in rainy seasons.  Deep incisions while tapping often kill the plants.  Protection and care should be taken against these hazards.

The rooted cuttings are planted in the pits during the rainy season.  As the plants grow, they are trained properly by cutting the side branches.  Providing light irrigation during the summer season supports good growth of the plants.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests: 

Plants are attacked by a leaf-eating caterpillar Euproctis lanata Walker, white fly-Bemisia tabaci, which sucks the sap of leaves as a result of which the leaves turn yellow and finally drop off.  The rooted cuttings also suffer from termite attack in the early stage of growth.
Diseases: 

Leaf-spot caused by Cercospora spp., and bacterial leaf-blight.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Roots, Leaves, oleo-gum resin (guggul, exudate from injured stem)

Uses

Guggul is the aromatic resinous exudation from the trunks of several sps of Commiphora.  These plants contain numerous schizogenous ducts in the bark and these may form large interconnecting cavities from which the gum-resin flows freely on wounding the bark.  The resinous fluid is yellowish white at first; it soon hardens to a reddish brown mass.  The resin is collected at an interval of 10-15days.  Weather conditions also influence the success of obtaining gum.

  • Myrrh was esteemed in olden times for its use in incense and perfumes, but guggul acts as a substitute for it.  
  • Resin has aromatic taste and balsamic odour and is used in preparation of incense and perfumes and for embalming.
  • Oleo-gum resin has multiple uses in traditional medicines, curing arthritis, obesity and related disorders.
  • Guggul is a substitute for myrrh, a pharmacopeial drug. Locally, guggul is used to cure skin diseases, anaemia, oedema besides being used as a carminative, antispasmodic, diaphoretic and aphrodisiac.
  • Gum resin has astringent, antiseptic and antisuppurative properties and is used in the treatment of hemorrhoids. It is excreted by the mucous membranes of the body, in the course of which it stimulates and disinfects their secretions. It also contains guggulsterones Z and E, guggulsterols I - V, two diterperoids-a terpene hydrocarbon named cembrane A. It is used against obesity. Guggulipid is hypocholesteremic and assists in burning excess body fat.

     

Harvesting Practices
Gum-resin guggul is obtained by making oblique incisions 7-10cm. apart into the bark of the trunk with a pointed knife. These plants contain numerous schizogenous ducts in the bark and these may form large interconnecting cavities from which the gum-resin flows freely on wounding the bark.  An yellow milky juice oozes out from the incisions and darkens on thickening. The resin is then scrapped off the wound with the knife.

Application of ethephon  has been reported to enhance guggul production 22 times.


Produce and Products
guggul

In recent years anti-arthritic, hypo-cholesterolaemic and hypo-lipidaemic properties of guggul have been established. A commercial product, guglip has been marketed in India by CIPLA since 1988.

Chemistry
Guggul or the gum resin from the bark contains the octanordammarane terpenes manusumbionic acid and manusumbinone. It also contains guggulsterones Z and E, guggulsterols I - V, two diterperoids-a terpene hydrocarbon named cembrane.

BDELLIUM. Chiefly two varieties of this product are known in commerce, Indian and African. Indian bdellium, or East Indian bdellium, is believed to be the product of Balsamodendron Mukul, Hooker (Balsamea Mukul, Engler), of India, and possibly Arabia forms large, rounded, dusty fragments; has a flattish shell-like fracture; in thin section translucent, but in mass dark, even deep-brown, and possesses the odor and taste of myrrh in a lesser degree. Nitric acid does not impart a purplish color to the tincture of this or the next variety.

Processing Techniques
The resin can be separated from the gum either by hot expression or solvent extraction at 120-130 degree C.  The yield by former process is about 10% less than that obtained by solvent exgtraction(61%).  The purified resin is transparent in thin films, but translucent or even opaque in bulk.  It is completely soluble in  most of the organic solvents and in castor oil, drying oils and turpentine and mixes well with vegetable waxes, stearic and resin.

Other Uses
  1.  Roots- are astringent, sweet, cooling, aphrodisiac and diuretic    and are useful in diabetes, strangury, fever and vitiated conditions of vata and pitta.
  2. Leaves-are anodyne and are useful in rheumatalgia.
  3. Bdellium has been used as an adulterant of Myrrh, but in reality it has its own unique fragrance. It is rich, has a hint of something like pepper, and has background of something that is pleasant, forceful and fiery.
  4. Bdellium is used in perfumes and is highly revered in Ayurvedic medicine, where it is said to lower cholesterol

 Miscellaneous Information

Some adverse side-effects reported on taking guggul are mild diarrhea and nausea. It may possibly raise bilirubin levels, cause hemolysis of blood, hepatitis, and obstruction of the biliary tract. But these side effects need to be confirmed.

References of the properties and uses of guggul are listed in various medical lexicons written between 12th and 14th centuries A.D.
