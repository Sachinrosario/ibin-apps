Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Cumin is tropical, aromatic, small, bushy annual herb with semi-erect to erect growth habit, 15-60 cm in height. Cumin is the dried ripe seed (fruit) of annual herb Cuminum cyminum L.

Climate and soil
Cumin is mainly a tropical plant and thrives well in tropical and subtropical climate. It requires a season of 3-4 months of rather cool and dry weather with full sunlight as as can be found in semi-arid areas with a moderate winter or at higher altitudes (up to 2200 m). temperature range is 9-24°C and frost, excessive heat or moisture or heavy rains during the growing period is not tolerated (1). In India it is usually cultivated as a winter crop (rabi crop) in irrigated condition in areas where atmosphere humidity is low at the time of flowering and seed formation (February- March). High humidity and cloudy weather during flowering and fruiting stage induces diseases like powdery mildew and blight as well as attack of aphids which in turn reduce yield and quality of produce. All types of soil that are well drained and having good water holding capacity and neutral status; sandy loam to loamy soil, medium to heavy textured soils of medium fertility; soil pH acidic to alkaline are suitable. Crop is tolerant to variability in soil temperature and pH.


Availability :

Egypt but now cultivated in many parts of the world in the tropical and subtropical regions. The main producing countries are India, Turkey, Iran, Lebanon, Egypt, Spain, Libya, Iran, Pakistan, Syria, Mexico, Chile, China, Cyprus, Indonesia, Morocco and Japan. In south east Asia found only in mountainous regions (eg. Indonesia) (1). Indian cumin grows abundantly in the mild, equable climate of Gujarat, Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh where rich, well-drained, sandy, loamy soil and the sunny, conducive environment are available (2).
Conservation/ Threat Status : Cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

It is a small, height of about 15-60 cm, slender, glabrous, herbaceous bushy annual plant with a much branched stem and long finely dissected narrow, deep green leaves. All green parts glabrous but usually covered with a bloom. Stem subterete, up to 3 mm in diameter, finely sulcate, branching at all heights. Leaves alternate, compound, green; petiole subterete, 2-25 mm long, finely sulcate, sheathing at base with scarious margins; upper leaves usually only with sheathing part; blade consisting of 3 slender, filiform leaflets, each leaflet often forked 2-3 times into filiform lobes up to 7 cm long (1). Plant comes to bearing in 60-90 days from sowing. Elongated greyish (yellowish brown) bristly fruit (schizocarp) are about 6 mm long, tapering towards both ends, laterally compressed with a short stalk. Mericarp is concave on one side with longitudinal ridges and numerous hairs. Cumin is an aromatic spice with stimulating properties. It has a characteristic strong flavour and slightly bitter in taste. Seeds have typical pleasant aroma and contains 2 to 5% volatile oil of which 40 to 65% is cuminaldehyde (cuminic aldehyde) (1).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:
Inflorescence a compound umbel, up to 3.5 cm in diameter; peduncle subterete, up to 7 cm long, finely sulcate; bracts often as many as primary rays, sheathing at base, linear, often up to 3-forked into 2-35 mm long; primary rays 2-10 per umbel, terete, unequal in length, up to 18 mm long, finely sulcate; bracteoles 3-5 per umbellet, linear, up to 25 mm long, sheathing at base, sometimes 2-3-forked; secondary rays 3-8 per umbellet, up to 6 mm long; flowers bisexual, regular, protoandrous; sepals 5, narrowly triangular, unequal in length, up to 2.5 mm long; petals 5 all equal, oblong, up to 1.5 mm x 1 mm, whitish at base, pinkish to reddish at top, apex strongly inflexed and narrow; stamens 5 with filiform filaments 1.5 mm long; pistil with ribbed ovary, 2 styles on a conical, persistent stylopodium, stigma semiglobose. Plants come to bearing in 60-90 days (1).

Fruit
Fruit greyish, about 8 cm long, narrow, tapering at both ends compressed laterally with ridges and papillose hairs. An ovoid-oblongoid, erect or slightly curved greenish (yellow brown) schizocarp, 3.5-6.5 mm x 1-2 mm x 0.8-1.5 mm, crowned by persistent, sharp stylopodia and sepal bases, yellow brown; primary ribs 8, secondary ribs prominent and alternating with and wider than primary ones, whitish setose but bristles breaking off easily; at slight pressure the fruit splits into 2 mericarps; mericarp strongly concave ventrally, convex dorsally, usually bearing one oil duct (vitta) below each secondary rib and two vittae on the commissural ventral side (1).

Breeding system and varietal improvement:
Although flowers are protoandrous, self-pollination is the rule; cross-pollination is also present (sometimes 70%) (1).

Phenology: Flowering starts about 1.5-2.5 months after sowing.

Seed Biology
Seed with testa adnate to the fruit wall, endosperm grey, fatty.

CULTIVATION ASPECT:
Seed germination
Epigeal. Heavy rains during sowing period will adversely affect germination.

Seedling development
Cumin seeds take 2-3 weeks to germinate.

Released varieties
S-404, MC-43, Guj. cumin 1, Guj. cumin 2, Guj. cumin 3, RZ-19 and RZ-209.

Chromosome number
2n=14

Selection of planting material
Seeds are the planting material.

Planting
Field preparation: Choose a field in which cumin was not cultivated in the past 3 years. Cumin requires thorough land preparation, as the seeds are very small. So two to three ploughing and levelling to have very fine tilth. Apply FYM 10-15 t/ha at the time of last ploughing. Incorporate 25 kg Endosulfan/4% Quinalphos (1.5%) or methyl parathion powder (2%)/ha to control termites and other insects. Beds of convenient size are then to be made (4 x 3 m).

Sowing time, seed rate and spacing: Depending upon the agro-climatic condition, cumin is sown from second fortnight of November to first week of December; sowing around 15th November is ideal. Cumin is either broadcasted or sown in lines (dibbling) at 30 cm apart at a seed rate of 10- 12 kg/ha. When broadcasted the field is divided into beds of convenient length and seeds are uniformly broadcasted and covered with soil (20 kg seeds/ha for broadcasting - Slow and delayed germination are constraints in cumin cultivation). Second method is line sowing at 25 to 30 cm row spacing, and this is better than broadcasting for inter-culture operation that can reduce cost of cultivation. In line sowing shallow furrows at a distance of 22.5 to 25 cm with wooden or iron hooter are made in the beds. Depth of sowing should be 1.5 cm to 2.0 cm. Rabi crop is sown directly, Kharif crop is either sown directly or transplanted.

Before sowing, soaking seeds in water for 24 hrs is recommended for better germination. Pretreatment with fungicides like Captan/Agrosan/Difolitan/Bavistin/Thiram @ 3 g/1 kg seed is also recommended.

Management after planting
Irrigation: Light irrigation immediately after sowing is important. Generally the crop should be irrigated 3-5 times depending upon weather and soil conditions. First irrigation just after sowing and second 7-8 days of sowing when radicle start peeping out and 15 DAS for completion of germination are essential. Depending upon the soil type and climatic conditions subsequent irrigation may be given at an interval of 15-25 days. Irrigation at the time of flowering and seed formation is essential. Irrigation should be avoided during the period when the crop is maturing as this may affect the seed quality and variety.

Weeding: The crop suffers from severe weed problem during the growth period because of slow growth and short stature. Minimum two weeding and hoeing are necessary for proper aeration and control of weeds. Adopt inter cultivation operations in line sown cumin next day after irrigating the crop. The first weeding and hoeing should be done about 30-40 days after sowing to facilitate proper growth of crops. At the same time plants are also to be thinned out to a spacing of 12-15 cm in broadcasted crop and 5 cm in line sown crop. Another one or two weedings and hoeing are done if weeds appear again and crust formation takes place. Weed control can also be achieved by use of pre-emergent herbicides; Tebutyryn (0.5 kg a.i./ha) or Oxidiazone (0.5 kg/ha) or Pendimethalin (1.0 kg/ha).

Manures and fertilizers: For sustained production 15-20 tons of organic manure as basal and NPK@ 25:20:20 kg/ha is recommended. Studies at Gujarat has shown that FYM at 15-20 t/ha followed by 20 kg P2O5 while sowing and 30 kg N as top dressing in two equal splits during 30 and 60 days after sowing is required for proper growth and good yield.

Fertilizer and manure recommendation for cumin for major states
Gujarat :30kg N and 15kg P/ha. Half N and full P+10 t compost/ FYM as basal dose and the balance N given monthly after sowing as top dressing. 25:20:20 kg/ha is also recommended.

Rajasthan: 30 kg N and 20 kg P/ha. The N is given in two splits at 30 and 60 DAS and full P as basal +10 t compost/FYM at sowing

Tamil Nadu: 70:60:40kg NPK/ha 30kg N and P and K are applied as basal and the remaining N in two equal splits at 30 and 60 DAS


MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
Aphids: Myzus persicae, Acyrthosiphon pisum and Aphis craccivora attack the crop. They suck the sap of plant from tender parts and flowers. Due to the continuous desapping of flowers, seed formation gets affected.

Control measures: Endosulfan has been considered relatively safer which can be sprayed @ 0.07%. Careful spraying 400-500 Lits. of solution of 0.03% Phosphamidon (100 EC) or Dimethoate 30 EC or Metasystox 25 EC per hectare. Repeat spray after 10 days if required. Always safe not to use any insecticides unless it is absolutely essential.


Diseases:

Fusarial Wilt: Caused by Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cumini. Disease is a typical vascular wilt. Disease appears in patches and cumin plants of all ages are liable to be attacked, but generally appear when the crop is about one month old. At this stage damage appear in severe form compared to disease appearance at seed formation. Loss due to this disease is estimated at 5-20%.

Symptoms: Leaves drop and turn yellow. In older plants colour of the leaves change to yellow, beginning from oldest leaves followed by wilting of the entire plant. When attacked at flowering stage no seed is formed, seed if formed is thin and tiny and shrivelled. Diseased plants can easily uprooted and the roots show dark brown markings.

Control measures: pathogen is soil borne, infection occurs in roots and it is very difficult to control this disease. Use healthy and disease free seeds. Seed treatment with a mixture of Carbendazim + Captan (1:1) @ 4 g/kg of seed is essential. Soil solarization and ploughing during summer months keeps infection low. Timely hoeing and weeding of field, keeps proper aeration and reduces severity of disease. Organic amendments with neem cake @ 10 q/ha is recommended. A three year crop rotation sequence to accommodate cumin once in three year i.e. clusterbean-cumin-clusterbean-wheat-clusterbean-mustard crop sequence. Integrated management consists of T. harzianum application, seed treatment @ 4 g/kg of seed as well as soil application along with fungicide spraying Topsin (0.07%) or Indofil (0.3%).

Blight: Caused by Alternaria burnsii is the second most serious disease. Disease appears at flowering stage and spread rapidly in cloudy weather. Pathogen is seed borne, infection takes place in early stages of plant growth. Temperature range of 23-28°C promotes the development of blight. About 70% of crop loss has been reported. It is severe in Gujarat, Rajasthan and West Bengal.

Symptoms: Infected plants show dark brown lesions on leaves and stems, and tops bend down. Minute necrotic areas on all above ground plant parts which turn purple and later brown to back and get blighted. Severely affected plants either bear no seeds or seeds become shrivelled, dark coloured, light and sterile.

Control measures: As a prophylactic measure seed dressing with Carbendazim (0.1%) or Captan (0.1%) and foliar spray of 400-500 Lits. of solution of any one fungicides viz. Indofil M-45 (0.25%), Bordeaux mixture 1%, Blitox 50 or Copper oxychloride (0.8 to 1.0 kg/ha) at the time of flowering followed at fortnightly intervals.

Powdery mildew: Causal organism Erysiphe polygoni. Disease appears at the time of flowering and aggravate at 26.7 to 35°C. Warm humid climate is favourable for spread of this disease. Varieties MC-43, Gujarat cumin 1 & 2 are tolerant.

Symptoms: Small white or greyish specks on lower leaves that enlarge and cover lower surface with fungal growth and gradually spreads to whole plant. In severe infection leaves show ashy-white appearance. Infection readily proceeds to stem, flowers and fruits.

Control measures: Dusting of sulphur @ 25 kg/ha, spraying 400-500 Lits./ha. of wettable sulphur 85 WP @ 2.5kg/ha or Dinocap (0.25%) or Karathane LC or Calaxin (0.05 to 0.1%) and repeating after 15-20 days commencing from 45 days after germination.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Cumin is an aromatic spice having bitter taste and has an intensely strong flavour, much similar to caraway. Indian cumin finds worldwide use in foods, beverages, liquors, medicines, toiletries and in papads, pickles, for seasoning, salad varieties, side dishes, masala mixes, breads, snacks, meat varieties and fish dishes. It is either dry roasted or roasted in ghee or oil. It is pungent and has a strong flavour. In most savoury preparations, cumin is used with pepper. It is also used for Kichdi, Pongal and Kadi varieties. Jeera rice is a popular dish (4). The spice is of particular value in the blending of Indian curry powder and widely used as a condiment, form an essential ingredient in all spice mixes and curry powders (2). Cumin fruits were highly prized in ancient civilizations as one of the best appetizers of all culinary spices. Even small quantity imparts flavour. Main constituent is cuminaldehyde present in the essential oil. Seed is used extensively in culinary preparation for flavouring in many dishes, bread, confectionary curries, chutneys, sauerkraut, cold/soft drinks like Jaljeera, perfumery etc. Its major use is in blending with curry powder, soups, stew, vegetables, sausages cheeses, pickles, and meats. Ground fruits are essential ingredient of curry and chilli powder, especially in India, Egypt and Turkey. Aromatic oil is used for flavouring curries, liquors, cordials and has use in perfumery industry. Most of the produce in India is consumed domestically (1).

Parts Used:

Seed (Dried ripe fruit).

Harvesting Practices:
Fruits are harvest about one month after flowering, when they wither and fruits turn yellow. Crop matures in 130-150 days after sowing. Harvesting is usually done in the morning hours to avoid shedding losses by uprooting followed by drying in sun. Seeds are separated and cleaned by mechanical winnowing. Threshing is done with sticks or by trampling with cattle on a threshing floor. In large-scale production it is mechanized. Average yield of cumin is 350-800 kg/ha (1).

Post Harvest Handling
Cleaned and dried seeds are filled in gunny bags and stored in polythene lined gunny bags at 9% moisture level in humidity free godowns.

Produce and Products:
Dried cumin, powdered cumin and Cumin essential oil - Obtained by steam distillation although hydro-diffusion gives largest amounts. Oil is colourless at first, turning bright yellow later (1).

Medicinal uses
In indigenous medicines cumin seeds have long been considered as a stimulant and carminative. In Ayurvedic medicines used in urinary and gastric troubles. Fruits used as a stimulant, antispasmodic, carminative, stomachic, astringent and have antimicrobial properties. Cumin is widely used in traditional medicine to treat flatulence, digestive disorders, diarrhoea and in the treatment of wounds. Essential oil is used as stimulant, antispasmodic, carminative, aphrodisiac, emmenagogue and has a light anaesthetizing action. Also used as fungicide, insecticide and a veterinary medicine. In Ethiopia, pounded leaves are applied to the skin to treat skin disorders (1). Fruits are acrid, sweet, cooling, anaphrodisiac, astringent, digestive, carminative, anthelmintic, anti-inflammatory, anodyne, constipating, stomachic, stimulant, depurative, revulsive, diuretic, galactagogue, febrifuge, uterine and nervine stimulant, ophthalmic and tonic. Useful in vitiated conditions of vata and kapha, dyspepsia, colic, helminthiasis, inflammations, flatulence, anorexia, vomiting, haemorrhoids, strangury, renal and vesical calculi, leucorrhoea, chronic diarrhoea, skin diseases, leprosy, leucoderma, agalactia, fever, ophthalmopathy, gonorrhoea, hiccough, asthma, cough, bronchitis, ulcers and general debility (3). This decoction is also a home-made recipe for menstrual pains (4). Traditionally included in a diet of pregnant women as an effective antidote to morning sickness. Mothers use it as a carminative to make gripe water for their infants. Used to increase the secretion of mother's milk. Seeds taken in for snakebite. Stimulant, beneficial in hoarseness of voice, paste externally applied to allay pain and irritation due to worms in the abdomen. Oil useful in eczema (5).

Chemistry:
Most important component of the volatile oil is cuminaldehyde (15-40 %), about 10% fixed oil together with tannins, oleoresin, mucilage, gum, protein compounds and maltase. Other components include menthadienal (30 %), variable quantities of cineole, p-cymene, limonene, about 18-29 % ?-terpinene, a-pinene (1.4 %) and b-pinene (2.0 %). The characteristic flavour of cumin is due to dihydrocuminaldehyde and monoterpenes. Odour and taste of cumin is described as strong, warm, pungent and persistent. Fatty oil present in cumin is 10.0 %. Indian cumin oil contains 30.0% ?-terpinene, 25.5% cuminaldehyde, 16.0% para-cymene, 9.1% p-mentha-1,3-dien-7-al, 8.9% ß-pinene, 2.7% p-mentha-1,4-dien-7-al, 0.6% a-pinene, 0.6% p-menth-3-en-7-al, 0.3% sabinene, 0.3% myrcene, 0.3% a-phellandrene, 0.3% limonene, 0.3% ß-phellandrene, 0.2% (Z)-ß-farnesene, 0.2% cumin alcohol, 0.1% a-terpinene, 0.1% terpinene-4-ol, 0.1% isocaryophyllene, 0.1% p-isopropylphenol, 0.1% a-terpineol, 0.1% trans-a-bergamotene, 0.1% trpinolene, 0.1% bornyl acetate, 0.1% cis-sabinene hydrate, trace linalool, trace thymol, trace camphene, trace d-3-carene, trace trans-sabinene hydrate (Source: Baser et al., 1992) (6).

Per 100 g edible portion seeds contain approx.: water - 7 g, protein - 18 g, fat - 4 g, carbohydrates - 29 g, fibre - 17 g, ash - 6 g, ascorbic acid - 3 mg, foreign matter (sand, etc) - 17 g, Ca - 605 mg, Fe - 175 mg, P - 570 mg, niacin - 8 mg. Maximum permitted level in food is 0.025%. Accorded the regulatory status 'generally regarded as safe' (GRAS) by US (GRAS 2340/2343) (1).

Quality: Depends primarily on its cleanliness, colour, lustre, grain size, aroma, flavour, volatile oil range is 23 to 4.8%. Proper care must be given in harvest operations like threshing, winnowing, cleaning, grading, storage. Colour and volatile oil content of grain is important in marketing. Aromatic flavour in the cumin seed is due to the presence of volatile oil, the percentage of which varies with variety and from 2.0-4.0 %. The oil has an unpleasant characteristic odour and is spicy, somewhat bitter in taste principally due to the aldehyde present. Composition of Indian cumin seed is as follows.

Moisture - 11.9%, Protein - 18.7%, Ether extract - 15.0%, Carbohydrates - 36%, Fibre - 9.1%, Mineral matter - 5.8%, Calcium - 1.08%, Phosphorous - 0.49%, Iron - 31.0 mg/100 g, Carotene - 8701 IU/100 g, Vitamin C - 3 mg/100 g - Volatile oil - 2.5%. Adoption of improved package of practices is necessary to obtain high yield better quality, and high returns. Following major points should be taken care of:
1. Use of a high yielding, elite cultivar/or a released variety.
2. Adopt good agronomic management practices in sowing, irrigation and fertilizer application.
3. Adopt timely phytosanitary measures /control measures to check diseases and pests.
4. Employ clean post harvest on farm processing methods including -use of clean concrete drying floors clean polythene lined gunny bags for packaging etc.

Economic potential to the country

India is the largest producer of cumin, with an average production of about 127980 tons. The major producing states are Rajasthan and Gujarat and to a limited scale in Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Madhya Pradesh, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu. The North Gujarat region accounts for more than 85% of the area and production of cumin in the state of Gujarat, which contribute to the major share of cumin production.

Area : 285560 ha (2000-01); Rajasthan (147300 ha), Gujarat (134700 ha)
Production : 127980 t (2000-01); Rajasthan (33660 t), Gujarat (57700 t)
Productivity : 448 kg/ha (2000-01); Rajasthan (230 kg/ha), Gujarat (430 kg/ha)
Field productivity (range) : 0-800 kg/ha.
Potential : 1600 kg/ha.

 

Export potential:
Export value from India is 4250 t valued Rs 2944.75 lakhs (1999-00). India exports cumin to USA, Canada, Nepal, Singapore, Japan, UK, Middle East, Malaysia and Sri Lanka in a variety of forms including seeds, powder and volatile oil. The average annual export is around 11000 tons worth 5973 lakhs rupees. Indian cumin is exported in its natural as well as powdered form, besides as essential oil. Exports are mostly to USA, Singapore, Japan, UK and North Africa (2).

Major importing countries: UK, USA, Canada, Singapore, Japan, Malaysia, Saudi Arabia. Bangladesh, Japan, Malaya, Nepal, Pakistan, Singapore, South Africa, UAE, UK, USA and cumin powder to Canada, UK, USA etc.
