 Other Biological Details
HABIT & HABITAT
A perennial prickly creeper or climber growing to 6m by 3m, common  throughout  India and mostly common in North-west Himalayas ascending to an altitude of 3,750m.

The plant prefers light (sandy), medium (loamy) and heavy (clay) soils. The plant prefers acid, neutral and basic (alkaline) soils. It can grow in semi-shade (light woodland). It requires dry or moist soil.


Availability :  
It is distributed throughout North-eastern Asia from Dahuria to Java and Japan and is found also in Tropical Africa.  It is found on the Himalaya appearing near the Chenab and extending eastward to Sikkim and Bhutan to the Khasia and Naga hills, Burma, South India and Ceylon.  

Status : 

Not much under cultivation, but is largely collected as a wild dye stuff.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
It is a rugose scandent vine or herb.Rootstock perennial, roots long cylindric, flexuose with thin, red bark; stem 4-angled; leaves variable in shape, cordate to ovate-lanceolate, 2-8 in a whorl, normally 4, sometimes 1 pair larger; flowers small, white or greenish or in shades of red and yellow, sweet-scented in many in terminal panicles of cymes; fruit globose or slightly 2-lobed, dark purplish or black, fleshy with 2 small seeds.

Seed Biology :

Seedling Germination :
Seed - best sown as soon as it is ripe in a cold frame. Stored seed can be very slow to germinate. Prick out the seedlings when they are large enough to handle and grow them on in light shade in the greenhouse for the first year. Plant them out into their permanent positions in early summer.

Division in spring or at any time in the growing season if the divisions are kept well watered until established. Larger divisions can be planted out direct into their permanent positions. It is found best to pot up the smaller divisions and grow them on in a lightly shaded position in a cold frame, planting them out once they are well established in the summer.

Vegetative propagation is through tillers.  Tillers that arise from the mature plant can be separated and planted individually, between spring and autumn.

They can also be developed from stumps.


Cultivation:
Prefers a loose moist leafy soil in some shade. Tolerates dry soils but quickly becomes scorched when growing in full sun.

Four leaved seedlings are transplanted to polythene bags and when they produce 3 whorls of leaves, they are planted in the field.  The planting is done in 30cm cube pits, spaced at 2.5 x 2.5m.  Support plants need to be provided for this climber.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Roots, leaves, stem

Uses
Dye.

  • Manjit Root or East Indian Madder, obtained for the most part from this species, is much employed by the Natives of India in dyeing coarse cotton fabrics or the thread which is woven into such fabrics, various shades of scarlet, coffee brown or mauve.  The  colour obtained from manjit is brighter though more fleeting than that of European Madder.  
  • The roots yield a substance becoming reddish brown with alum. The dye from roots is used to dye coarse cotton fabrics, blankets and carpets.  Roots are also used for colouring medicinal oils.
  • A red dye obtained from the stems and the root is inferior to that of R. tinctoria, the madder plant.  
  • Dye is used for improving the colour of the leather.
  • In Muhammadan works on medicin, Manjit is described as having deobstruent properties, and is prescribed for use in paralysis, jaundice, obstructions in the urinary passages and in amenorrhoea.  
  • The root or rhizome contains around 2% tannins.


Harvesting Practices
The roots are harvested in the autumn from plants that are at least 3 years old. They are peeled and then dried.

Chemistry
Purpurin, Munjistin, Xanthopurpurin or Purpuroxanthin and Pseudopurpurin

Colouring matters of Manjit were not identical with those of the common madder since it contained no alizarin, and its colouring matter is a mixture of purpurin and an orange dye which is called Munjistin and is thus nearly related to the composition to purpurin and to alizarin.

The inferiority of manjit as a dye stuff results from its containing the comparatively feeble colouring matters purpurin and munjistin, of the lastter of which only a small part is available, while its presence in large quanitity appears to be positively injurious; so much so indeed that manjit, garancin, freed by boiling water from the greater part of the munjistin which it contains, yields much richer shades with alumina mordants than before.

Other Uses
  • The roots are alterative, anodyne, antiphlogistic, antitussive, astringent, diuretic, emmenagogue, expectorant, styptic, tonic and vulnerary. They have an antibacterial action, inhibiting the growth of Staphylococcus aureus, S. epidermidis, Pneumococci etc. They are used to lower the blood pressure. The roots are used internally in the treatment of abnormal uterine bleeding, internal and external haemorrhage, bronchitis, rheumatism, stones in the kidney, bladder and gall, dysentery etc.  Used in rheumatism and as an ingredient of several ayurvedic preparations.  The root is used in dropsy, paralysis & jaundice. It is used for its red colour pigment. Also used in various skin tonics. It is an important ingredient of various ayurvedic tonics for blood purification. It is used for snake bite & scorpion sting. 
  • Paste of the roots – applied to ulcers, inflammations and skin troubles. 
  • A decoction of leaves and stem is used as a vermifuge.
  • Ethanolic extract of the aerial parts of the plant show hypoglycaemic activity in albino rats. 
  • Leaves - cooked. Used as a side dish with rice. It is much esteemed as a lab-lab by the Javanese.
  • Fruit - eaten raw. The fruit is about 8mm in diameter.
  • The stems are used in Tibetan medicine, where they are considered to have a bitter taste and a cooling potency. Febrifuge, they are used in the treatment of blood disorders and spreading fever of kidneys and intestines.

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country
Earlier considerable amount of import trade in manjit and madder existed, but the substitution of artificially-prepared alizarine and other aniline dyes has caused it to decline greatly.  In 1885-86, Rs. 202,048 worth of madder and manjit was imported into India, while in the year 1889-90 the value of the total imports of alizarine and aniline dye has, on the other hand, almost quadrupled itself, during that period.  The principal exporting country is Persia, and Bombay is the only importing town.  The exports of Manjit and Madder are comparitively small.  In 1889-90, they amounted the value to Rs. 6,987 in all. Of which  most of it was exported from Bombay, Sind and Madras.

Miscellaneous Information

This plant was used in Indian medicine as a coloring agent and Chakradatta recommended it as an application to the brown spots of pityriasis vesicolor. Ainslie observed that the hakims were in the habit of prescribing an infusion of this plant's root as a deobstruent drink in cases of scanty lochial discharge after lying-in.
