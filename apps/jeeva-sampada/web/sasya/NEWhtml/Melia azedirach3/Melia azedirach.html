HABIT & HABITAT

A moderate-sized deciduous tree growing 9m by 9m at a fast rate, found growing wild in the sub-Himalayan tract up to 1800m. Often cultivated.
Native originally to northern India, China and the Himalayas, Chinaberry is planted widely in warm climates as an ornamental and shade tree. For more than 200 years it has been popular in the southern US where it is grown for it attractive flowers and for a quick shade tree. 

A tree of the subtropical climatic zone, the natural habitat of M. azedarach is seasonal forest, including bamboo thickets, Tamarindus woodland. It is highly adaptable and tolerates a wide range of conditions; for example, the most frost-tolerant cultivars can be planted outdoors in sheltered areas in the British Isles.

The plant prefers light (sandy), medium (loamy) and heavy (clay) soils and requires well-drained soil. The plant prefers acid, neutral and basic (alkaline) soils. It cannot grow in the shade. It requires dry or moist soil and can tolerate drought.


Availability :  
Persian lilac is said to be a native of China.  The trees grows wild in Baluchistan, Persia, China, Myanmar and Sub-Himalayan tracts.  As the name indicates, the original home of the tree is possibly Iran.  The plant is introduced into India during the Muslim invasions of medieval periods.  The plants are well distributed in Siwalik tracts and North West India.  In Punjab, the tree is common around the irrigation wells, bore wells and tanks.  The lilac is well spread in India during the dynasty of Sultans.

This tree, although a native of several Asiatic countries, is cultivated in the warm climates of Europe and America; it does not grow to any extent north of Virginia, and flowers early during the spring. Its name of Bead tree was derived from the use to which its hard nuts are put in Roman Catholic countries, viz.: for making rosaries.

In Southern France and Spain it is found growing in avenues. 

This tree, well known as Persian lilac, is native to India but is now grown in all the warmer parts of the world; in many of these places it is naturalized. It is widely planted in Nigeria, for example.

Native: Bangladesh, India, Indonesia, Laos, Malaysia, Myanmar, Nepal, Pakistan, Papua New Guinea, Sri Lanka, Thailand, Vietnam

Status : 

It is well suited for afforestation.  It is recommended for ravines and mined sites.  Also planted as an ornamental shade tree.

It is a native of west Asia and is now naturalized throughout the warm countries. In India, it is often cultivated in the plains as an ornamental avenue tree and in waste lands.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES


This elegant tree, attains the height of 30 or 40 feet, with a trunk about 11 feet in diameter, and divaricate branches. The bark is rough. The leaves are alternate and unequally bipinnate; the leaflets opposite, ovate, acute, serrated, sometimes incised, and in pairs with an odd one. Lilac-colored flowers are borne in terminal panicles, on axillary peduncles. The corolla consists of 5 petals, patent, pale-pink inside, deep-lilac outside; the calyx is 5-parted. Stamens with tube 10-cleft at top, deep-violet; anthers yellow. Ovary 5-celled; stigma 5-lobed; style columnar. The fruit is a drupe the size of a small olive, with one 5-celled, bony nut; the cells are 1-seeded.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Breeding System :


The scented flowers are hermaphrodite (have both male and female organs).

It flowers from March to May in the northern hemisphere, although some forms flower throughout the summer and even throughout the year.


Phenology :


It is in flower in March to may and soon after fruits appear in cluster, ie., may to November.  

Pollinators:

 Seed Biology :


The fruit has usually five seeds and has a long and very hard endocarp.  Fruits are collected from the tree; they are depulped and washed.  The seeds are dried in sun for two days and dried in shade for another 2 days and dressed with 50% BHC before storing in sealed containers.  The seeds should not be kept in gunny bags.  One year of storage does not seem to impair seed viability; on the contrary it may improve germination.

Seed storage behaviour is orthodox. Viability is maintained for 1-3 years in hermetic storage at room temperature with 11-15 % mc. There are 470-2800 seeds/kg.

 

Seedling Germination :

Fruit drop is limited, and ripe fruit clings to the branches for several months even after the leaves have fallen. Propagation is by direct sowing or by planting out seedlings or stumps; 85% germination may be expected in 2 months.

Propagation could be through seeds and also through cuttings.  

Seed - sow late winter in a greenhouse. The seed is best sown as soon as it is ripe in the autumn. The seed usually germinates well. When they are large enough to handle, prick the seedlings out into individual pots and grow them on in the greenhouse for at least their first winter. Plant them out into their permanent positions in late spring or early summer, after the last expected frosts.
Under natural conditions, the plant regenerates readily from seeds during the rains. The plant grows fast in the early stages, the annual girth increment being 1.5 – 2.0 in. It coppices well up to a girth of 3 ft.

Shoot cuttings of pencil thickness of 30cm length are treated with root inducers and planted in small earthen pots or polybags filled with sand and kept in the mist-chamber for 25-30days.  Then plants are transplanted into bigger bags and maintained for 6 to 10months in order to attain good height.


Cultivation:


Requires a sunny sheltered position. Succeeds in most well-drained soils and in hot dry conditions. Likes sandy soils. Grows well in mild coastal areas.

A very ornamental tree, it is not very cold tolerant being killed by temperatures lower than about -5°c. It only succeeds outdoors in the mildest areas of Britain, seldom growing larger than a shrub. It is often cultivated in warmer regions than Britain for its many useful qualities.

The flowers are produced on the current years wood and have a delicate sweet perfume. The trees do not normally require pruning.

The seeds have a strong scent of musk and the wood is also musk-scented.

Trees are very susceptible to forest fire, though they sprout back readily from the roots. They are planted for re-afforestation in their native areas, where they are fast growing though short-lived.

Under optimal conditions, M. azedarach grows fast. It is generally deciduous, but some forms in the humid tropics (e.g. in Malaysia and Tonga) are evergreen. Does not coppice well from large stumps, but excellent coppice is obtained from trees up to a girth of 0.9 m. The tree re-sprouts after cutting and re-grows after pollarding, making it suitable for pole production.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests: 

The tree is prone to the attack of  Fomes senex Nees Mont.  Certain larvae defoliate the tree and mine the leaves.

Diseases: 

Plants in this genus are notably resistant to honey fungus.

The trees are attacked by fungi that cause brownish butt rot and brownish pocket rot.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used

Bark, Occasionally root bark, Leaves, Flowers, Fruits, Seeds, Kernel, Oil from seeds, Exudate or gum and sap.

Uses

  • Gum: The Persian Lilac yields a brownish adhesive gum similar to that of the nim and unlike that of the wood-apple, Feronia elephantum, in appearance.  It is apparently not used to any extent.
  • A cooling drink is made from the sap - it is actually a gum. This gum is tasteless, clear to dark amber and of good solubility. The sap is obtained from incisions that are made near the base of the trunk in the spring.
  • A gum that exudes from the tree is considered by some to have aphrodisiac properties.
  • Dye: Leaves of this plant are used as dye material and they impart green colour.  Leaves are also have an astringent in principle.

Chemistry
Several terpenoids, steroids, alkaloids, flavonoids, glycosides etc. have been isolated and characterised. Margosic acid, nimbin, nimbidin, nimbinin, kaempferol, quercureetin, ß-sitosterol, azadirone, paraisine, vanillic acid, nimbicetin, meliacins etc. have been isolated.

Jacobs, in 1879, obtained the bitter resinous constituent of this bark, of a yellow-white color. It dissolves readily in alcohol, chloroform, and ether, and is but partially soluble in carbon bisulphide, turpentine, benzin, and water; scarcely at all in the last three. Its other constituents are thought to be similar to those of the Azadirachta indica

Other Uses
  1. Well suited for afforestation purposes. Timber used for cigar and ammunition boxes,  sportswear, roofing material and agricultural implements, musical instrument and as  fuel wood.
  2. Roots-are acrid, bitter, astringent, mildly healing, anodyne, depurative, vulnerary, antiseptic, anthelmintic, constipating, expectorant, febrifuge, antiperiodic, urinary astringent, emmenagogue and tonic. They are useful in vitiated conditions of vata, sciatica, lumbago, cephalagia, leprosy, leucodema, skin diseases, wounds, ulcers, haemorrhoids, helminthiasis especially tapeworm, cough, asthma, amenorrhoea, dysmenorrhoea, diabetes, urethrorrhea, chronic and intermittent fever, vomiting, uteralgia after delivery, burning sensation and general debility. In excessive dose it is emetic and purgative.
  3. Bark – insect-repellent properties and effective ascariasis. Outer bark is astringent and is the seat of tannin.
  4. A kind of whisky prepared firm fruits. Oil suitable for making  soaps and hair oils.
  5. Leaves are bitter, astringent, anthelmintic, antilithic, diuretic and emmenagogue,  stomachic, expectorant, vermicidal antilithic. They are useful in hysteria, leprosy, scrofula, splenomegaly, cardiac diseases, urolithiasis, verminosis, cough, bronchitis and scabies. Also used as fodder and manure. The flowers and leaves are applied as a poultice in the treatment of neuralgia and nervous headache.  Leaves - cooked. they are used as a pot-herb, in curries, soups etc. Fruit. A sweetish flavour, it is eaten by children though some people believe it to be poisonous. The fruit is between 1 and 5cm in diameter and contains a single seed. (Both these reports, of edible leaves and fruits, should be treated with some caution. The fruit is poisonous according to one report where it says that the ripe fruit is more poisonous than the green fruit and that they have sometimes caused human fatalities). The leaves repel mosquitoes and other insects.
  6. Seeds are bitter, expectorant, and aphrodisiac and are useful in helminthiasis, typhoid fever, pain in the pelvic region, uropathy, leprosy, vitiated conditions of vata and scrofula. The seed contains up to 40% of a drying oil.  It is used for lighting, varnish etc. Seed oil is laxative, anthelmintic, depurative maturant and tonic and is useful in helminthesis, otalgia,  haemorrhoids, splenopathy, hepatopathy, inflammations, leprosy and dermatopathy.  
  7. Flowers are astringent, refrigerant, anodyne, stomachic, vermifuge,  diuretic, emmenagogue,deobstruant, alexipharmic. They are useful in cephalolgia, gastropathy, verminosis, strngury, dysmenarrhoea and fever.
  8. The stem bark is anthelmintic, astringent and bitter tonic. It is used as a tonic in India. It can be harvested at any time of the year and is used fresh or dried.
  9. The fruit is antiseptic and febrifuge. The pulp is used as a vermifuge. The fruit is harvested in the autumn when it is fully ripe and can be used fresh or dried.
  10. The root bark is emetic, emmenagogue, purgative and vermifuge. It is highly effective against ringworm and other parasitic skin diseases. It can be harvested at any time of the year and is used fresh or dried.
  11. This plant should be used with caution, preferably under the supervision of a qualified practitioner. Excess causes diarrhoea, vomiting and symptoms of narcotic poisoning.
  12. The musk-scented seeds are used as beads in rosaries.
  13. The fruits are a source of a flea powder and an insecticide. The whole fruit is ground up and used. The fruit pulp is also used as an insect repellent.
  14. Wood - tough, durable, moderately heavy, somewhat brittle, handsomely marked, takes an excellent polish. It has a musk-like aroma. It is used for making furniture, packing cases etc.
  15. As scent: Stem: Fresh Crushed Dried-The wood smells strongly of musk.
  16. Fodder: Leaves are lopped for fodder and are highly nutritious.
  17. Fuel: Fuelwood is a major use of M. azedarach. It has a calorific value is 5100 kcal/kg.
  18. Timber: M. azedarach wood (the ‘white cedar’ of commerce), which resembles mahogany, is used to manufacture agricultural implements, furniture, plywood, boxes, poles, tool handles; it is used in cabinet making and in construction because of its resistance to termites. The density is 510-660 kg/cubic m.
  19. Lipids: Oil suitable for illumination has been extracted experimentally from berries.
  20. Poison: Aqueous and alcoholic extracts of leaves and seed reportedly control many insect, mite and nematode pests. However, because they contain toxic components, care is needed in their use. The fruit of M. azedarach is highly toxic to warm-blooded animals; the consumption of 6-8 fruit can cause nausea, spasms, and in children, even death.
  21. Other products: Fruit stones make ideal beads and are used in making necklaces and rosaries.
  22. Shade or shelter: Widely planted as a shade tree in coffee and abaca (Musa textilis) plantations.
  23. Ornamental: A well-known ornamental grown, for example, as an avenue tree, for its scented flowers and shady, spreading crown.
  24. Intercropping: M. azedarach is a useful species for growing with crops such as wheat. It has been successfully planted with sugarcane.

 Miscellaneous Information

The name Azadarach implies a poisonous plant and the fruit is considered to be so.

The stony seeds are employed in making necklaces and rosaries.  The rosaries worn by Muslim Fakirs and Sadhus are usually made with these seeds.  It is believed that these rosaries drive away the evil spirits.

Because of the divided leaves, the generic name is derived from the Greek ‘melia’ (the ash); the specific name comes from the Persian ‘azzadirackt’ (noble tree).
