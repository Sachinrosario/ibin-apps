 Other Biological Details
HABIT & HABITAT
A large deciduous tree, indigenous in the forest of Madhya Pradesh. Cultivated mainly in Eastern U. P. and Bihar.  Mahua is ordinarily drought and frost hardy, but suffers under severe conditions.  It is reported to be unaffected by excessive monsoons and is found in waterlogged or low lying clayey and shallow soils in Mysore.  It is strong light demander and is readily suppressed under shade. When young, it may be browsed by deer and cattle.  It coppices fairly well when cut in the dry season, but not when cut during rains.  

Altitude: up to 1 200 m Mean annual temperature: 2-46 deg C Mean annual rainfall: 550-1 500 mm Soil type: M. latifolia grows best in deep loamy or sandy-loam soils with good drainage, it also occurs on shallow bouldery, clayey and calcareous soils.

Natural habitat
Mahua is a frost resisting tree of the dry tropics and sub-tropics, common in deciduous forests and dry sal plain forests. The tree is usually found scattered in pastures and cultivated fields in central India. It is extensively cultivated near villages.

Availability :  
Mahua tree is a native of Central India.  It mostly distributed in Western Ghats, Eastern Ghats, Gujarat and Chota Nagpur of Bihar.  It can also be seen in Sub-Himalayan region of Punjab, and Himachal Pradesh.  Mahua was found from plains to 900m. altitude.  Mahua trees are planted extensively in South Indian States.


Status : Commonly found in open and deciduous forests and also as cultivated.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Attains a height of 40-60 ft.. Thrives  on dry, stony ground, . It sheds leaves from February to April. Leaves are elliptic or oblong , shortly acuminate; calyx  lobes 4, rusty tomentose; corolla-tube fleshy, lobes erect; anthers 20-30, 3-seriate sub sessile. Flowers are cream-coloured, clustering near the ends of the branches. Fruits are green when unripe, and reddish yellow or orange when ripe, fleshy, one to two inches in length, with 1-4 seeds which ripen about three months after the petals  have fallen.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Breeding System :

Phenology :

Leaf fall occurs from February to April, flowers appear in March-April, fruits ripen from June to August. M. latifolia is long-lived and starts bearing from about the 10th year. A full grown tree can produce up to 90 kg of flowers in a year. 

Pollinators:
It is believed to be pollinated by bats which feed on the corollas

Seed Biology :
There are about 450 seeds/kg. Seeds are produced plentifully every second or third year. They lose viability within a short period and the oily fruit should be sown directly in the field as the seeds become available

Seedling Germination :

Natural regeneration: is by seeds which germinate early in the rainy season, soon after falling.

Artificial regeneration; is by direct sowing and transplanting nursery raised seedlings.  Mature fruit is collected from the ground from May to July .
Fresh seeds should be sown about July-Aug and covered with earth.  Direct sowing in prepared lines or patches is preferred to transplanting which is troublesome as the seedlings develop long and delicate tap-roots.  For transplanting seeds should be either sown direct deep pots or baskets or seedlings transplanted into them from the nursery during the first rainy season a few weeks after germination, they are planted in forest early in second rainy season.

M. indica is propagated by direct seeding, seedlings or stumps. Seeds should be sown when fresh in long polypots to accommodate the long taproot. Seedlings should be ready to plant in 2-4 months, or can be maintained for longer with regular root pruning. Seedlings are frost tender. 1-year-old stumps establish more successfully than bare root seedlings.

Cultivation:
Mahua can be planted at a spacing of 3-8 m x 3-8 m and worked on 25-30 year coppice cycle to produce a mean annual increment of 3-5 cu. m/ha. Fire tracing and fencing of plantations are essential in the early stages along with clean weeding and soil working around seedlings. The tree is a light demander, drought resistant and frost hardy. It coppices well if felled in the hot season.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests: Tree is damaged by Loranthaceous parasites.

Leaves are eaten by caterpillers ; Achaea janata, Anuga multiplicans, Bombotelia megatrix, Metanastria hyrtaca and few species of Phodoneura.

Two species of Acrocercops mine the leaves and blotch them.

Stathmopoda basiplectra is a serious pest of seeds. Among the defoliators of the tree are Achaea janata, Anuga multiplicans, Bombotelia nugatrix, Metanastria hyrtaca and the larvae of Acrocercops euthycolona and A. phaeomorphia mine the leaves. Unaspis acuminata is a sap sucker and Indarbella quadrinotata feeds on the bark. 

Diseases: 

The fungi, Polystictus steinheilianus causes decay in felled timber, Fomes caryophylli causes heartrot; Cercospora haticola causes leaf spot and Scopella echimulata is a leaf rust. Loranthus is a serious pest of trees in some localities. Leaf blight is caused by Pestalotiopsis dichaeta.

Rust - Scopella echinulata, White spongy rot-Polystictus steinheilianus, heart rot of stems-Fomea caryophylli,; root and butt rot-Polyporus gilvis.

Other Uses
  1. The flowers are fresh and rich in sugars and used for making syrups, which produce coolness during summer.  Flowers are demulcent and used as tonic for general weakness.  The flowers are fermented and distilled for making liquors and vinegar.  Corolla is eaten raw and as dried.  The stamens are boiled for about 6 hours, left to simmer until the water evaporates.  The softened stamens are eaten with rice and tamarind.  These are cooked with rice, made into sweet meats.  A favorite liquor is distilled from corollas.  One gallon of liquor can be distilled from 100kg of dried flowers.  This liquor contains about95%alcohol.  Syrup can be made extracted from corollas.  Jam can be prepared from the flowers.  Mahua berries are eaten after cooking.  It is used as a substitute for honey.
  2. Wood is very hard and employed from making carriages, sports goods, musical instruments, and oil presses and sugarcane crushers.  The wood is also used for ship building.
  3. Almost the entire production of oil from this seed is used in the production of washing soaps. Refined mahua oil can be utilized for cooking, confectionery and in chocolate making. Refined oil is used in the jute industry and in the manufacture of lubricating greases, candles, bathing oil, fatty alcohols, and stearic acid. Mahua oil has emollient properties and is used in treating skin disease, rheumatism and headaches. It is a good laxative and is used in treating habitual constipation, piles, and hemorrhoids. 
  4. Food: The sweet, fleshy corolla is eaten fresh or dried, powdered and cooked with flour. The fruit contains valuable oil that is sometimes used for cooking by the locals. Outer fruit coat is eaten as a vegetable and the fleshy cotyledons are dried and ground into a meal. Ripe fruits are used for fermenting liquor.
  5. Fodder: Leaves, flowers and fruits are lopped for goats and sheep. Seed cake is also fed to cattle.
  6. Timber: The heartwood is reddish brown, strong, hard and durable; very heavy (929 kg/cu. m), takes a fine finish. It is used for house construction, naves and felloes of cartwheels, door and window frames.
  7. Lipids: Oil from the fruit kernels principally consists of palmitic and stearic acids and is mainly used for soap and candle making.
  8. Poison: Mahua oil is used to treat seeds against pest infestation.
  9. Other products: De-fatted seed kernels contain 26-50 % saponin.
  10. Erosion control: Mahua has a large spreading superficial root system that holds soil together.
  11. Shade or shelter: The wide spreading crown provides shade for animals.
  12. Reclamation: Mahua is planted on wasteland with hard lateritic soils in India.
  13. Nitrogen fixing: Vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizal associations and root colonization have been observed in mahua.
  14. Soil improver: The seed cake has been used as fertilizer.
  15. Ornamental: Mahua is occasionally planted as an avenue tree.
  16. Boundary or barrier or support: It is planted along the boundaries of fields.
  17. Intercropping: M. latifolia can be raised with agricultural crops.
