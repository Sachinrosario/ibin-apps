HABIT & HABITAT
Coconut is a majestic perennial palm. It is grown in numerous islands and also in the humid coastal tracks of tropical countries. India ranks second in the world production of coconuts 6088 million nuts from 108 million hectares.

The palm is most widely cultivated in the tropics. So ancient and so wide are its cultivation and uses, that the task of assigning an original habitat for it is a formidable one. There are good reasons for placing it in the Melanesian region. Many varieties of Cocos are found in that region and also a large number of insect species specific to it. Evidence has been adduced to assign its origin to a "submerged land to the north west of New Guinea" (6)

The coconut palm thrives best within the tropic zone. The major coconut areas lie between 200N. and 200s on both sides of the equator. Though it is found beyond this region, as far as 27°N. and 27°S., cultivation in these extreme regions has not been successful and the palm does not fruit.

The most important coconut producing countries in the world are India, Ceylon, Malaya, Indonesia, Philippines and the South Sea Islands in the Pacific. It is cultivated to a small extent in East Africa, West Indies and Central America. In India, the bulk of the acreage under coconut is concentrated in the coastal and deltaic regions of south India. Successful inland cultivation is by no means rare, and large estates are situated far inland and up to an elevation of 2,000-3,000 ft., in Tumkur, Hassan and Mysore districts, where favourable soil and climatic conditions render its cultivation profitable. Coconut is cultivated also in parts of Orissa and Bengal. The principal areas of cultivation in India are Travancore & Cochin, Malabar, S. Kanara, East Godavari and Tanjore areas of Kerala and Tamil nadu, Puri and Cuttack districts in Orissa.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A tall and stately palm growing to a height of 80 ft. or more when fully mature, bearing a crown of large, pinnate leaves. The trunk is stout (1-1.5 ft. diam.), straight or gently curved, rising from a swollen base surrounded by a mass of roots, rarely branched. The trunk is columnar, light greyish-brown in colour, usually erect, but often leans towards the light at the edge of plantations and along the direction of the wind in windy areas. The scars of encircling petioles are clearly seen where leaves have fallen and on the upper centre of each scar are the scars left by the peduncles in bearing palms. As 12-14 leaves are shed per year, the age of the palm may be ascertained by counting the scars. The leaves are 6-18 ft. long, pinnatisect, leaflets 2-3 ft. long, narrow and tapering (6). Leaves borne in a terminal radiating crown, which, in an adult palm, consists of 25-35 opened leaves and a central bud, which is the most vulnerable part of the tree, with leaves in various stages of development. Leaves large, paripinnate, 4.5-6.0 m long, weighing 10-15 kg, petiole, stout with clasping sheath at base, grooved above, channelling water into centre of crown, rounded beneath. Pinnae 200-250 in number, along upper three-quarters of the petiole, 50-120 x 1.5-5 cm, sessile, narrowly linear lanceolate, base conduplicate, apex acute. Stomata confined mainly to lower surface, about 200 per mm2, cuticle thick.

Inflorescence, 1-2 m long, consists of a central axis or rachis, with up to 40 lateral branches which bear 200-300 male flowers above, opening from tip of branches downwards, and one or more female flowers at base which are receptive after the pollen has been shed. Male flowers borne singly or 2's or 3's, sessile, 0.7-1.3 x 0.5-O.7cm, pale yellow, perianth segments 6 in 2 whorls, 3 outer tepals small, 3 inner tepals larger, ovate, acute, coriaceous, stamens 6 in 2 whorls, dehiscing longitudinally, pistil rudimentary with 3 teeth at apex bearing nectaries. Female flowers globose, 2-3 cm in diameter, bracteoles 2, tepals 6 of nearly equal size in 2 whorls, suborbicular, concave, coriaceous, persistent and enlarging in fruit, ovary large, 3-locular, globosely 3-sided, stigmas 3, terminal, triangular, white, with 3 nectaries near base. Number of female flowers per inflorescence varies, but is usually 20-40. Young palms have fewer female flowers per spadix (4). The fruit is ovoid, three-angled, 6-12 in. long, containing a single seed.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Back to Top

Breeding System :
Coconut is monoceious with numerous male and few female flowers in each spadix, which is borne singly in axil of each leaf of a bearing palm. Coconut is both self and cross-pollinating. The flowers are nectiferous and sweet scented and are visited by insects particularly bees and to a lesser extent by flies and ants, so that fair amount of cross-poliination is possible. The pollen is dry and some wind pollination may also occur.

Phenology :
Under favourable conditions coconut palms begin to yield in 5-6 years after planting, and give full yields from 10th year onwards.

Back to Top

Pollinators:
Flowers are self pollinating. The male flowers are the first to open, beginning at the top of each branch and proceeding towards the base. After the pollen has been shed in the bud, the flowers open and later abciss, the whole process taking one day. The male phase corresponds with the opening of the first flower and the shedding of the last flower, and in tall palms usually takes 16-22 days. A female flower remains receptive for 24 hours, the female phase is the interval between the first flower at the top of each spadix becoming receptive when the stigma opens and the last flower at the bottom of the spadix becoming post-receptive, when the stigma turns brown and dries up. The female phase lasts 5-7 days and usually begins 22 days after the spathe has opened.

Seed Biology :
The fruit is ovoid, three-angled, 6-12 in. long, containing a single seed. The exocarp (outer husk) is thick and fibrous and encloses a hard and bony endocarp (shell). Adhering inside the endocarp is the testa with a thick albuminous endosperm (meat). At one end of the shell are three pores below one of which, and embedded in the meat, is the small embryo. The cavity of the endosperm is large and is filled in the unripe fruit with a watery fluid (Coconut water); it is only partly filled in the ripe fruit (6).

Back to Top

Seedling Germination :

The seed has no dormancy, and growth of the embryo and seedling are continuous. Plant fresh coconuts (with husks) on their side, half buried in moist soil (half of the coconut is exposed) -- if kept moist, will sprout in 2-3 months. Growth is slow at first but picks up as the palm matures (9). The first internal sign of germination is the swelling and elongation of the cotyledon, one end of which carries the growing embryo through the softest and largest of the three eyes. The plumule then emerges from the sheathing cotyledon into the mesocarp, followed later by the radicle. The tip of the plumule is protected by a horny scale leaf and more scale leaves are then produced, each larger than the preceding one, until the shoot emerges from the exocarp and the first simple leaves are produced. The short- lived radicle is the first root, followed by adventitious roots which develop from the nodes of the shoot. Side rootlets are produced which ramify in the mesocarp. The first roots emerge from the exocarp some time later than the shoot (4).

Cultivation:

Climate: Coconut is essentially a crop of the humid tropics. It is mainly grown in the coastal plains. Rainfall is the most important factor affecting its growth. A rainfall for 100 to 225 cm per annum, evenly distributed is most important. Coconut requires an equable climate. The optimum mean annual temperature for its best growth and maximum yield is 270 C . Frost and low humidity adversely affect the yield of the palm. Persistent high humidity is also harmful and bud rot is more common under such conditions. The palm requires bright sunshine of 2000 hrs a year (1, 12).

Soil: The coconut palm adapts itself remarkably well and thrives in almost all types of well-drained tropical soils, such as coastal sand, red loam, laterite, alluvial and reclaimed soil of the marshy lowland. A water table that is too high and remains static for too long is harmful.

Planting material: Since coconut is a cross pollinated perennial crop the selection of seeds is of vital importance. Selection has to be made at the mother-palm level and at the seedling stage. The mother palms should be healthy, high yielding and regular in bearing. Also factors such as proportion and distribution of female flowers, high setting percentage and a high copra content should also be taken into consideration. If prepotent palms can be identified by progeny testing the yielding ability of the planting material can be further assured. A careful selection of the seedlings is important. The seedlings should be healthy and have a minimum of six leaves when they are one year old. The early splitting of leaves is a desirable character. Nine month old seedlings having a minimum of four leaves can also be planted (1, 12).

Preparation of land: The clearing and levelling of land have to be done before preparing the pits for transplanting seedlings. The depth of the pit depends on the soil type. In the sandy loam of low water table the planting pits of the size 1mx1mx1m is recommended. In laterite soils with a rocky substratum, deeper and wider pits 1.2x1.2x1.2m are necessary. At the time of planting the pits have to be filled up with loose soil leaving a final depth of about 60cm. However when the water table is high planting on the surface or on soil mounds may be necessary. In a littoral sandy soil, circular pits 1.5mx1.5mx1.5m are made and partially filled with alternate layers of red earth and coconut husks (150 husks).

Spacing: The spacing of palms requires careful consideration. A spacing of 7.5 to 9 m accomodating 177 to 124 palms per hectare is adopted under the square system of planting. The triangular method accomodates 20 to 25 more palms per hectare. In the hedge system of planting, a spacing of 5 to 5.5m along the row is given. Larger number of palms can be planted in a unit area ensuring sufficient light on each palm.

Transplanting and after care: In well drained soils seedlings can be transplanted at the beginning of the South-Westerly monsoon. If facilities for irrigation are available, it is advisable to take up planting at least a month before the onset of the monsoon. In the low lying areas, seedlings are better transplanted after the rainy season. The transplanted seedlings should be shaded and irrigated properly during the summer. Irrigation with 45 litres of water once in four days is the optimum in sandy soils. Provision for drainage is also important. Apply the complete NPK fertilizer from the early stage. The first dose should be applied 3 months after planting. Large quantities of green leaf or compost to the soil can be applied where it has a poor organic matter content. The pits should be cleared of all the weeds and the soil washed down by rain. The palms should be frequently examined for insect attack or fungus diseases and the necessary remedial action adopted promptly (1, 12).

Care of adult palms: The palms generally start bearing at the age of five or seven years after planting and the stabilized yield is obtained from about the tenth year onwards for 50 to 60 years. Regular intercultivation and manuring of the palm are essential for stepping up and maintaining the productivity of palms at a high level. Tillage including digging, ploughing, piling of mounds, levelling of mounds, etc. and the making of shallow basins each with a radius of 2 m, at the beginning of the monsoon and filling them up at the close of the monsoon are beneficial. In sandy soils, which are of low fertility and do not have a luxuriant growth of weeds, regular intercultivation may not be quite necessary but in other types of soils, intercultivation is essential for keeping down the weeds under check and for creating a soil mulch. The method of intercultivation mainly depends on the local conditions, soil type, topography, size of holding, intensity of rainfall, etc.

Manuring: The recommended dose of fertilizers is 500 g of N, 300 g of P2O5 and 1,200 g of K2O per palm per year for the ordinary tall variety. For soil which are poor in organic matter, the application of green manure or compost at 50 kg per palm is recommended. Green-manuring crops usually grown are cowpea, sunnhemp. Fertilizers may be applied in two spilt doses. After the receipt of summer showers one third of the dose of fertilizers is spread around the palm within a radius of 1.8m and raked in. Circular basins 1.8 m in radius and 25 cm in depth may be dug in July-August and the green leaf manure or compost applied and partially covered. The remaining two-third dose of the fertilizers may be spread in the first week of September and the basins closed. Under irrigated conditions and in areas with assured supply of moisture throughout the year the annual dose of fertilizers may be applied every three months in equal split doses (1, 12).

Irrigation: In regions with a long spell of dry weather, there is a definite set back in the growth and yield of the coconut palm. An increase of 50% to 100% in the yield of nuts can be obtained by giving summer irrigation. The quantity and interval between irrigations depend upon the soil and the climatic conditions. In loamy soils copious irrigation, once a week is sufficient. In sandy soils along the sea coast during summer it grows without any adverse effects.

Mango, jackfruit, arecanut and other tree crops and a number of annual crops are commonly grown along the coconut palms in household gardens. Recent studies have shown that under rainfed conditions tuber crops, banana, pineapple, upland rice and pulses can be grown successfully as intercrops in adult coconut plantations without any detrimental effects on the fertility of the soil and the productivity of the palms. Under irrigated conditions cacao forms a very beneficial crop combination with coconut. Other crops suitable for mixed cropping include pepper and tree spices, such as cinnamom, clove, and nutmeg. Mixed farming including the raising of milch animals on fodder grass and legumes raised as intercrops are manured with cowdung and urine. This practice improves the soil fertility by recycling organic wastes, enhances the yield of palms and generates additional income and employment potential (1, 12).

Varieties:

There are two distinct varieties of coconut the tall and the dwarf. Dwarf X Tall and Tall X Dwarf hybrids have been found to be early bearers and superior to their parents in yield potential under favourable conditions. The tolerance of these hybrids to the root wilt disease adds to their virtue.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES
pests:

The main pests of coconut palms are the Rhinoceros beetle, nettle caterpillars, Red stripe palm weevil coconut case caterpillar and other bagworms, and larger animals such as squirrels, rats and wild pigs.


Rhinoceros beetle (Oryctes rhinkoceros, L., O. trituberculatas, Lansb.)

This pest is large black beetle with a horn on its head, measuring about 1 and a half inches in lenght. It is the adult stage which causes damage to the coconut palm.. The young stages or grubs are never found on the palm. They live in old logs, sawdust and similar places on the ground.

Damage: The adult beetles bore through the palm founds leaving hole in the midribs. They also eat up the leaves eventually open large pieces are missing from them. This damage weakens the palms and reduces yields. Sometimes the beetle bores right through the main shoot of the palm killing it completely.

Life cycle: The female beetle starts laying eggs one month after emergence from the cocoon. The eggs, averaging 30-40 per female, are deposited in decaying logs of palm trees, in refuse, leaf mould, seadust of timber mills, etc.,but never in living tissues of plants. The eggs hatch in about two weeks or less. The larvae feed on decaying plant material and the larval stage last from two to three months. Pupation occurs in the deeper layers of the soil within a cocoon made of earth or earth and vegetable matter. The adults emerge about 3 to 4 weeks, but remain inside the cocoon for about the same period of time. The whole life-cycle lasts at least 4 to 6 months. The adult beetles fly in the evening after dark and greatly attracted by light.

Control: For control of this pest prevention is much more important and effective than any cure. Attacks of the insect can be avoided or reduced by: (1) Planting the palms at least one mile or more from the nearest sawmill (sawdust is the breeding ground of the pest); (2) Getting rid of (by burning) all old logs and other waste from the ground around the palms, and keeping the grass slashed low.

Chemical control is only possible in very young palms when the growing point can still be reached. A mixture of 2 parts of BHC (Agrocide 26 D.P.) and 10 parts of damp sawdust can be pushed into the bore holes of the pests.

The larvea of the Rhinoceros beetle in Sarawak may be attacked by a scoliid wasp Scolia procer, III.


Coconut Mite ( Aceria guerreronis)

This is considered to be the serious pest of coconuts. The adult female is between 36-52 µm in width and 205-355 µm in length. It is able to penetrate between upper and lower tepals and finally to the fruit surface covered by the perianth within a few weeks or month after fertilization. With development cycle from egg to adult of about ten days mite numbers can build up rapidly. Apparently, reproductive activities take place beneath the perianth. Fruits remain susceptible to mite attack throughout the whole development, but on more mature fruits, coconut mite are found rarely and in small numbers.

Damage: mites feed on the perianth region of the fruit and cause physical damage so that as newly formed tissue expands, the surface becomes necrotic and suberized. Uneven growth results in distortion and stunting of the coconut, leading to reductions in copra yield of up to 30% or greater; losses are greater from earlier infestations. In addition to damaging the fruit, it can kill coconut seedlings by feeding on their meristematic tissues. These are also known to be disease vectors.

Control: 1)Chemical- Spraying monocrotophos, dicrotophos or chinomethionate on to branches of fruits every 20-30 days significantly reduces damage.

2) Spraying 1% of azadirachtin (Neemazal) at the rate of 4ml/litre of water or root feeding/stem injection at 7.5 ml with same quantity water significantly control the level of infestation.

3) Natural agents- predators on coconut mites like Bdella Distincta ( phytoseiids) and Neosciulus mumai & N. paspalivorous (tarsenomid), acerogenous fungus Hirsutella thompsonii are able to control these mites to a great extent.

4) Resistant cultivars: cultivars with improved coconut mite resistance are future possibility. This may involve bringing of new cultivars or replanting using seednuts from productive trees which show little mite damage.


Red stripe palm weevil (Rhynchophodrus schach, Oliv.)

This is a large black beetle with a long 'snout' or 'nose'. It has a red stripe running down its thorax. Unlike Oryctes the larvae does the damage. The adult weevils, reddish-brown, 3.5cm long lay eggs in soft and damaged tissues of the palm. The pale brown larva live for about 2 months and burrow into the crown and trunk. Young palms are especially liable to damage. It also attacks by bore holes of Rhinoceros beetles or through the wounds in the trunks.

Control : Phytosanitary measures including the destruction of fallen trunks,


Nettle caterpillars

These are the young stages or larvae of certain moths. They are characterised by their stinging spines which are most painful to touch. They are brightly coloured, mostly bluish or greenish in colour, and sometimes with stripes. They are sometimes called 'slug caterpillars' and are locally known as There are four main species of slug caterpillars found: Setora nitens Wkr., Parasa lepida Cram Darna trima Moore., and Chalcoscelis albiguttatus Snell.

Damage: Nettle caterpillars feed on the leaves of coconut. In a serious attack only the midrib remains.

An outbreak of slug caterpillars was reported in 1969 (February / March) along the Rambungan coastal coconut plantations of the first division an estimated area of 500 acres or more.

Life cycle: The scale-like eggs are laid on the underside of the coconut leaves. They hatch in about a week or less. The caterpillar stage lasts about one month. The mature larva migrates to the leaf axil or to the soil where they pupate in a spherical brownish scocoon. Adults emerge after 2 to 3 weeks.

Control: In the nursery or very young palms, Dipterex 80 per cent D.P. may be used at the rate of 1 oz. per gallons of water.


Bagworms

These pests are the young stages or caterpillars of certain moths. They are characterised by the possession of a case, which they inhabit throughout their development. The case is made of silk and is built up in size as the caterpillar matures. Finally pupation takes place in the same case, which is first attached to the plant and closed off (forming the 'bags').

There are 3 species of bagworms recorded in coconut: Clania sp., Cremastopsyche pendula Joannis. (Metisa plana Wlk.), and Mahasena sp.

Damage: The young caterpillars scrape the epidemis whilst the more mature ones make holes in the leaf. In a serious attack the coconut or oil palm leaves may be skeletonised.

Control:

In the nurseries the bagworms may be removed by hand and destroyed. If a serious attack occurs bagworms can be controlled by: BHC 26 per cent D.P. e.g. Agrocide 26 D. at 4 oz. in gallons of water; (2) Dipterex 80 per cent S.P. at 11/2 oz. in 4 gallons of water; (3) Rotenone (Tuba) as described for the control of vegetable pests or (4) Lead arsenate at 4 lb. per acre.


Rats, Squirrels, Pigs

Squirrels and wild pigs are important pests of newly-established coconut holdings, whilst rats are of particular importance as pests of the fruits of soil palms.

Control: These pests are very difficult to control. Pigs can only be kept out of a holding by a high barbed wire fence or similar barrier. This methods is unfortunately very expensive over a large area. Trapping and hunting are the only means to reduce the numbers of this pest.

There is little that can be done against squirrels at present except hunting and trapping, despite the general inefficiency of these methods. They will not take the kind of poisoned baits used against rats.

Rats can be prevented from climbing palms by placing metal bands around the trunks. Baiting with zinc phosphide or warfarin (as described earlier under rice pests) should also be carried out.


Diseases:[4, 21]

Coconuts suffer from a number of diseases, few of which can be accurately characterized, and the cause of most of them is not known with certainty. If the central meristem is killed the palm dies. If the leaves wilt and are shed quicker than they can be produced, photosynthesis and assimilates are reduced. Temporary or permanent tapering of the trunk reduces translocation of water and nutrients. It is difficult to differentiate between symptoms caused by pathogens and physiological causes produced as a result of drought, waterlogging and nutrient deficiencies. Observable symptoms may be the result of factors, which were operative one or two years previously. Tall palms are difficult subjects for detailed investigations. More detailed and prolonged studies on the structure and physiology of the palm and the relationship to its environment are required if some of the disease problems are to be solved satisfactorily.


Bud disease

Is caused by Phytophthora palmivora (Butl.), results in wilting and collapse of the youngest unfurling leaves, followed by similar symptoms in successive lower leaves; the central bud rots and is foul-smelling. The disease appears to be endemic in high rainfall areas in many parts of the tropics and becomes epidemic following hurricanes and other damage. It should not be confused with lightning injury, which is common in coconuts, causing rotting of the central bud by saprophytic organisms and with the outer leaves usually dying first.

Control: Infected palms should be cut down and burned.


Lethal yellowing

Was formerly called 'the unknown disease', was present in Jamaica in 1870, and is now found in the Cayman Islands, Cuba, Florida and Haiti. The cause is not yet known. It results in quick mortality. The first visible symptom is shedding of young and maturing nuts, followed by sodden patches at the base of the folded leaves, after which the expanded leaves turn a bright yellow.

Control: Growing resistant varieties like 'Malayan Dwarf'.


Kerala wilt

Also known as 'wilt (root) disease', became important after the great floods of 1882. It is almost certainly caused by a virus. It is estimated to cause an annual loss of 1 million rupees per year. It takes 3-15 years to kill the palm. Chlorotic streaks occur on the leaves; leaflets become flaccid and ribbed, with tip necrosis, leaves and crown become smaller; inflorescence and nuts become smaller with fewer female flowers; the root system deteriorates from the tip backwards.


Bronze leaf wilt

The leaves become bronze in colour, wilt, hang down and are shed. It is usually considered to be the result of adverse soil conditions, particularly drought or waterlogging.

Economic Potential and Uses

Coconut oil is used in a wide range of food and nonfood products. It is a raw material for the production of medical foods and infant food formulations. In industry, the fatty acids of coconut oil provide a versatile feedstock for an array of products from diesel fuel substitute to hygienic products (2).


(a) Edible Products: Coconut oil is used extensively as frying oil. Physical blends and interesterified mixtures of coconut oil and hydrogenated palm oil are processed into margarines and shortenings. Coconut jam. emulsion derived from coconut cream in cane sugar, is consumed as bread spread and rice cake topping. Filled milk, in liquid or powder form contains coconut oil and polyunsaturated oil emulsified in skim milk. As spray oil for crackers and cookies, coconut oil improves shelf life of these products because of its resistance to oxidative rancidity. Coconut oil is widely used as cream fat and as a component in biscuit cream and confectionery oil. In many food products, coconut oil is a lipid component where natural resistance to oxidative rancidity is sought (2).

(b) Nonfood products: A commonly used nonfood product derived from coconut oil is soap. Laundry bar soaps made by boiled or cold procedures have excellent lathering property even in moderately hard water. A blend of tallow-coconut oil in ratios from 67:33 to 85: 15 form an "ideal" fat charge for toilet soaps. Soap from such blends exhibit desirable characteristic." related to lather quickness, low mechanical erosion, and absence of swelling or cracking of soap bars is processed for nonfood products.

(c) Copra Meal: The extraction of oil from the dried coconut kernel leaves the residue called copra meal. Copra meal is utilized as a component in animal feeds (2).

Uses:

The coconut palm or 'kalpa vriksha' provides many necessities of life like food and shelter. Of all the tropical palms it is the outstanding one. It is mainly cultivated for the nuts from which 2 important products 'copra' and fibre are obtained. Copra yields oil and oil cake. The trunk of the mature palm is used as timber for houses and the leaves are used for thatching houses, fencing etc. The unopened spathe is tapped for toddy called 'neera'. Sweet toddy can be converted into jaggery and sugar, fermented toddy is a mild alcoholic drink and vinegar can be made from it. Water from tender coconut is a refreshing drink. In Kerela the extraction of coir from the husk of nuts and the manufacture of coir products provides employment for thousands of people. The coconut shell is largely used for fuel and for the production of charcoal and making a variety of curios. The shell flour is used as a filler for plastics. Thus every part of the palm is useful in one way or the other (1, 12).

Harvesting Practices
The coconut palm is unique in that once it attains the normal bearing stage, it continues to bear a bunch of nuts in every leaf axil almost at monthly intervals all year round and throughout its life extended over 50 to 60 years. The nuts mature nearly one year after fertilization. Generally, harvesting is done once in 45 to 60 days. Nuts must be harvested fully ripe for making copra or desiccated coconut. For coir they are picked about one month short of maturity, so that husks will be green. Coconuts are usually picked by human climbers, or cut by knives attached to end of long bamboo poles, this being the cheapest method. With pole, a man can pick from 250 palms in a day, by climbing, only 25. In some areas nuts are allowed to fall naturally, and collected regularly (19, 20). Tender nuts in demand as a delicious drink in West Bengal and Maharashtra are best harvested at the age of 6-7months. Large scale harvesting of the tender nuts is not being done in other parts of the country. Nuts for culinary purposes, for making cup copra and good quality fibre are harvested at the age of about 11 months whereas those for ball copra and coarse coir are harvested only when they are fully ripe.
The average yield per hectare varies from 10,000 to 14,000 nuts per annum. From a well maintained garden an annual yield of 25,000 nuts per hectare can be obtained. Nearly one-third of the annual yield is harvested during the three months March, April and May (1, 12).

Produce and Products

The main product obtained from the coconut is copra or the dried kernel of the nut. The cup copra is prepared by splitting the husked nut into two halves and drying them in the sun. After drying for a day or two the kernel gets detached from the shell. The drying is continued for another four to five days. Ball copra is prepared by storing fully ripe nuts for 8-12 months on a raised platform in a shed. They are sometime smoked or heated with a slow fire to accelerate drying. During storage water inside the nuts gets dried up and the kernel gets detached. Nuts are then husked and the shells are carefully broken. The ball copra thus obtained is clean, sweet and highly valued (1, 12, 17).


The coconut fibre is known as coir. For retting, husks are buried in shallow pits in low-lying areas subject to the tidal flow of backwaters for about 6-12 months. The coir obtained from dry husks would be of inferior quality. It is easier to extract coir from green husks after sufficient retting and the quality of coir is good. The coir is then dried in the sun and spun into coir yarn by hand or with a machine. Coir yarn is used as such or made into ropes, mats, nets, bags etc.

Processing Techniques

Extraction of Oil

The extraction of oil from copra is one of the oldest seed-crushing industries in the world. The processes employed to obtain oil from copra include the traditional milling, improved wet milling and dry milling.

Traditional Milling:

It is essentially a wet-milling process. However, the recovery and quality of oil is slow, and the byproduct is not useful for any food application. It is practiced only at the domestic level. Fresh coconuts are grated and mixed with hot water, the mixture is hand-kneaded into a white emulsion. The emulsion is poured into clay or wooden containers with a plugged opening at the bottom. The grated coconut is repeatedly pressed and the emulsion is collected. The combined emulsion in the container is allowed to settle for a few hours for separation of a creamy layer at the top and a watery layer at the bottom. The watery layer is removed through the bottom hole and the creamy layer is boiled to separate the oil from water, which is cooled and filtered through cloth. The residue on cloth contains about 16% oil, 50% protein and 12% hydrates. The oil recovery in the traditional extraction process is only about 60%.

Wet milling:

The advantage in this process is the utilization of byproducts for food applications, besides the extraction of oil. The technology has not been adopted for large scale processing, mainly due to technical difficulties in obtaining a high oil, percentage and the high cost involved in the installation of the plant. Tbc problem exists in extraction of the emulsion from the fresh endosperm an breaking the emulsion to separate oil. The percentage of cell rupture influences the recovery of oil and proteins.

Dry milling:

This involves the extraction of oil from copra. It is a conventional method of industrial coconut oil extraction on a commercial scale. The processing, system is almost similar to that for other oilseeds. Copra is dried, if necessary cleaned of stones, fibers and other foreign materials and crushed to a coarse and subsequently, into a fine powder. This facilitates the extraction of oil, improves its efficiency. The fine powder is heated to about 160°C using a traditional process, while only up to 120°C using modern processes. The optimum moisture content of copra to be treated in expellers is about 2-3%. This moisture level is regulated during cooking, either by continued drying of copra or by the addition of water. Hydraulic presses require more moisture than expellers. The efficiency of oil extraction generally low and depends on the nature of the expeller (2, 18).

1. In India copra is crushed for oil in primitive ‘chekkus’, as well as in rotary ‘ghanis’, expellers and hydraulic presses. Maximum yield on pressing is 62.5%.

2. Common practice is a 2 stage process involving

- light pressing with a single stage expeller.

- cake then reground and pressed in a hydraulic press or solvent extracted.

3. The latter can reduce the oil content to below 1%. It is important to separate the ‘foots’ (cake residues etc.) from the crude oil by filtering or settling.

4. For edible purposes, commercial oil is refined by neutralizing the free fatty acid with caustic soda, washing the oil with warm water and deodorizing in a high vacuum, deodorizer with superheated steam (7).

Other Uses

Medical and infant food formulations: Medium chain fatty acids (MCFA), mainly C8 and C10, obtained by hydrolysis of coconut oil, is reesterified with glycerol to form a mixture of randomized chain triglyceride (MCT). MCTs are absorbed and oxidized rapidly with energy rated at 34.7 kj/g. a number of medical and infant food formulations have MCTs as the principle source of fat supplemented with polyunsaturates. Polyunsaturated vegetable oil is given to malnourished children as supplemental fat source in their diet (2).

Coconuts are used in folk remedies for tumors. Reported to be anthelmintic, antidotal, antiseptic, aperient, aphrodisiac, astringent, bactericidal, depurative, diuretic, hemostat, pediculicide, purgative, refrigerant, stomachic, styptic, suppurative, and vermifuge. Coconut somewhere or other, is a folk remedy for abscesses, alopecia, amenorrhea, asthma, blenorrhagia, bronchitis, bruises, burns, cachexia, calculus, colds, constipation, cough, debility, dropsy, dysentery, dysmenorrhea, earache, erysipelas, fever, flu, gingivitis, gonorrhea, hematemesis, hemoptysis, jaundice, menorrhagia, nausea, phthisis, pregnancy, rash, scabies, scurvy, sorethroat, stomach, swelling, syphylis, toothache, tuberculosis, tumors, typhoid, venereal diseases and wounds (19, 20).

Miscellaneous Information

SUGAR PRODUCTS FROM COCONUT:

NEERA

Neera, the sweet sap of the Palm, is fast becoming a popular drink on account of its highly nutritive value, delicious taste and agreeable flavor. It requires neither mechanical crushing, as in the case of cane, nor leaching like that of beet-root; it is obtained by slicing the spathes of Coconut.

The chemical percentage composition of Neera varies, depending on various factors, namely, place, type of palm, mode and season of its collection

OPERATIONS ON THE TREE

The Coconut throw outspathes during the flowering season. When these spathes are tapped, exudation of the juice begins.

NEERA COLLECTION

Neera is collected every morning just at sunrise. Neera is brought down from the top of the tree in either earthen post or vessels, and then poured into stainless steel containers and bigger vessels, after being filtered through a fine mesh cloth or wire-mesh. Only neat, clean and sweet neera is so found. Any portion that is fermented is destroyed immediately.

All neera collected as above is brought to a central place where it is processed. It contains all the constituents of a cool and healthy drink with food and mineral value. It builds the body; keeps the human system cool and improves digestion. It can be consumed in fairly large quantity without any harm to the system.

FERMENTATION

Fresh neera, as it trickles from the tree, is easily susceptible to fermentation at ordinary temperature, unless it is quickly treated with some preservative.

PRODUCTS OF NEERA

Gur, syrup, rab, sugar, candy confectionery, ice creams and various sweets are produced from Neera.


PALM GUR

GUR

Neera is converted into a solid or semi-solid crystalline mass, it is called gur. It has got a wide use as sweetening agent in Indian village food products. Palm gur has a characteristic smell of its own.

CLARIFICATION OF JUICE

Clarification of palm juices, commonly known as deliming process, is done using the phosphoric acid or super phosphate solution. Half of the quantity of the raw juice is taken with the pan and heated at 40°C. This juice is then delimed to neutrality ie., pH by adding either phosphoric acid direct or triple super phosphate solution slowly and stirring all the while.

BOILING

Boiling of the filtered juice is done over open-fired improved furnaces using metal pans of 20 to 24 gauge thickness. As boiling proceeds, the froth and foam coming up to the surface is removed by means of a perforated ladle. The juice is stirred at intervals to facilitate mixing and rapid evaporation.

When the juice thickens, the fire is judiciously controlled in order to prevent it from caramelising. Correct strike temperature is judged by patting a small quantity of the thickened mass in water and rolling in into ball shape. If ball forms into a hard one, the strike is over and the mass is moulded into moulds. The strike temperature can also be controlled through the use of thermometers. When the boiling syrup is allowed to crystallize, a candy known as Palm Candy is obtained.

MOULDS

Now, that the viscous mass has reached the right consistency, it is poured in suitable moulds. In South India, Coconut shells are generally used as moulds. Wooden and iron moulds are used in other parts of India.

In order to facilitate easy removal of the blocks from the moulds, the moulds are either moistened with water or besmeared with fresh sweet oil before putting the thick mass into them. After allowing the gur to set for some time, the gur is removed from the moulds and packed. The quality gur is always hard, crystalline and golden-coloured.


PALM SUGAR

CRYSTAL SUGAR MAKING: The initial process of sugar making is practically the same as that of gur. In this case also, juice is delimed, filtered and boiled. The juice when it reaches 110°C temperature is poured in the crystalliser.

Now the mass is stirred, twice or thrice in an hour, for about 3 to 4 hours. On cooling, crystals begin to form. After 24 hours, the mass is removed and poured in small quantities in the cage of the Centrifugal machine which when rotated separates the sugar from the rab. On working the machine with the handle, the cage rotates with a speed of 2,000 revolutions per minute and expels the molasses through the perforated side walls of the cage, fitted with copper gauge filter depositing the sugar crystals on the inner walls of the copper or brass gauge. In order to wash the sugar, water is sprayed on the sugar while centrifuging. This water cleans the sugar and passes through the cage flow, through a spout, down to the bucket kept below. The sugar deposited on the inner walls of the copper gauge is removed and the cage is fed with rab for the next charge.


The quality of sugar obtained by this method is slightly brown in colour and not or well defined crystalline structure. Various experiments are being conducted to manufacture white regular crystalline variety of sugar from Palm juice with the help of small vacuum pans. In the case of vacuum, boiling the caramalisation of sugar is much reduced as it boils at a low temperature. The size and shape of the sugar crystals can also be better controlled in a vacuum pan.
