Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Cardamom, popularly known as ‘Queen of Spices’, is the dried fruit of the tall perennial herbaceous plant Elettaria cardamomum Maton, belonging to the family Zingiberaceae. The generic name is probably derived from the ancient Tamil word ‘elattari’ meaning the seed of ‘Elam’ (1).  
                                                                                                                                               
Soil and climate
Cardamom is a shade loving plant (40-60%) cultivated in an altitude of 600 to 1200 m above MSL with an annual rainfall of 1500 to 4000 mm and a temperature range of 10 to 35°C. It is susceptible to wind and hence, gentle sloppy lands facing eastern or northern directions are considered ideal for its cultivation. It is generally grown in the evergreen forests where the surface soil is enriched by the deposition of organic matter due to organic recycling. The cardamom soils are found to be clay loam, distinctly acidic (5.0 to 6.6 pH), rich in organic matter (5.92%) and low in available phosphorous (14.71 kg/ha) and potassium (156.2 kg/ha) depicting the characteristics of the humid tropic soils (Kulkarni et al., 1971). It grows on a variety of soils from white quartz gravel, with only a shallow zone of humus accumulation, to chocolate coloured forest loams extending to considerable depths below the humus layer and red loamy soils. The soil depth is fairly deep with good drainage. Most of the cardamom growing soils are situated in heavy rainfall areas and therefore suffers from leaching of nutrients (Zachariah 1975) (5).  

Availability :

Western Ghats and adjoining areas of Kerala, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu (1).

Distribution 
Cardamom is indigenous to the evergreen rainforest of Western Ghats (Cardamom hills) of Southern India. Presently it is being cultivated in countries lying between 20° latitude north and south of equator at American, Asian and African continents. Guatemala, Tanzania, Sri Lanka, El Salvador, Vietnam, Laos, Costa Rica, Nicaragua, Thailand, Honduras, Cambodia and Papua New Guinea are the other cardamom growing countries. In India, cardamom is cultivated in the southern states of Kerala, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu lying between 8-30o and 14-30o N latitude and the longitude 75-77o (11). In Kerala, the main cardamom growing areas are in the high ranges of Kottayam district, the Nelliampathy range in Palakkad district, the Wynad and Kozhikode districts. 

Conservation/ Threat Status : Wild and cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES: A herbaceous perennial plant having subterranean rhizomatous stem with tall (around 5m) tall pseudostem formed by leaf sheaths. The plant has a characteristic fibrous root system of monocots and is very shallow in nature. Leaves are petiolate, distichous, linear, lanceolate, acuminate, sessile or very shortly petioled; glabrous above, softly pubescent beneath, 1-2 feet long and 2-3 inches wide (1).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR: Inflorescence is a long panicle, upto about 2 ft. long, erect or prostrate arising directly from the base of the pseudostem. Each panicle bears lateral racemes on which flowers bloom sequentially from base to tip. The number of panicles varies from 2-4 per tiller. Flowers are large, bisexual, attractive, self fertile, zygomorphic, 4-5 cm long and 2 cm across. Individual flowers are consists of tubular calyx and three pale green petals (11). Bracts 6-7, linear oblong, obtuse about 1.5 inches long; calyx 0.5 in. long, corolla tube white, shortly exserted, lobes 0.5 in. long; lip longer, white stripped with violet; capsule subtrigonous, about 0.4 in. long, striate (7). The androecium consists of a single fertile stamen, of the five others, one is completely absent, two modified into a labellum and the remaining two form staminodes. The filiform style protrudes through the groove between the anther lobes. Stigma is funnel shaped, ciliated and the surface is sticky. Fruits are small, trilocular many seeded capsules. The capsules are fusiform to ovoid in shape and pale green to deep green in colour. Mature seeds are hard, brownish black covered with thin mucilaginous aril inside the fruit wall (11).


Breeding system and varietal improvement:

Phenology:
The flowering period is during March-September with peak period during June-August. The mean number of days required from initiation of flower to opening of last flower in the panicle is 210. The mean number of days to reach full bloom from bud initiation is 34.5. Time taken for full maturity of a capsule from flower opening is 114 days. The flowers remain open for a period of 13-14 hours. Flower opening starts at 4.15 am and extends up to 9.00 am during winter. Maximum flower opening is during 5 to 6 am. Dehiscence of anthers occurred within half an hour of flower opening. The highest percentage of pollen germination occurred between 6 to 10 am. Stigmatic receptivity is maximum during 8 to 11 am (3). The flowers are often cross-pollinated but self-pollination is not uncommon. Young panicles require 6- months to complete the flowering and flowering to harvest stage of capsule is extended about 120-130 days depending on the climatic conditions. 

Pollinators: Cardamom flower is bisexual and is adapted to entomophilous type of pollination. They very structure of the flower, attractive nature of the petals, and the production of nectar in the epigynous glands are adaptations for accomplishing cross-pollination (11).
                                                                                                                                              
Role of honey bees
The chief pollinator of cardamom is honey bee. Two species namely Apis cerana indica and A. dorsata contribute to over 90% of pollination. Peak foraging is recorded between 9 am and 11 am depending upon environmental conditions. Three to five visits per flower is optimum for pollination. The flora in cardamom plantation comprises of 37 species, out of which 19 are trees. Shade trees such as Vernonia arborea, Ficus retusa and Cassia fistula are the major sources for nectar (3). A limited pollination occurred through ants and rainwater.

Pests :
Fifty five insects and three non-insects have been identified as pests of cardamom. Among these thrips, stem borers, root grubs, whiteflies and hairy caterpillars are more destructive than others. 
1. Cardamom thrips caused by - Sciothrips cardamomi
2. Leaf eating and Hairy caterpillars caused by - Alphaea biguttata, Euproctis sp.,

6. White flies caused by - Dialeurodes cardamomi

Dieases:
Nematode
Meloidogyne incognita, a non-insect pest on cardamom roots. The leaves become narrow and brittle and tillers show stunted growth due to shortening of internodes. Profuse branching of roots and rootlets with formation of galls nearer the rootcap region are the symptoms. 

Control: Drenching nursery beds with formalin (2%) for 72 hours before sowing of seeds and treating germinating beds with granular nematicides like carbofuran or aldicarb @ 200 g/bed. Neem oil @ 500 g/plant around plant base also protects the plants.

Aphid: P. nigronervosa f. caladii.
Natural enemies: Verticillium intertextum (fungus), Pullus sp., Coccinella transversalis, Ischiodon scutellaris, Paragus tibialis, Episyrphus viridaureus and an unidentified hemorobiid are the natural enemies of the aphid (4).
Major diseases of small cardamom
1. Azhukal or Capsule rot
A major disease causing rotting of tillers, tender shoots, capsules and leaves. Occurs all over Kerala during monsoon season. In Tamil Nadu the disease incidence is only in high rainfall areas such as the Anamalai hills. Caused by the soil-borne fungal pathogen, Phytophthora meadii, Pythium aphanidermatum, P. vexans de Bary & Rhizoctonia solani Kunn and P. nicotianae. The disease spreads through water (rain splashes) and air (wind). Overcrowding, thick shade and water logging are ideal conditions for the disease spread. Outbreak is during June-July.

Symptoms: Water soaked lesions appear first on the infected leaves, leading to rotting and shedding of leaves. The infected capsules become dull greenish brown and decay that emits a foul smell and subsequently shed. Infection spreads to panicles also.

Control: Thrashing and destruction of the infected parts should be done as phytosanitary measure just prior to the onset of South West monsoon. Remove the trash (dried leaves and sheaths) from the basal region of the plant to the extent possible. Spray the shoots with 1% Bordeaux mixture with adhesive (Rosin-soda or any other sticker) @ 3 litre/plant by the commencement of the monsoon and continue the spraying operation 2 or 3 times up to November-December according to the intensity of the disease and rainfall. Drenching soil with 0.2% copper oxychloride and application of bioagent Trichoderma sp. is also recommended. 

2. Clump rot or Rhizome rot
A serious disease in Kerala, Karnataka and high rainfall areas of Tamil Nadu such as Anamalai hills. The disease is caused by the soil-borne fungi Pythium aphanidermatum, P. vexans, Rhizoctonia solani and Fusarium oxysporum. The rhizomes and the tillers are the affected parts. 

Symptoms: Affected shoots become brittle and easily break off from the rhizome at the bulbous base.

Control: Same as that of ‘Azhukal’. Spraying fosetyl-Al (0.3%) or potassium phosphonate (0.3%) 2-3 times a year also controls the disease. Bioagents such as Trichoderma viride and T. harzianum controls the disease. Application of lime followed by ammonium phosphate or super phosphate @ 100g/clump during last week of May, checks the spread of the disease. 

3. Leaf blotch disease
Symptoms: caused by the fungus Phaeodactylium venkatesanum, characterised by the appearance of large blotches of irregular lesions with alternating shades of light and dark brown necrotic tissues, mainly observed on matured leaves. On lower surface of the lesions, pinkish white superficial growth of the fungus appear during the moist weather conditions.

Control: The fungicides, Ediphenphos (0.3%), Captofol (0.3%), Bordeaux mixture (1%), Mancozeb (0.3%) and Carbendazim (0.3%) are effective in controlling the disease.

4. ‘Chenthal’ disease
Even though Corynebacterium sp. has been isolated from the infected leaves, the pathogenicity of this organism could not be established. The incidence of the disease appears to be more severe in areas which do not have proper shade. 

Symptoms: Characterised by the appearance of rectangular linear reddish brown lesions mainly on lower surface of the leaves, which are clearly visible even on dried leaves. 

Control: Providing adequate shade is the only measure recommended. 
Waiting period of insecticide/fungicide
Quinalphos, Monocrotophos and Mancozeb - 30 days (6)

5. Viral diseases
a. ‘Katte’ or Mosaic (Cardamom mosaic virus) –transmitted by banana aphid, Pentalonia nigronervosa. The virus is flexous, rod shaped and belongs to the potato virus ‘Y’ group. 

Symptoms: Mild to severe mosaic, yellowing, chlorotic flecks, necrotic and ring spots and discontinuous stripes of light green colour running almost parallel to each other from the midrib to the margin of the leaves which form a mosaic pattern. On young shoots, such stripes are seen on the leaf sheath also. The infected clumps will be smaller in size with fewer tillers.

Control: Eradication of source of inoculum by destroying infected plants, alternate hosts once in two months and destruction of the vector by insecticide application. 

b). Nilgiri necrosis - caused by virus of Carla virus group. Mode of transmission not known.
Symptoms - leaves become narrow and brittle. Alternate white green and whitish to yellow streaks develop on the leaf blades. Size of leaves, pseudostems, panicles and capsules reduces, thereby reducing the yield drastically.

c). Stunted disease - caused by pepper yellow mottle virus (PYMV).

d). ‘Kokkekandu’ (hooked tiller) - caused by cardamom vein clearing virus (CdVCV) 
Transmitted by P. nigronervosa f. caladii. The disease is also known as ‘vein clearing disease’.
Symptoms: Clearing of veins, rosetting and shedding of leaves and formation of hooked tillers. 

Control: There is no control, except to prevent it by adapting phytosanitary measures and destroying the diseased plants and alternate host plants. 

6. Minor diseases
a. Leaf blotch - caused by Phaeodactylium alpiniae 
Symptoms: Discoloured necrotic brown patches on leaves. Affected portions dry and tear off.

Control: Hinosan (0.2 %) or Dithane M-45 (0.3 %).

b. Leaf blight - caused by P. meadii
Symptoms: Necrotic and greyish brown on blotches on leaves. Greyish coloured powdery masses of spores of pathogen at lower surface of leaves.

Control: Bordeaux mixture (1 %).

c. Leaf spots - caused by Sphaceloma cardamomii & Cercospora zingiberi
Symptoms: Small spherical scattered blotches of various sizes. Necrotic lesions with brown margins on leaves.

Control: Bordeaux mixture (1 %) or Dithane M-45 (0.3 %).

d. Capsule brown spot (Anthracnose) - caused by Colletotrichum gloeosporioides
Symptoms: Minute water soaked lesions on the capsules that turn reddish brown. 

Control: Dithane M-45 (0.3 %).

e. ‘Chenthal’ disease - caused by Corynebacterium sp.
Symptoms: Water soaked rectangular lesions of varying sizes on lamina. 

Control: Shade regulation and organic manure supply.

f. Capsule canker - caused by bacteria Xanthomonas sp.
Symptoms: Spongy discoloured eruptions on the capsule surface and a glazed appearance. 

Control: Spraying Mancozeb (0.25%), Carbendazim (0.25%) or Captafol (0.25%). 

Diseases in nursery
7. Damping off and seedling rot 
Occur in the primary and in the secondary nurseries. The diseases are caused by soil-borne fungi such as Pythium, Rhizoctonia, Fusarium etc.

Control: Drenching copper oxychloride (0.2%) and application of Trichoderma @ 2 kg/m2 (3).

Major pests of cardamom
1. Thrips
Thrips (Sciothrips cardamomi (ramk)- Thysanoptera), a serious pest of cardamom, suck and feed plant sap. Both nymphs and adults are harmful to the crop. They breed and multiply fast during summer. It lacerates and feeds on the exuding sap from the aerial parts.
Symptoms: Infestation on the panicle and the flower buds results in stunted growth of panicles, shedding of flower buds and warty growth on the surviving capsules. The infested capsules are light in weight, inferior in quality and fetch very low price in the market. 

Control: Removal of dry leaves and old plant parts during February/March. Spraying with Quinalphos 0.03%, Fenthion 0.03%, Phenthoate 0.03%, Phosalone 0.05%, Monocrotophos 0.03%, Fenitrothion 0.05%, Formothion 0.03%, Dimethoate 0.05% (EC formulation); Quinalphos 1.5%, Carbaryl 10%, Phosalone 4%, or Phenthoate 4% each at 25 kg/ha (Dust formulation) during August-November and again during December – April. 

2. Shoot borers
Shoot borers (Conogethes punctiferalis – Lepidoptera; Pyralidae) infest shoot, panicles and capsules by feeding internal contents.
Symptoms: Decay of central spindle and production of dead heart. Faecal matter of the caterpillar can be seen coming out through the holes. Infestation is high during peak periods of panicle elongation (January-March), immature capsule state (May-June) and tillering stage (September-October). At the time of flowering when the caterpillars attack the panicles and spikes it may lead to flower shedding and drying up of the attacked portions. At a later stage of the crop, the caterpillars bore into the capsules, feed on the seeds and make them hollow. 

Control: Spray insecticides during February, May and September with Quinalphos 0.025%, Carbaryl 0.1%, Monocrotophos, Fenthion or Dimethoate at 0.05% or Endosulphan/Phenthoate at 0.1%. 

3. Root grubs
Root grubs (Basilepta fulvicorne Jacoby – Cleoptera: Chrysomalidae) infest roots and rhizomes of cardamom seedlings. They are short, stout, pale white grubs of small, greenish blue beetles seen in the root zone of cardamom clumps. 
Symptoms: yellowing of leaves, which later results in the drying up and death of the plants. Infestation is at its peak during May-July and October-February. Beetles are abundantly seen during March-April and August-September. 

Control: Collect the beetle with hand nets or sticky traps at the time of mass emergence (March-April and August-September) and destroy them. Apply Phorate granules @ 20-4- g/clump or spray Chlorpyriphos 0.04% (200 ml/100 lit) during May-June & October-November. Beetles are susceptible to the fungus Beauveria bassiana and their grubs to another fungus Metarhizium anisopliae, and the nematode heterorhabditis sp.

4. Whiteflies 
Symptoms: Infestation of whiteflies (Dialeurodes cardamomi – Homoptera; Aleyrodidae) leads to yellowing and drying of leaves. 

Control: Install yellow sticky traps to attract and kill adults. Spray insecticides on under-surface of leaves 3 times at 10-15 days interval. The parasitoids Encarsia septentrionalis and E. dialeurodis and the predators Mallada bonninensis, mites etc can control. Aschersonia placenta, an entomopathogenic fungus also infects the nymph.

5. Rhizome weevil (Prodioctes haematicus)
Serious pest in the secondary nursery especially where seedings are raised continuously year after year. The grubs feed on rhizome and basal portion of stem.
Symptoms: Drying of leaves and breaking of stem at the base. 

Control: Drench the nursery beds with chlorpyrifos at 0.04%.

6. Shoot fly (Formosina flavipes)
Observed in the nursery during the period from January to May. 
Symptoms: Dead heart or decay of the central spindle. 

Control: Spraying of Quinalphos 0.025% or applying Phorate granules 1 g/a.i./sq.m.

7. Leaf eating and Hairy caterpillars (Alphaea biguttata, Euproctis sp., Eupterote spp., Pericallia sp.)
There are 10 species, of which 7 are hairy and appear in large numbers during certain seasons causing extensive defoliation. 

Control: Mechanical collection and destruction of larvae and spraying of any contact insecticide are recommended. Install light trap during July. Caterpillars are susceptible to insect parasite Apanteles glomeratus and to the fungal pathogen Beauveria sp.

8. Cardamom scale (Mytilaspis sp.) 
Found on the lower surface of leaves, leaf sheath, panicles and fruit stalk. 
Symptoms: Capsules get shrivelled, panicles become dry and leaves become yellow. The pest is mostly seen during summer months.

Control: Spray Monocrotophos or Fenthion @ 0.05% during the peak season.

A few pests of minor importance are shoot flies, scale insects, tingid bugs, leaf rollers, mites and nematodes (6).

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Cardamom oil is used in food, perfumery, liqueur and pharmaceutical industries as a flavour and a carminative. Its use in the food industry is in flavouring pickles, meat and canned soups. However, the oil is reported to develop some off flavour in a few days when it contacts with air; its use is therefore restricted to fresh meat products and foods with short shelf-life. Increasing use of cardamom oil is reported in compounded flavours for baked goods, sauces and condiments. Cardamom oil is reported to be gaining increasing use in perfumery, with a trend to spicy tones modifying the dominant lavender group perfumes for women (ITC, Markets for Essential Oils and Oleoresins 1974).

Cardamom is used as an adjuvant to carminative drugs. It is officially recognised in British and US pharmacopoeias and used as an aromatic stimulant, carminative and flavouring agent. It can be used to ease cigarette addiction. Eating a few seeds of cardamom can safely be recommended to initially minimise the number if cigarettes being smoked, and slowly the smoker may give up the chronic addiction to chain smoking (2).

Parts Used: Dried mature fruits and seeds.


Harvesting Practices: 1. Vegetative propagation: By means of rhizome division. Rhizomes from large clumps of plants are dug up and separated into smaller clumps each of which contain atleast two ‘eyes’, one old and one young shoot. The rhizomes from 18-24 months old plants make the best propagating material. The vegetative propagation is simple, reliable, less costly, produces a crop within 2 years as against 3-4 years with seeds. 

2. Seedling propagation: Seeds for sowing are collected from mature, healthy, vigorous, disease-free, high yielding types producing large fruits on compact panicles which are preferably more than 5 years old. Then they are opened and the seeds inside are washed with water to remove the adhering mucilaginous matter. Seeds are then sown within 24 hours of harvesting. Compact, healthy fruits, which have attained full maturity, are air-dried, separated and soaked in water for 2 hours prior to sowing either in a nursery bed or a seed box of fine textured rich soil. Seed beds are prepared with 1 m width, 6 m length and 20 cm height. Fumigation is beneficial before sowing seeds for eliminating nematodes. 

Weed control: Weed growth must be controlled during the first year as with all perennial plants. In most areas of the world the soil around the crop is weeded atleast 2-3 times a year until the plant is large enough to resist any competitive weed growth.

Agrotechniques
Standardization of nursery practices
 Seed capsules collected in the first round after fly picking are the best for seed extraction. Seeds should be sown in October or latest by early November under Kerala conditions. However, when seedlings are grown in nursery beds for nearly 20 months, seeds from second and third harvest could also be used.
 Sowing of seeds should be done immediately after harvest of seed capsules as storage reduces viability. Germination is reduced by 20% in 15 days and seeds lose viability in about 5 months. 
 Seeds can be sown closely in rows 10 cm apart. 2 cm of mulch can be given over the bed and sufficiently watered. 
 Scarification using conc. sulphuric acid hastens germination. Seeds are washed in clean water and soaked overnight before sowing.
 Germination commences in 20-25 days after sowing for scarified seeds and 3 months for non-scarified seeds. 
 Sowing in September is best for good germination. Sowing in winter and peak monsoon should be avoided.
 Dibbling of two seeds per hill at 15 x 10 cm spacing and later thinning to one healthy seedling helps uniform establishment in the primary nursery. Hence, seed requirement is reduced to one half.
 Seedlings of three to four leaf stage should be transplanted to the secondary bed nursery at a distance of 20 to 25 cm. Overhead pandal be erected to provide about 55% light to protect seedlings from direct sunlight.
 Raising of seedlings in polybags instead of secondary nursery has many advantages like uniform growth, more tillering and better establishment in the main field. The bags should be filled with a mixture of Jungle top soil, cowdung and sand (3:1:1).
 Soil mixed with vermicompost is the best medium for filling polybags. Spraying biostimulants such as Vipul (1%), Ergostim (300 ppm) and Maleic hydrazide (250 ppm) enhances seedling vigour in polybags.
 Fertilizer should be applied for the bed nursery once seedling starts growing. Either 250 g of 17:17:17 complex or a mixture of amophos (200 g) and muriate of potash (75 g) can be applied for a bed of 6 m2. If the seedlings are not healthy, application of 100 g urea and 200 g muriate of potash may be made during April-May. Alternatively diammonium phosphate and muriate of potash (2 kg of each fertilizer dissolved in100 l water) may be sprayed on both the sides of the leaves during April-May. Approximately 2.5 l spray volume will be required for 100 plants (3).

Field preparation 
About 10-15 cm depth topsoil should be removed before making terraces. The pit size can be 60x60x45 cm and should be prepared before commencement of monsoon. About half of the pit should be filled with topsoil alone and remaining upper half with 3:1 mixture of organic manure and top soil.

Spacing
Plant to plant distance can be 3 m x 2.5 m in high rainfall areas or irrigated conditions and 2.0 m x 2.0 m or 2.0 m x 1.0 m in low rainfall areas. In Kerala spacing adopted is more while in Karnataka it spacing is closer. 

Planting 
The selected plantlets are planted the already prepared pits and should be supported by stakes from falling due to wind. Immediately after planting, the base should be mulched for preventing soil erosion and moisture conservation. Planting can be done with the commencement of south west monsoon and before the heavy rains commence (11).  

Shade management 
In general 40-50 percent light intensity under rainfed and about 55-60 percent light intensity under irrigated conditions is required. May-June is the ideal season for shade regulation. In newly established plantations, where shade is inadequate, fast growing species of Erythrina may be planted for immediate shade along with other shade trees. Some of the shade trees found suitable for cardamom are Vernonia arborea (Karuna), Cedrella toona (Chandana vayambu), Acrocarpus fraxinifolius (Korangatti), Cassia fistula (Kanikonna), Dysoxylum malabaricum (Vellakil), Terminalia tomentosa (Thempavu), Hopea parviflora (Thambakam) etc (3).
Mixed cropping systems
Cardamom offers great scope as a mixed crop with coffee. Arecanut and coconut in high ranges can also provide adequate shade and so cardamom can be introduced in these plantations. 

Aftercare
During the initial stage of growth, sickle weeding with light forking around plant followed by mulching in May, September and January enhance tiller and panicle production. Application of paraquat @ 0.4 kg ai/ha or glyphosate @ 0.8 kg ai/ha controls weed growth and the cost is less by 50% compared to manual weeding. Removal of yielded old suckers at a height of about 15 cm from ground level soon after completion of final harvest is beneficial for production of more tillers and higher yield. Besides creating favourable environment like allowing more light penetration, better utilization of nutrients and moisture, this method also reduces pest load. Judicious irrigation during peak dry months (February to April) ensures increase in yield. For maximum efficiency, fertilizers are to be applied in a circular band of 15 cm width leaving a distance of 30 cm from the plant base. Foliar application of two rounds of zinc sulphate in May and September (250 g in 100 l water) increases yield by 25% mainly by reducing premature capsule shedding and greater capsule size. Application of boron as borax as a foliar spray @ 0.4% (400 g in 100 l water) in Kerala region and 0.3% (300 g in 100 l water) in Karnataka region enhances yield by more than 30%. In Tamil Nadu region, soil application of borax (7.5 kg/ha) increases the yield by more than 20%. Soil application of molybdenum as ammonium molybdate @ 0.5 kg/ha in Karnataka region improves the yield by more than 48%. Incorporation of VAM (Vesicular Arbuscular Mycorrhizae) at the seedling stage boosts up the growth and vigour of the crop (3).  

Production 
Harvesting: The peak period of harvest is October-November. The average yield of cardamom is around 150 kg (dry)/ha; however, record yield of 695 kg/ha (average of 9 crop seasons) was obtained by adopting high production technology directly in farmers’ plantations (Korikanthimath 1995). Just ripened fruits or physiologically ripened fruits are generally harvested. In overmatured capsules more splitting is observed (Korikanthimath and Naidu 1986). Percentage of dry recovery is highest (29%) in the fully ripened capsules followed by the one harvested at physiologically maturity (24%) and in immature stage (14%). Capsules are washed to remove the adhering soil and a treatment with 2% washing soda (alkali) for 10 minutes enables retention of the green colour and prevents growth of mould.

Curing: In curing, moisture of green cardamom is reduced from 80% to 12% (wet basis) at an optimum temperature (50°C) so as to retain its green colour to the maximum extent.
1. Natural (sun) drying: This requires 5-6 days and the green colour is bleached by this method.
2. Electrical drier: Fifty kg capsules can be dried in 10-12 hrs at 45-50°C retaining the green colour.
3. Flue pipe curing: This is the convenient method for obtaining high quality green cardamom. Drying takes about 18 to 22 hours (Korikanthimath 1993).

Produce and Products: Bleached cardamom: Bleached cardamom is creamy white or golden yellow in colour, done with either freshly harvested or dry capsules. It is prepared using sulphur dioxide, potassium metabisulphite (25% containing 1% HCl for 30 minutes) and hydrogen peroxide (4-6% at pH 4.0). However, bleached cardamom tends to lose more volatile oil.

Ground cardamom: Proper maturity reflected by deep brown, shining seeds with satisfactory weight per litre specifications and good characteristic aroma are the factors necessary for making good quality ground cardamom (2).  

Cardamom volatile oil: The most functionally important constituent of cardamom. The volatile oil content of seeds vary from 6.6 to 10.6% for two types of cardamom (cv. Mysore and Malabar) grown in India (Krishnamurthy 1964; Krishnamurthy et al. 1967 and Korikanthimath et al. 1999). In immature capsules the volatile oil content is low, in the order of 4-5%. The husk yields 0.2%, having properties similar to those of seed oil (Rao et al. 1925). Yields of the volatile oil varied over a wide range: for seeds 3.4 to 8.6% and for dried capsules 5.2 to 11.3% (2).  

Value added products
Encapsulation of cardamom flavour, cardamom cola, a fizzy drink, a ‘flan’ mix, cardamom tea, cardamom coffee, dry powder for cardamom soft drink mix and instant pongal mix flavoured with cardamom are some of the products brought out by Central Food Technological Research Institute (CFTRI).


Chemistry: The chemicals present in cardamom are 1,8-cineole, d-α-terphenol, terpinyl acetate, limonene, sabinene and borneol (Guenther 1975). 1,8-cineole and α-terpinyl acetate are the major compounds in the cardamom volatile oil. Besides the usual terpene hydrocarbon and alcohols as minor compounds and the dominance of 1,8-cineole and α- terpinyl acetate, methyl eugenol has also been identified (Lawerence 1979). The percentage of main components given by Lawerence is as follows:
α-pinene - 1.5,β-pinene - 0.2, sabinene - 2.8, myrcene - 1.6, α-phellandrene - 0.2, limonene - 11.6, 1,8-cineole - 36.3, γ-terpinene - 0.7, p-cymene - 0.1, terpinolene - 0.5, linalool - 3.0, linalyl acetate - 2.5, terpinen-4-ol - 0.9, α-terpineol - 2.6, α-terpinyl acetate - 31.3, citronellol - 0.3, nerd - 0.5, geraniol - 0.5, methyl eugenol - 0.2, trans-nerolidol - 2.7.

The volatile oil has characteristic aroma, described generally as comphory, sweet, aromatic and spicy. It has few mono- or sesquiterpenic hydrocarbons and is predominantly made up of oxygenated compounds, all of which are potential aroma compounds. While many of the identified compounds – alcohol, esters and aldehydes – are commonly found in many spice oils, the dominance of the ether, 1,8-cineole and the esters, α-terpinyl and linalyl acetates in the composition, make the cardamom volatiles a unique combination. The aroma differences in different sources of cardamom are attributed to the proportion of the esters and 1,8-cineole (Wijesekera and Jayawardena 1973; Korikanthimath et al. 1997) (2).  

Quality standards and grade specifications: The dried cardamom require cleaning to remove all stalks and dried remains of floral parts. This is be done by rubbing dried cardamom over a coarse surface of wire-mesh or bamboo trays. With regard to the quality of the dried product, larger, round and uniform pods having a good dark green colour always fetch the highest price. The grading is based on the Govt. of India and the Indian Standards Institution (ISI) – the ‘Agmark’ grades (2).

Fertilizer requirement
The most optimum time is during May and September. Under rainfed conditions, fertilizer is applied in two split doses, the first helps in the production of suckers and development of capsules and second, at initiation of panicles and suckers (5). Application of NPK @ 150:75:350 kg/ha is optimum for obtaining higher yields under the traditional method (pit) of planting. A dosage of 120:120:240 kg/ha of NPK is recommended for trench method and 75:75:150 kg/ha for normal spacing (2 m x 2 m). Application of neem cake or vermicompost @ 2.5 t/ha gives about 150% increase in yield. Overhead (sprinkler) irrigation is ideal for cardamom. It could be provided at an interval of 12-15 days (4).

Area and Production
As per 2000-2001 estimates the total area under the crop in India is 53654 ha spread over Kerala (53%), Karnataka (40%) and Tamil Nadu (7%). The world production of the crop is around 20,000 MT. Of late Guatemala has emerged as a keen competitor to Indian cardamom in the international spice market and presently is the prime producer and exporter of cardamom (11). The production in India is 9390t (1999-2000), the productivity is 174 kg/ha and production is 1100 MT.

Miscellaneous Information

 Cytology
Cytologically cardamom is classified as a tetraploid with chromosome number 2n=48 (Sudharshan, 1989) and basic chromosome number x=12 (Darlington and Wylie, 1955) (11).

Cultivars and varieties
About 8 species occur in the Malaysia-Indonesia regions, only E. cardamom occur in India. The closest relative is E. ensal, the Sri Lankan wild cardamom. Two botanical varieties based on the size of the fruits are recognised. They are: a) E. cardamomum var. Major Thwaites comprising the ‘wild’ indigenous cardamom of the Ceylon or Greater oblong cardamom or long cardamom and b) E. cardamomum var. Minor Walt (syn. var. minuscule Burkill) comprising all the cultivated races, particularly those included under the names Malabar, Mysore and Vazhuka cardamoms. Var. Major is now recognised as a separate species Elettaria major Thw., which is the wild cardamom of Sri Lanka. The genus Elettaria is represented by a single species in India. In the Malaysian and Indonesian region there is richer variability for the genus and about 8 species have been reported (1).
Three important types of cardamom cv. Mysore, cv. Malabar and cv. Vazhuka are recognised primarily on the nature of panicles. cv. Mysore has prostrate panicle, cv. Malabar possesses erect panicle and cv. Vazhuka, a natural hybrid between the two has a semi erect (flexuous) panicle (Sastri, 1952; Mahdusoodanan et al., 1993) (11).

Cultivar Mysore – a hardy, vigorous plant, which is found growing to a height of 14-18 feet, possesses glabrous leaves which are 1.0-2.75 feet long x 2.0-6.5 inches wide on pronounced 2 inches long petioles. This cultivar has erect panicles which often reach up to 4.5 feet in length. Flowers are produced in 6-40 short racemes which are 0.5-4.5 inches long, capsules are fusiform, oblong, 0.5-0.8 inches long by 0.3-0.45 inches wide. Each cell contains 3-6 angular transversely wrinkled brown-black seeds. The seeds of this type found to be larger and slightly flatter than other types. 
 More susceptible to the mosaic disease.
 Less susceptible to the thrips damage. 
 7-10 years economic life. 
 25,000 ripe seeds per pound.

Cultivar Malabar – a sensitive, not too vigorous plant which rarely exceeds 10 feet in height, possesses either glabrous or softly pubescent leaves which are 1.0-2.5 feet long by 1.75-4.75 inches wide, on short petioles. This cultivar has flexuous, decumbent panicles which often reach 2 feet in length the flowers are produced in 2-12 racemes which are 0.5-3.5 inches long. The capsules are sub-globose to sub-trigonous, indehiscent, 0.5- 0.7 inches long and 0.3-0.5 inches wide. Each cell contains 3-8 oblong to ovoid seeds. 
 Less susceptible to the mosaic disease.
 More susceptible to the thrips damage. 
 10-15 years economic life. 
 10,000 ripe seeds per pound. 
 More adapted to lower elevations.
 Tolerate water stress to a longer period

Related taxa 
Elettaria cardamomum Maton has close relationships with allied genera viz. Aframomum K. Schum and Amomum Roxb. of the same family (Pruthi, 1991). Aframomum melegueta (Rosc.) K. Schum (syn. Amomum melegueta Rosc.) is the meleguete pepper, also known as the grains of paradise or Guinea grains. It is a West African plant used as a spice and stimulating carminative. It is a perennial herb growing to a height of 1.8 m with short rhizomes from which leafy shoots and short inflorescence arise. The narrow leaves are 18-22 cm long and 1.8 to 2.5 cm broad; mostly glabrous, glossy green with acuminate apex. The inflorescences are borne at the base of the leafy shoots on very short peduncles with reddish bracts. The fruits are ovoid or pear shaped with 5-10 cm long, red or orange colour. Substitutes for cardamom from the same genus are A. granumparadisi (L.) K. Schum, A. danielli K. Schum (syn. A. hanburyi K. Schum), A. korarima Pereira and A. angustifolium (Somn.) K. Schum.

Amomum subulatum Roxb. is called the greater Indian cardamom or Nepal cardamom or Large cardamom. Phenotypcially the plant resembles small cardamom except in the nature of inflorescence and the colour of the tiller and fruit. In Amomum inflorescence is a dense, short, globose spike with short peduncle borne near the ground from rhizome. The fruit is 2.5 cm long, triangular, deep red when ripe and echinated. It is a native species of Eastern Himalayas (Rao et al., 1993b). Cardamom substitutes belonging to the genus Amomum, which are in use, include A. aromaticum Roxb. The Bengal cardamom, A. kepulaga Sprague and Burkill (syn. A. maximum Roxb.); the round cardamom of Java; A. dealbatum Roxb. (syn. A. maximum Roxb.) also from Java; A. krervah Pierre, Cambodian cardamom; A. xanthoides Wallich, Bastard cardamom and A. compactum Soland, round cardamom of Malaysia (Arora, 1985) (11).
For details on Amomum see section on Large cardamom (Amomum subulatum Roxb.)

 The ancient Egyptians chewed cardamom to whiten their teeth and simultaneously sweeten their breath.

 Used in Saudi Arabia for preparing traditional coffee – cardamom drink called ‘Gahwa’, which is given to visitors as a symbol of Arab hospitality and as an inducement to tranquil thoughts. It is a tradition that the ritual of coffee is not disturbed by discussion of business terms, and so negotiations are not entered into until the coffee has been sipped and enjoyed in peace. Gahwa is made from roasted green coffee beans, crushed with brass pestle and mortar and put into a small brass coffee maker with hot water, broken cardamom pods, cardamom seeds, sugar and a pinch of ground cloves. The coffee is then boiled for 2-3 minutes, strained and served ‘black’ in delicate little cups (9).
