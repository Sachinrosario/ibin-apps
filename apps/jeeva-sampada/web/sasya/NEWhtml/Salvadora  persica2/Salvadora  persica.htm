HABIT & HABITAT
A large, much-branched, evergreen shrub or a tree much resembling S. oleoldes, found in the dry and arid regions of India and on saline lands and in coastal regions just above the high-water mark. Also found in drier parts of India, from Punjab & Sind to Patna, and extending Southward to the Konkan, the Circars & North Ceylon.

Availability :  
It can be found in dry wastelands, Gardens and Roadsides, Midlands, along Rivers and Streams, open places, Owk forest, Road sides, Saline land, salt marshes, dry places, Sea shore, Villages, waste land.
BioticZone: Deccan and carnatic, Deccan Plateau, Kanara and Konkan.
Soil:Saline soil and sandy loam.
Misc: Abu Road, Amer, Antapur Reserve Forest, Balsar, Baran, Benkal Forest Plantation, Circars, Fatehpur Sikri, Ghumli, on Bardahills from Bordevion Girnar & Sasangir, Gunthgola, Jodiya, Kadlimatti, Kileshwar on Barda hills, Loharia, Loharia forest, Sasan & Kinkai in Gir forests, Shivbari, Varacunda and Yerramalais.
Country: Abyssinia, Asia, Ceylon, Egypt, India and West Asia.
Zone: Central, North, South and Westzone.
State: Andhra Pradesh, Goa Daman Diu, Gujarat, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Punjab, Rajasthan, Tamil Nadu and Uttar Pradesh.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Bark dull grey or grey-white,deeply cracked; leaves variable in shape, el-liptic or ovate-lanceolate, somewhat fleshv; flowers pedicellatc, greenish white or greenish yellow in lax panicles - drupes globose or round, smooth, red when ripe. The wood is white & soft. Weight about 46 lb per cubic foot.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES
The tree is attacked by defoliating larvae of several beetles. 
A number of fungi, such as Cercospora udatpu., Placosoma salvadorae and Septogloeum salvadorae  have been reported for the first time, damaging the leaves.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used: 
Leaves, Fruits, Seeds, root bark.

Uses

  1. Leaves are eaten as a vegetable in eastern tropical Africa and are used in the preparation of a sauce.
  2. Tender shoots and leaves are eaten as salad. 
  3. They are also used as camel fodder. Fruits are sweet, edible, deobstruent, carminative and diuretic. Also purgative.
  4. A fermented drink is reported to be made from the fruits.

Chemistry
A sample of seeds from Lahore (Pakistan) yielded a pale yellow, solid fat (39-3% yield) having the following characteristics: acid val., 2-2: sap. equiv., 243-1; iod. val., 6-1; and un- sapon. matter. 0-8%. Its fatty acid composition was as follows : capric, 1-0; lauric, 19-6; myrisdc, 54-5; palmitic, 19.5; and oleic, 5-4%. Air-dried root bark yields 27.1 per cent of ash, containing considerable amounts of salts, mostly as chlo-rides. It is also reported to contain a fairly large amount of alkaloidal constituents (including trimethylamine and an unidentified alkaloid), small amounts of resin and colouring matter, and traces of tannins and saponin.

Other Uses
  1. Fresh root bark is used as a vesicant and is employed as an ingredient of snuff. 
  2. A paste of the roots is applied as a substitute of mustard plaster, and their decoction is used against gonorrhoea and vesicle catarrh. 
  3. The extract of the root is said to relieve the pain due to spleen troubles. 
  4. A decoction of the bark is used as a tonic and stimulant in low fevers and as an emmenagogue. 
  5. Stem bark is used as an ascarifugc and for gastric troubles. 
  6. Leaves possess antiscorbutic and astringent properties. A decoction of the leaves is used in asthma and cough, and a poultice made out of them is applied to painful tumours and piles. They are also used as an external ap plication in rheumatism. Dried leaves in small doses are given with copious amount of water for treatment of flatulent dyspepsia. Fruits possess deobstruent, carmi- native, diuretic, lithontriptic, and stomachic properties, and are used in biliousness and rheumatism. 
  7. Seeds have a bitter, sharp taste. They are considered purgative, diu retic ,and tonic. Seed oil is applied on the skin in rheu- matism. Seeds of S. persica are similar to those of S. oleoides in size and colour and yield a pale yellow, solid fat. Seed fats of these two species are almost identical in compo sition and arc used for similar purposes.   8. The tree is suitable for growing in shelter-belts and as wind-breaks for protecting the farm habitations, gardens, orchards, etc., Wood (wt., 609-721 kg./cu.m.) is soft, white, easy to work and takes good polish. It is not liable to be attacked by termites, and is used by the Egyptians for coffins. Like S. oleoides, it is also not a good fuel.
