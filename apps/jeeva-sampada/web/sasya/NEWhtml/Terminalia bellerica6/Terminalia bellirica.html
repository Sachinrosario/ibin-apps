 Other Biological Details
HABIT & HABITAT
A handsome tree found in deciduous forests throug out the greater part of India, but not in the arid regions; usually scattered in moist valleys. Native to Indian , Bangladesh, Burma, Sri Lanka, Thailand and Malaysia.

Found in deciduous forests throughout the greater part of India, but not in the arid regions.

It grows on a wide range of soils provided moisture in the soils is not deficient.  Best growth is seed in deep, moist, sandy loam with good subsoil drainage.  It grows well up to an altitude of 1000m and in areas with rainfall ranging from 900 to 3000mm.  This species can tolerate temperature up to 46°C.  This tree is a light demander and should be planted at sites receiving maximum sunlight.  It is fairly drought resistant but very frost sensitive.


Availability :  
The species is found practically all over the tropical India, Nepal and Bangladesh except in very moist and the desert areas.  It is a common associate for both teak and Shorea robusta and is also found in mixed hardwood forests.  It is also distributed in Malaysia and Sri Lanka.

It is found throughout the forests of India, Burma and Ceylon, below elevations of about 3000 feet, but not in the very arid regions.  

These species is distributed in the Indo-Malesian region, Srilanka and Indo-china.  Within India it is found throughout the plains and in the lower hills in Arunachal Pradesh and in the Western Ghats of Karnataka and Tamil Nadu.

Status : Common  in mixed deciduous forests, waste lands and as avenue tree.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A medium-sized to large tree up to 40 m tall, with a straight stem, sometimes buttressed when large. Bark bluish-gray with many fine vertical cracks; young branches and young leaves appressed,  pubescent. Leaves simple, clustered or sometimes spirally arranged or whorled at the ends of branches, coriaeous, broadly elliptic to ellipti-obovate, 10-20 cm long and 4-15 cm wide, broadly rounded to emarginate at base, margins entire, main nerves 6-8 pairs spreading, prominent, petiole 2.5-10 cm long, without gland at apex, flowers pale greenish-yellow  or creamy white with an offensive odour, borne on solitary, simple axillary spike, 7-11 cm long; those in the upper part of the spike are male and very shortly pedicellate, those in the lower part hermaphrodite, sessile. Bract linear, early caducous, calyx pubescent outside, woolly inside with long brown hairs, teeth broadly triangular, acute. Fruits broadly ellipsoid to subglobose, 2.5-3 cm long and 1.5-2.5 cm in diameter, gray, obscurely longitudinally 5-6 ridged. Densely fine yellow to brown –pubescent.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Breeding System :

Phenology :
Flowers from March to May in Peninsular India.  Fruits mature between January  and April in the following year. In Central Indian deciduous forest, trees remain  leafless from February to March or April to May.
Pollinators:

Seed Biology :

Mature fruits are collected from March to April under the trees.  Such fruits are dried in the sun and stored properly.

Seed has hard thick walled coat which inhibits germination.  The seed coat can be removed mechanically by hitting with wooden mallet.  In order to induce the germination, the fruits are soaked in water for about a week.
Seedling Germination :

Fruits are collected from healthy trees, depulped and seeds are dried in sun and stored.  Seeds are viable for a year but their germination percent will be reduced to 40 to 50 percent.  

Seedling Germination :

Seeds are soaked in water for 24hrs for better germination.  Fermentation or cow dung slurry treatments for two weeks or more until the seeds show sprouting is also recommended.  As a pretreatment, immersion in hot water(80 to 100°C), and then allowing it to cool followed by soaking for 24 hrs is also employed.  Seeds are sown in March-April in nursery or in June-July in the field, directly.  In nursery sowing, soaked seeds are sown in lines spaced at 20cm and 5cm apart within lines.  For direct sowing, seeds are dibbled at 1m apart and covered with soil about 1 to 1.5cm deep.  Germination takes about 10 to 40 days and 65 to 70 percent seeds germinate.  When seedlings are 3 to 5m tall they are shifted to polybags. Seedlings may be transplanted in the second rainy season.  The rate of growth is fairly fast.

Vegetative propagation is through stumps: The plant can be propagated through stump planting.  For this purpose, 12 to 15 months old plants provide satisfactory stumps.  They can be planted in pits of crow-bar holes after the start of the rains.

Direct sowing of Fruits:  Treated fruits are sown on raised bed.  Sometimes fruits are sown directly in sunken beds and water is allowed to stand for 7 days.  After 7 days the water is let out and only watering is done every day.  This type of treatment makes seed coat soft and thus early germination is induced.  Germination starts after 25 days and continues till 80-85days and 70-80% germination is observed.  After 60days of germination or before the roots strike deep seedlings are carefully removed with a ball of earth and planted in polybags.


Cultivation:

The seedlings raised in polybags are planted in pits.  Initially they need shade but later on overhead light is preferred.  Good growth is observed where seedlings are planted in moist areas.

Transplanting in case of nursery raised seedlings or saplings is done in June-July when the plants are about 3to 4 months old.  About 52 percent plants successfully establish.  Regular weeding and watering is necessary.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Wood, kernel, leaves, seeds, bark

Uses

  • The dried fruit of this species forms one of the myro-balans of commerce used in  medicine, inferior dyeing and tanning of cloth and tanning leather.  Native ink is made from it and is also used in medicines.
  • As a dye, the myro-balans are used to give yellowish or brownish-yellow colour to the cloth, or with various other dye-stuffs, to produce dark brown and black.
  • Bark is used as a diuretic and the gum as a demulcent and purgative known commercially as belleric myrobalan. The fruit pulp is also used for tanning and the seed oil for soap manufacturing.
  • Whole fruit yields about 12% tannin and 24% soluble non tan on zero moisture basis.  The tannin is found mainly in the pericarp of the flesh.  Its tannin content is low and is a cheap substitute for chebulic myrobalan.

Chemistry

Gum is obtained from the wounds in the bark in large quantities.  It much resembles Gum Arabic, is perfectly soluble in water and burns away in flame of a candle, with little smell, into black gritty ashes. The gum is in vermicular pieces, about the thickness of a finger, of the colour of inferior Gum arabic.  Hardly at all soluble in water in which it swells up and forms a bulky gelatinous mass; its taste is insipid.

Fruits contain 1.25% tannic acid,a green fixed oil, saponin, tannins, a resitious residue and three amorphous and hygroscopic glycosidal compounds. A non-nitrogenous crystalline substance isolated from the fruit pulp which is identical with phyllembin (ethyl gailate) an active principle isolated from Eniblica officinalis (Hindiamla).

Other Uses

  1. Wood - Building, rafters, board, packing cases, plough, coffee boxes, heavy packing, plywood, and in making paper (writing, printing and wrapping ).

  2. Kernel - non-edible oil from the kernel is used in the  manufacture of soaps.
  3. Ripe Fruit - Astringent, used to treat dropsy, piles, diarrhoea and leprosy. Extract of the fruit is antibacterial. Prefered by Monkeys, deer, squirrels, pigs, goats and other animals greately favour this fruit and few are allowed to remain long on the ground.
  4. Seeds - Adhesives, contain proteins, ink-making
  5.  In Ayurvedic medicine, the fruit is considered to be a laxative and anthelmintic, and used to treat bronchitis, sore throat, biliousness, inflammations , strangury, asthma and diseases of the eye, nose, heart and bladder.
  6. The ripe fruit usually in combination with Chebulic myrobalan (from the fruit of Terminalia chebula ) and those of Phyllanthus emblica are used to  form Triphala (three fruits) a well-known Ayurvedic drug prescribed for a variety of diseases.
  7. Oil obtained from the fruits is used as a  hair oil and as an external application for rheumatism. In southern Orissa, the ash of the burnt fruits is taken  with rock salt to treat diarrhea.
  8. Seeds kernel possess narcotic properties. Seeds are used to treat guinea-worm infestation, leucorrhoea, veneral diseases, hypertension, pox and dyspnoea.
  9. Bark is used as a diuretic and the gum as a demulcent and purgative known commercially as belleric myrobalan. The fruit pulp is also used for tanning and the seed oil for soap manufacturing.

 Miscellaneous Information

This tree, called Vibhitaki in Sanskrit, meaning fearless, was avoided by the Hindus of Northern India, who would not sit in its shade as it is supposed to be inhabited by demons. As long as the influence of Arabian medicine prevailed, it was used medicinally in Europe, having been introduced by the Arabs from India.
