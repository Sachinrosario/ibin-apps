HABIT & HABITAT
Erect, much branched, perennial, strongly aromatic herbs, 0.1-1 m high, hairy found in Throughtout  tropical & hotter parts of India. Common weed near cultivated fields. Often planted in gardens, nurseries and sometimes cultivated.
Availability :  Plenty, Throughout India

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:
Erect, much branched, perennial, strongly aromatic herbs, 0.1-1 m high, hairy. Stem and branches 4-angled hairy at nodes. Leaves ovate-lanceolate or elliptic, 3-8 x 0.8-3.5 cm, acute or sub-acuminate at apex, cuneate or tapering at base, entire or serrate along margin, glabrous on both surfaces; petiole 1-2 cm long, ciliate. Flowers bluish-white or pale purple, in dense whorls on close racemes or panicles; bracts stalked, oblanceolate, acuminate, ciliate; pedicels 3 mm long. Calyx lobes 5; upper lip 3 mm long; lower lip 4 mm long, glabrous outside and pubescent inside. Corolla 2-lipped, white or creamish, 8-12 x 1.5 mm; tube inflated below; lobes 5. Stamens 4. Nutlets oblong to ellipsoid, 2.5 mm long, pitted, black.
REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:   
Floral and seed  biology:
Inflorescence  long terminal racemose ; Flower  small white flowers in clusters, two-lipped, pedicels densly hairy; Calyx  is 0.4 cm long at first afterwards 0.5-0.6 cm long, hairy outside, glabrous inside, at the base with dense whorls of hairs above  it glandulose, upper tip ovate to rounded,0.3 cm long, lower one 4-dentate ; Corolla   white or pale purple tube 0.6 cm long Androecium  stamens exerted, posterior filaments short, white hairy transverse processes; Gynoecium bicarpellary, Syncarpous, ovary superior, deeply 4-lobed,bilocular, style gynobasic, stigma bifid ; Fruit  nutlets oblong, ellipsoid, dark- brown or black,0.15-0.2 cm long, pitted ;  4 seeds in each nutlets ; 

Mode of pollination and seed dispersal: 
Plants are pollinated by insects and persistent calyx help
Genetic variability:
 Plant is tetraploid with (2n=48) with basic chromosome number x=12. However, chromosome no. 2n=26 to 28 are also reported and also it has been suggested that euploidy might have played an important role in speciation of this genus.

AGRO TECHNOLOGY:

Propagation:  Plant is propagated through seeds.

Soil type : Medium and light loam soils, with good water holding capacity   with pH of 4.3-8.3 are suitable for growing sweet basil.

Nutrient requirements: In the soil of average fertility 40kg/ha of nitrogen 40kg/ha phosphorous and 40kg/ha, potassium is   applied as basal dose at the time of transplanting.40kg/ha   nitrogen is applied in 2 split doses during the growing season.

Water management: After transplanting field should be irrigated once a month, especially after the end of monsoon

Harvesting period / Method: Plant is harvested when the crop is in full bloom and the lower leaves start turning yellow.

Economic Potential and Uses
UTILIZATION:
Uses:    The whole plant is pungent and has got stomachic, anthelmintic, alexipharmic, antipyretic properties. It improves taste and used in heart and blood  disease, leucoderma (Ayurveda). It acts as diuretic, emenagogue and used in heart and brain  disease, chronic pains in  joints, asthma, inflammations. Juice is used for toothache, earache, headache. The juice with camphor stops nasal haemorrhage (Yunani) (Kirtikar & Basu, 1933).
NBPGR:
Flower - carminative, diuretic, stimulant, demulcent, seed - antigonorhoeic, antidysenteric, root in bowel complaints of children.


Chemistry:
Essential oil contains 1, 8-cineole, eugenol, limonene, ocimene, geranial – cis–3 hexenol, citronellol, α-terpineol, camphor, methyleugenol, methyl cinnamate as minor and linalool, methyl chavicol (estragole) as major components, methyl cinnamate may be a major constituent in some chemovars.

·         Methyl cinnamate, methylchavicol, linalool, cineole, ocimene, borneol, sambulene and safrole obtained from essential oil (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1960-1969)

·         New sesquiterpene hydrocarbon – 1-epibicyclosesquiphelandrene – isolated from oil. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·         Methyl chavicol (90%) and linalool present in essential oil obtained from green leaves. ((Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·         Methylchavicol (70-80%) and linalool (15-25%) identified in oil by GLC. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·         Oil contained about 70% citral. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·         Plant contained oil (0.28%); main components methylchavicol and linalool identified. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·         Oil contained estragole, linalool, eucalyptol, ocimene, linalool acetate, eugenol, 1-epibicyclosesquiphellandrene, menthol, menthone, cyclohexanol, cyclohexanone, myrcenol and nerol. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·         Juvocimene I and juvocimene II isolated from oil and their structures determined and confirmed by synthesis. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989).

·         Leaves afforded thymol, xanthomicrol and butyl caffeate. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989).

·         Methyleugenol (33.95) and eugenol (23.1%) determined as major components in supercritical carbon dioxide extract of oil by GC-MS. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989).

·         Essential oil at concentration of 0.15% compeletely inhibited mycelial growth of twenty two species of fungi including mycotoxin – producing strains of Aspergillus flavus and A. parasiticus and repelled the insect, Allacophora foveicollis. Toxic dose of oil against test fungi was much lower than that of some fungicides and fumigants. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).

·         Linalool, estragole and eugenol identified in various basil oils by GC-MS. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).

·         Isolation of p-conmaric acid,aesculetin, eriodic tyol, its 7-glucoside and vicenin 2 from leaves. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).

·         Linalool (32.2) and methyl chavicol (16.42%) determined in essential oil of plants grown in Portugal. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).

·         Linalool (52.3-59.3), eugenol (9.67-17.63), cineole (3.88-5-.26), b-terpinene (2.07-2.80), methyl cinnamate (2.79-4.52), methyl chavicol (2.20-4.02) and terpinen -4-ol (1.90-2.43%) detetnmined in various samples of essential oil by GC. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).

·         Determination of methyl chavicol (66.75), 1,8-cineole (5.44), linalool (4.95) and a-bisabolene (3.60%) in essential oil from Cuban basil by GC-MS. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).

·         Characterization of nutrition value of Ocimum basilicum. (Anikina et al., 1994) BA. 1995, 100: 189002.

·         Extraction and GC/MS analysis of the essential oil of Ocimum basilicum. (Riaz et al., 1994) BA. 1995, 100: 190754.

·         A system for the classification of essential oil chemotypes in O. basilicum is proposed. (Grayer et al., 1996)

·         Anthocyanins were isolated and characterized utilizing high-performance liquid chromatography, spectral data, and plasma desorption mass spectrometry from O. basilicum. 14 different anthocyanins were isolated, consisting of 11 cyanidin-based pigments and 13 peonidin-based pigments. The large leaf-basil varities, purple, ruffles, rubin, and dark opal, had an average extractable total anthocyanin content ranging from 16.63 to 18.78 mg/100 g of fresh tissue, while the ornamental small leaf variety, purple bush, had only 6.49 mg/100 g of fresh tissue. The highest concentration of total anthocyanins occurred just prior to flowering, although by day 8 seedlings already had accumulated all 14 anthocyanins. (Pnippen and Simon, 1998).

·         Ocimum basilicum cv. Genovese Gigante is the basil cultivar used most in the production of a typical Italian sauce called pesto. The aromatic composition of plants at different growth stages was determined. Plants from different areas of northwestern Italy were analyzed at 4 and 6 weeks after sowing and showed methyleugenol and eugenol as the main components. The content of these compounds was correlated with plant height rather than plant age. Particularly, methyleugenol was predominant in plants up to 10 cm in height, whereas eugenol was prevalent in taller plants. These results are important in the evaluation of risk to human health posed by dietary ingestion of methyleugenol contained in pesto. (Miele et al., 2001).

·         Chemical studies revealed that rosmarinic acid is the predominant phenolic acid present in both flower and leaf tissues. Unusual basil (Ocimum basilicum L.) accessions were identified that can serve as genetic sources of phenolic acids for crop improvement. (Javanmardi et al., 2002)

·         Sweet basil (Ocimum basilicum L.) is an herb that is used to add a distinct aroma and flavor to food. Volatile compounds emitted from fully expanded fresh leaves grown in drip-irrigated plots that were covered with six colors of mulch were compared. The colors reflected a range of photosynthetic photon flux, far-red, red, and blue light from the soil surface to developing leaves. Objective was to determine whether reflection from the different colors could influence concentrations of volatile compounds emitted from the fresh leaves. Volatile compounds were isolated by headspace sampling and quantified by gas chromatography. Twenty-six compounds were identified, of which the terpenoids linalool and 1,8-cineole comprised more than 50% of the total yield. Concentrations of volatile compounds from leaves that developed over green, blue, yellow, white, and red mulches followed the same patterns as they did for air-dried leaves of the same cultivar. However, the concentration of volatile compounds from fresh leaves was about 50-fold higher than those found in the previous study of air-dried leaves. (Loughrin and Kasperbauer, 2003)

·         The essential oil basil is obtained from the aerial parts of Ocimum basilicum L. After gas chromatographic separation, the following components were identified: linalol (54.95%), methylchavikol (11.98%), methylcinnamat (7.24%) and linolen (0.14%). The activity of basil against multidrug resistant clinical isolates from the genera Staphylococcus, Enterococcus and Pseudomonas has been studied. For this purpose, standard and modified broth macrodilution methods were used and time kill kinetic of basil was studied. The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were reported between 0.0030% and 0.0007% (v/v). These concentrations were compared with the inhibitory concentrations (ICs) and the logs of the bacterial counts reduction both obtained by basil diluted in 1% Tween (Tw) 80, saline test solution (STS) and spiritus vini (Sv) 95 degrees instead in a broth. The data, obtained after application of different methods of investigation and validated with membrane filtration, showed a strong inhibitory effect of basil on the test bacteria. The chosen bacteria are widespread and pose serious therapeutic difficulties because of their high level of resistance. For this reason, the results obtained were considered encouraging. (Opalchenova and Obreshkova, 2003)

·         Leaf-derived suspension cultures of sweet basil, Ocimum basilicum L. accumulated rosmarinic acid up to 10 mg g-1 dry wt, a value up to 11 times higher than in callus cultures or in leaves of donor plants. Immobilized cells accumulated less than 15 mg rosmarinic acid g-1. (Kintzios et al., 2003)

Pharmacology:

·         Essential oils extracted by hydrodistillation from five different varieties of Ocimum basilicum L examined for antimicrobial activity against a wide range of foodborne Gram-positive and -negative bacteria, yeasts and moulds by an agar well diffusion method. (Lachowicz et al., 1998)

·         Basil showed potent anti-HIV-1 activity (with an ED of16 mg/ml). The active components in the extract sample were found to be water-soluble polar substances, not nonpolar compounds such as essential oil. In addition, aqueous extract inhibited giant cell formation in co-culture of Molt-4 cells with and without HIV-1 infection and showed inhibitory activity against HIV-1 reverse transcriptase. (Yamasaki, et al., 1998)

·         Fixed oil of O. basilicum was found to possess significant antiulcer activity against aspirin, indomethacin, alcohol, histamine, reserpine, serotonin and stress-induced ulceration in experimental animal models. Significant inhibition was also observed in aspirin-induced gastric ulceration and secretion in pylorus ligated rats. The lipoxygenase inhibiting, histamine antagonistic and antisecretory effects of the oil could probably contribute towards antiulcer activity. (Singh, 1999).

·         Antioxidant activities of volatile extracts isolated from basil evaluated by two independent assays: the aldehyde/carboxylic acid assay and the conjugated diene assay. The antioxidant activities of the extract decreased in the following order in both of the lipophilic assay systems. (Lee and Shibamoto, 2002)

·         Basil essential oils and their principal constituents were found to exhibit antimicrobial activity against a wide range of Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, yeast, and mold. (Suppakul et al., 2003)

·         The antioxidant activity of a methanolic extract of Ocimum basilicum L. (sweet basil) was examined using different in vitro assay model systems. The crude extract was fractionated on a Sephadex LH-20 column, and six fractions were identified. The major antioxidant compound in fraction IV was confirmed as rosmarinic acid by (1) H NMR and characteristic fragmentations in the mass spectrum. Moreover, the native of antioxidant activity of rosmarinic acid in the liposome system was examined. The results showed that one rosmarinic acid can capture 1.52 radicals, and furthermore, the existence of a synergistic effect between alpha-tocopherol and rosmarinic acid was revealed. (Jayasinghe et al., 2003)
