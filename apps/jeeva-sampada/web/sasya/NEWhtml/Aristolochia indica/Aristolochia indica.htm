DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Herbs; leaves digitately or pinnately 3-5 lobed, segments lanceolate, entire or toothed; flowers in heads, radiate, ray florets white, disc florets yellow; fruits achenes fusiform, striate. 

Stamens usually 6-12; filaments distinct; pollen grains usually lacking apertures. Carpels 4-6, connate, often twisting in development; ovary/ovaries half inferior to inferior. Stigmas 4-6, often lobed and spreading. Ovules numerous. 
Flowers are mainly fly-pollinated. Flies are attracted to the flowers by their dull red and and often mottled coloration and odor.
Glandular hairs on the calyx tube produce nectar which is a treat for the pollinator. Pollen-bearing flies are trapped in the calyx tube by downward pointing hairs. During the first phase of the flower's life, pollen is deposited on the stigmas that crown the gynoceium. After pollination, the stigmas dry up and become erect. The flies become pollen covered, and are freed when the hairs and calyx tube wither. The flowers seeds are usually flat, wind and water dispersed, or have sticky seeds, for external transport by animals.

Aristolochia consists of the dried stem and root of Aristolochia indica, Linn. (N.O. Aristolochiaceae), a shrubby, twining plant indigenous to India. The stem, which forms the bulk of the drug, occurs in yellowish-brown more or less cylindrical pieces, usually varying from 5 to 10 millimetres in diameter, and bearing occasional scars of leaves and branches. The bark of the younger pieces is marked with conspicuous longitudinal furrows, but the older ones have a somewhat warty appearance. The transverse section exhibits a large porous wood traversed by a few medullary rays; the wood bundles are characterised by a disposition to divide dichotomously, owing to the production of secondary medullary rays. The root is tortuous, reddish-brown in colour, and exhibits occasional transverse constrictions. The bark, like that of the stem, is easily separated, disclosing a conspicuously furrowed wood, which has a structure resembling that of the stem. The drug has a camphoraceous odour, and a bitter, camphoraceous taste. Aristolochia is official in India and the Eastern Colonies, where it is used as an equivalent of serpentary rhizome.

Chemistry:  

Constituents.—The chief constituents of the drug are a bitter alkaloidal substance named aristolochine, and a volatile oil which probably contains borneol; the drug is also said to contain aristin, aristinic acid, resin, tannin, and starch.
Action and Uses.—Aristolochia is used entirely for its bitter properties, its action resembling that of gentian and serpentary. The alkaloid aristolochine has an action like that of aloin, but is more toxic. In rabbits it causes necrotic nephritis with albuminuria and uraemic symptoms. It is administered in the form of the tincture and the concentrated solution.  

This family produces aristolochic acid and has silica bodies in the parenchyma tissue and spherical oil cells. The flowers usually have the smell of rotting flesh, which acts as an attraction for fly pollinators. The leaves of several members of the family are capable of producing dermititis.

Miscellaneous Information

Aristolochiaceae are often placed with Magnoliales, but RNA sequences suggest that they may be related to the monocots group. Studies indicate that the family could be paraphyletic (having a common ancestor and some but not all of its descendents), with Lactoris placed within it, but the family here is considered monoplyletic because of its typically inferior ovary and connate sepaled flowers. Thorne (1992) and Huber (1193) divided the family into Asaroideae, which are perennial herbs with radial flowers that have free stamens, and Aristolochioideae, which are subshrubs to lianas with typically bilateral flowers in which the stamens are attached to the style.

The aristolochic acid in Wild Ginger's (Asarum canadense) roots is used to relieve gas and promote sweating. The acid is also an anti-tumor compound. Native Americans use Wild Ginger for many illnesses including heart pain and ear problems. Tea is made from the root and leaves and is effective in surpressing indigestion, coughing (whooping cough), colds, cramps, sore throat, and nervousness. In China it is used in the healing of wounds and various bodily infections. 
Wild Ginger is also used in cooking, candy, perfumery, cultivation and horticulture. In cooking its roots can be dried and chopped to be used as seasoning and candy can be made from the rootstock. It is considered a high quality garden plant because it is easy to grow, forms a carpet, smells nice and has beautiful heart shaped leaves.
When Birthwort (Aristolochia longa) is in flower the entire plant can be used for medicinal needs. It is said to be useful in treating abdominal problems, caner, tumors and wounds, and as an aromatic stimulant in rheumatism and gout. The ancient Egyptians used it for such ailments as snakebites, indigestion, and promoting menstration. Its leaves can be used on external wounds because it contains a substance that is similar to alkaloid colchicine.
Dutchman's Pipes (Aristolochia durior) vine is planted often as a screen or cultivated as a porch vine because it is easy to grow and has large attractive heart shaped leaves. *Recent studies on this family have shown that internal use can cause carcinogenic effects.*

A few species in the family are vital for the survival of butterflies. In India there has been concern that the swallowtail butterfly Tros aristolochae may become endangered because the Aristolochia indica is endangered. This butterfly will only lay its eggs on Aristolochia vines, and its catepillars feed on the Aristolochiales foliage.
