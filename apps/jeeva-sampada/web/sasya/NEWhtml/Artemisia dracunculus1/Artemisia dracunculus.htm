Taxonomic Details

Name : Artemisia dracunculus L.
Family : Asteraceae (Compositae)
Classification :
 Class : Liliopsida 
 Subclass : Asteridae
 Order : Asterales
Synonyms : Artemisia indora Willd. (1809), A. redowskyi Ledeb. (1815), 
Oilgosporus condimentarius Cass. (1826)
Common Names: 
English : Tarragon, Estragon, Dragon mugwort

Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Perennial green herb. In India it is found in the alpine regions of Kashmir and Western Himalayas at altitudes of 4000-4500 m. 

Climate and soil
Primarily a crop of temperate climates. Thrives in well-drained, light soil under full sun, does not tolerate water logging. Requires a pH soil of 6.3-7.8. Should be protected from excessive moisture, requires an annual rainfall of 300-1300 mm only. Can survive light frost and plant growth is stimulated by long days. Wild plants occur in open, rather dry places, eg, in meadows and steppe zones, from the plains to moderate elevations in mountains (1).  

Availability : 

Origin not known, may be Siberia. Has very wide distribution, from central Europe and western and northern Asia (Asia Minor, Mongolia, northern China, Siberia) to western north America. It is also cultivated in France, Italy, Germany, Netherlands, US, Brazil, Egypt and India. In south east Asia locally cultivated in mountainous region of Java and introduced into Philippines (1).  

 

Conservation/ Threat Status :  Wild and cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Perennial, green, erect herb, 0.5-1.5 m tall, glabrous, strongly aromatic or almost odourless. Rhizome woody, up to 1.5 cm thick, sometimes with well-developed underground shoots. stem erect, solitary or several, branched, furrowed, lower branches not flowering. Leaves alternate, sub-sessile, entire, simple, olive-green, lowest ones deciduous and sometimes ending in 2-5 narrow lobes; blade linear-lanceolate to almost linear, 2-10 cm x 1-14 mm, margin sometimes weakly serrate (1).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Inflorescence a drooping subglobose head, 2-4 mm diameter, aggregated at apices of stem and branches into racemes which together give a paniculate appearance; peduncle 0-5 mm long; involucre 2-4 mm long, outer bracts oblong to lanceolate, inner ones rounded-ovate, margin membraneous; outer florets usually 7(-15), pistillate and fertile, with tubular yellowish, whitish or reddish corolla 1 mm long and 2 spreading, often twisted style branches; disk florets 11-14, bisexual but functionally male, corolla conical, 5-toothed, up to 2 mm long, anthers linear, pistil rudimentary with more or less connate style branches (1).  

Fruit
Fruit an achene, flat-ovoid, 0.6-1 mm long, finely grooved, glabrous, brown (1).

CUTLIVATION ASPECT:

Local cultivars :
Two types or tarragon occur- the French tarragon and Russian tarragon. French tarragon (A. dracunculus) is the true tarragon and is preferred for culinary use. Plant is called tarragon because it was thought to cure the bites and stings of venomous animals. Russian tarragon (A. dracunculoides) occurs naturally in many temperate Asian regions and is considered to be of inferior quality. Russian tarragon is of bigger stature, reaching about two meters in height. Russian tarragon occurs in Afghanistan, Tibet and in Northern Nepal.

Chromosome number:
2n=18, 36, 54, 72, 90

Selection of planting material:
Stem cuttings or root cuttings are used as planting material.

Planting:
Planting is done in summer months and the basins have to be mulched heavily to protect the plant from frosty weather. Stem cuttings 15 cm long are taken from shoot tips and rooted in mist beds or coarse river sand with regular watering. Presoaking the basal ends in 100 ppm Indole butyric acid (IBA) overnight or dipping in 2000 ppm IBA for a few seconds enhances rooting. In rhizome cuttings, pieces of main rhizome, 5 cm long, together with roots and a new shoot, are severed from the mature plant. Planting material is established in poly bags before field planting. It is planted at 20-25 cm distance in rows 25-40 cm apart (1).  

Management after planting:
Mulch with straw, which prevents weed growth, conserves soil moisture and lowers soil temperature. Regular watering is necessary. Too much fertilizer and too much watering should be avoided (1).
MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:

No serious pests have been reported. Beetle Cantharis lateralis feeds on the leaves. 

Diseases:
No serious damages are reported.
Rust – caused by Puccinia dracunculina – affects leaf.
Stalk and root rot – caused by Sclerotinia minor (1).

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Tarragon is an important culinary herb and an essential ingredient in many famous French sauces such as béarnaise and hollandaise. It is often combined with chervil in cooking. Tarragon vinegar is made by saturating vinegar with young fresh shoots. Tarragon gives a special taste to fresh tomatoes, and is used in salads and salad dressings. It is used in all herb mixtures, in mayonnaise, preserves and pickles, in marinades for meat and in stuffings for meat and poultry. It is used as an accompaniment to steaks, chops and grilled meat. Fresh or dried leaves of tarragon have sweet anise-like scent and a peculiar bittersweet flavour and are used for seasoning foods like salads, soups, stews and sauces. They are particularly suited to flavour chicken, egg and lobster preparations. Tarragon vinegar is prepared by adding herb to wine vinegar. Due to low sodium content, safely used as a substitute sodium chloride in sodium-restricted diets. Tarragon oil is used in perfumery, in canning pickles, and in flavouring vinegar and liqueurs. Commercially plantings are renewed every 3-4 years as the oil content diminishes gradually in older plants (1).

Parts Used: Aerial leafy shoots.


Harvesting Practices: When started from cuttings, first leaves can be harvested 2-3 months after establishment. 
Aerial leafy shoots and tender tops are harvested periodically for use, during the growing season. Per year 2-3 harvests may be done. In commercial farms first harvest is done in 2 months after transplanting. Market demand is high during Christmas season (1).

Post Harvest Handling
Freshly cut shoots are bundled and immediately packed in styrofoam boxes for delivery to markets. Dried product is obtained by air-drying the shoots under shade or placing them in artificial driers with temperatures not exceeding 43°C. then they are stored in well-closed barrels. When oil is needed, the dried herb is steam distilled for 1-1.5 hours. Oil is stored in well-filled bottles and protected from light (1).

Produce and Products:


Chemistry: French or tarragon has sweet to slightly bitter taste, overall flavour quality is rated as delicate. Major components are: methyl chavicol (estragole), trans-ocimene + γ-terpinene, cis-ocimene and limonene. Russian type has a much bitter taste and is not used where delicate flavour is required. Shoots and leaves contain about 0.3-0.5% of volatile oil. Major components of the oil are: elemicin, sabinene, trans-ocimene + γ-terpinene, methyl eugenol, myrcene, cis-ocimene, citronellyl acetate and terpine-4-ol. There are also differences in flavonol profile of the air-dried stems and leaves of the two types. Russian tarragon contains patuletin glycoside, quercetin and quercetin glycosides. French tarragon has only quercetin glycosides. Has the regulatory status GRAS. Tarragon (GRAS 3043) and tarragon oil (GRAS 2412). Maximum permitted level of tarragon leaves in food products is 0.3% and the highest level of oil is 0.04% in baked goods.

Per 100 g edible portion of dried tarragon contains approx.: water – 5-8 g, protein – 23-25 g, fat – 7-7.5 g, carbohydrates – 43-45 g, fibre – 7 g, ash – 12 g, ascorbic acid – 100 mg, riboflavin – 1 mg, thiamine – 0.3 mg, Ca – 1.1 g, Fe – 32 mg, K – 3.0 g, Mg – 0.3 g, Na – 62 mg, P – 0.3 g, Zn – 4 mg, niacin – 9 mg, Vitamin A – 4200 IU, vitamin C – 12 mg. The energy value is about 1385 kJ/100 g. essential oil content – 0.2-1.0% (1).

Other uses: Medicinal uses
Has aperient, stomachic, stimulant and febrifuge properties. Tarragon oil possesses antibacterial and nematicidal properties (1).

Miscellaneous Information

1. Sweet scented marigold (Tagetes lucida Cav. (Compositae), also known as Mexican tarragon resembling tarragon with respect to flowers and fragrance, is used as a substitute. 
2. In northern Asia, the wild plants often become troublesome weeds in pastures and are difficult to eradicate because of strong rhizomes (1).
