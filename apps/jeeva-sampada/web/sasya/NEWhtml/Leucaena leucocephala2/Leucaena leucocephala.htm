Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Thornless, fast growing, perenial, evergreen tree. Native of southern Mexico (region of Chimpas and Yucatan), introduced to many countries. It was introduced in India long ago and is now being extensively cultivated in U.P., Maharashtra, W. Bengal, Andhra Pradesh, Orissa, Karnataka, Punjab and Himachal Pradesh. Occurs in tropical medium rainfall areas of Indo-Gangetic Plains and the Peninsula (moist tropical), dry areas of Rajasthan and Peninsular India (dry tropical). Fairly resistant to drought though prolonged drought may kill seedlings on clay soils. Light demander, frost tender, can tolerate partial shade, can withstand drought.

Leucaena is restricted to the tropics and sub-tropics. It is frost tender. This is a species for lowland areas mainly below 500 m. the plant continues growing at high elevations, but without its lowland vigour. Low temperatures limit its growth, since it is temperature-sensitive tree. It grows best where annual rainfall is 600-1,700 mm, with 4-5 months dry period. Its root system allows it to tolerate a wide array of soil conditions. It is found in soils varying from rock to heavy clay to coral. Unaided, it grows well only in neutral or alkaline (especially limestone) soils. It grows poorly in acidic soils, and much of the tropics has acidic latosolic soils high in alumina and often deficient in molybdenum and zinc.


Availability : Tidal shores of India and Ceylon, Malaya.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Breeding system and varietal improvement: Flowers are self-pollinated.

Phenology:

The plant flowers and fruits at a very early age and vigorous plants produce seeds in the first or second year.


Pollinators:


Floral and seed biology:

Seed Germination & Propagation: Seed viability is high and the seeds can be successfully planted by hand or machine. Seedlings are slow starters. It can be reproduced by cuttings or grafts, but only with difficulty. Seeds are treated with hot water (800 C) for 2-3 minutes which gives eighty per cent germination within 8 days. Further increase can be obtained by then soaking the seeds for 2-3 days. Sapling growth is rapid and on good sites a height of 18 m is recorded to have been obtained in just 6-8 years. Under Indian conditions, a height growth up to 1.5 m, 5.05 m and 7.5 m has been recorded in 3,6 and 15 months respectively.

Natural reproduction in this plant takes place through seed and coppicing. The plant (particularly the Hawaiian type) seeds profusely. The seeds get scattered and germinate in forest openings where conditions such as sufficient light and soil moisture are available. The Hawaiian type is recorded to regenerate better than the Salvadoran or Peruvian types. It also coppices well and coppice growth is much faster.

It can be raised by direct sowing or by planting nursery-raised seedlings. Reproduction by cuttings or by grafts has been reported, though with difficulty. Vegetatively it has been propagated through root stump and root shoot cuttings. It has also been possible to root branch cuttings with auxin treatments.

Direct sowing is done either by broadcast or in drills in patches on prepared soil at the rate of 12-20 kg of seeds per hectare while for broadcast 20-25 kg of seeds per hectare may suffice. Closer spacing is advocated for soil conservation and forage production than for timber where a spacing of 3 m X 3 m to 5 m X 5 m may be adopted. The seeds germinate within a week of sowing and 60-70% germination is expected. The seedlings are slow starters.

For nursery raised seedlings, the treated seeds are sown in the nursery during the months of March-April, in lines spaced 30 cm apart and seeds at 5 cm distance. Germination starts in about 4-6 days and is about 60-70%. The seedlings attain plantable height in July/August. Container seedlings are raised in polythene bags filled with a mix of soil and compost (2:1) and two seeds are sown per bag at a depth of 1.5 cm. One seedling is clipped after germination has taken place. Shifting of bags twice at an interval of two months is necessary as taproots develop fast and strike soil below.

Tree has coppicing ability.



MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:

Pests:

Seed weevil attack has been reported.

Termites may kill newly transplanted seedlings.

Psyllids attack the tender foliage.

Diseases:

In waterlogged areas fungi damage the roots and crown.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts Used:

Fuel:

This makes excellent firewood and charcoal. The wood has uncommonly high density and calorific value for a fast-growing tree, and because the stumps readily coppice, the plant could become a renewable fuel resource in areas suited to its agronomic requirements.

Wood: Yields 32-40 m3/ ha annually. Arboreal varieties yield wood lumber and timber suitable for agricultural implements. It has a potential as a major source for pulp and paper and wood for construction.

Other uses:
Soil improvement - Leaves and twigs rich in nitrogen, also in sodium and potassium, can be used for composting. Its aggressive root system also breaks up impervious subsoil layers, improving moisture penetration and decreasing surface runoff.

Forage – Cattle feeding on foliage in Queensland, Australia, have shown some of the highest weight gains ever measured in the tropics. Suited mainly to cattle, water buffalo and goats. Forage is highly palatable, digestible and nutritious. Both beef and dairy cattle thrive on it and can live on leucaena alone until mimosine-related toxicity occurs. This can be delayed or eliminated entirely by supplementing the diet with other forages. Leaves, pods and seeds used as fodder.

Paper pulp - Can be used as major source of short-fibred pulp for manufacture of paper in conjunction with long-fibred pulp.

Roots as medicine.

Pods yield a dye used to colour wool, cotton and fishing nets. Powdered seeds make good manure.

Fish-poison, worm-repellant.

Green manure - It can be used as green manure. 

Therapeutic - Mimosine is considered therapeutic as a contraceptive. The flowers, leaves and root bark are also used.

Pods - Green pods are eaten.

Miscellaneous Information

It can not tolerate partial shade but grows best in full sun.

Neutral to alkali soils best.

Over 100 varieties of this plant are known, which can be broadly classified as the Hawaiian type, Salvadoran type and Peruvian type. The first type includes short bushy types up to 5 m height, flowering when young (4-6 months old) and almost the year round. Seeds are produced in abundance. Suitable for fuelwood and charcoal. The second variety is tall, attaining 20 m height, having large leaves and pods and with a branchless trunk. Some high-yielding cultivars are called 'Hawaiian giants' and are cultivated for timber and wood. The Peruvian type is also tall (up to 15 m) but with short clean bole and extensive branching down the trunk. It produces forage. In India this plant has been extensively planted and is considered a cure for all land ailments which is not always the case.

Soil improvement - A nitrogen-fixing tree, which helps to enrich the soil through atmospheric nitrogen fixation.

Agro-social forestry - It can be used for betel leaf garden and banana plantations and can be intercropped with (5-6 m) millets, sweet potato and maize and back rows used as mulch where cutback material may be used as mulch/ green manure/ fodder. It reduces wind and water erosion when planted in rows along bunds and terraces. Suitable cover crop in plantation tree crops such as coconut, coffee, etc. the tree is planted for soil conservation.

Reforestation – It has an ability to thrive on steep slopes on marginal soils and in areas with an extended dry season. This makes it a potential plant species for restoring forest cover on denuded wastelands, slopes and grasslands. Suitable for planting in parks, gardens and avenues.

Seedlings show mycorrhizal association. Glomus fasciculatum is found to be associated with the plant. This helps the seedlings in successful transplantation and establishment on barren lands or under stressed conditions.
VAM fungi help the plant to avoid drought stress.
Mycorrhizal roots exude amino acids and sugars. These exudates result in the decrease in the no. of fungal colonies in the rhizosphere.
Due to mycorrhizal inoculation (Glomus fasciculatum), the survival rate in micropropagated plants improves.
Tree is a nitrogen fixer.
The plants have been micropropagated.
