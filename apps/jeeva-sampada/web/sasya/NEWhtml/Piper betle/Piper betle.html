Chemistry:

·        b-sitosterol isolated from roots. (Rastogi and Mehrotra 1970-1979)a.

·        Eugenol, methyleugenol, p-cymene, a-terpineol, terpinyl acetate and caryophyllene detected in essential oil by GLC. (Rastogi and Mehrotra 1980-1984)a.

·        Five propenylphenols–chavicol, chavibetol, allylpyrocatechol, chavibetol acetate   and allylpyrocatechol diacetate isolated from leaves (Rastogi and Mehrotra 1980-1984)b.

·        Chavibetol (53.1), chavibetol acetate (15.5), caryophyllene (3.79), allylpyrocatechol diacetate (0.71), camphene (0.48), chavibetol methyl ether (0.48), eugenol (0.32), a-pinene (0.21), β-pinene (0.21), a-limonene (0.14), safrole (0.11), 1, 8-cineole (0.04), p-cymene (0.11) and allylpyrocatechol monoacetate determined in leaf oil of plant grown in Philippines. (Rastogi and Mehrotra 1985-1989)a.

·        The constituents of the essential oil of Piper betle L. served as determinants to distinguish betelvine cultivars. Eugenol was found to be the major and common compound amongst the cultivars, being dominant in “Bangla” (64%). 1,3-Benzodioxole (5)-2-propenyl was maximum in cv. “Desawari” (45%). Cv. “Meetha” can be distinguished from others due to the prescence of Anethole (19.31%) and cis-caryophyllene. Amongst the five cultivars studied, “Kapoori”; appeared to be a distinct genotype as marked by large number of specific molecules such as a-thujene, trans b-ocimene, terpinolene, allo ocimene, D-cadinene, terpinen-1-ol, a-costol, D-cadinol, methyl-2-hexadecan-1-ol, geraniol, hexadeconic acid, methyl benzoate etc. “Sanchi” is marked by the prescence of stearaldehyde which is absent in other cultivars. (Rawat et. al., 1989).

·        Ursolic acid its 3β-acetyl derivative, ursonic acid and β-sitosterol isolated. (Rastogi and Mehrotra 1990-1994)b.

·        The chemical composition of leaf oils of P. betle was determined by co-chromatography with authentic samples on two columms of different polarity, GC-MS and Kovats retention indices. Chavibetol constituted 69.0 percent of the oils P. betle.   (Janatan, I.; et. al., 1994). MAPA 9503-1733.

·        Diosgenin content in the roots of Piper betle was estimated by GLC and was found to be 0.08 percent on dry wt. basis. The roots, a waste by-product obtained during replantation of the crop has been suggested to be tapped as cheaper source of diosgenin. (Dixit et. al., 1995). MAPA-9603-1599.

·        Piperamine (0.83 percent), pellitorine (3.1), 2, 4-decadienoic acid piperidide (0.59) and kusunokinin (0.68) were isolated along with previously isolated amino acids and monosaccharides, alkaloids, piperine, sylvatine piperllonguminine and piplartine and sitosterol from the stem barks of P. chaba. (Connolly, J. D., et. al., 1995). MAPA 9505-3144.

·        A hot headache sampling (HS) and gas chromatograph- mass spectrometric method (GC-MS) for the determination of amphetamine (AP) and methamphetamine (MA) in betel is described. The method uses potassium carbonate to alkalize the aqueous matrix and to salt out the analytes prior to HS. The complicated matrixes necessitate the separate analysis of Piper betle. Quality identification is aided by the mass spectrum provided by the proposed method. The limits of quantization vary from 0.02 to 1.20 mg/ml. Precisions calculated for the 5 and 50 mg/ml concentration range are ~20%. The method is simple, rapid, solvent less, and requires only a small amount of sample. It may serve as a screening protocol for the determination of AP and MA in betel. (Wang et. al., 1995).

·        Phenolic analyses were conducted and the anti-oxidative activity of Piper betle leaf was examined in this study. Furthermore, the effect of phenolics on the mutagenicity of arecoline was also studied. It was evident that Piper betle leaf contained rich carotenes (80 IU/g fresh weight), ascorbic acid (1.94 mg/g fresh weight) and phenolics. Hydroxy-chavicol which showed strong anti-oxidative activity and inhibited the mutagenicity of arecoline, was the most abundant phenolic compound in Piper betle leaf. The mixture of hydroxychavicol, ascorbic acid and carotenes from Piper betle leaf inhibited the mutagenecity of arecoline significantly. (Wang et. al., 1996).       

·        Thirteen cultivars of betelvine (Piper betle) collected from different regions were raised and their essential oil was extracted. The essential oil which constitute the flavour or aroma of the leaves been examined by gas liquid   chromatography (GLC). The results led to grouping of the cultivars, which had similar patterns of chromatogram with marked differences from one group to another. The variation in chemical constituents and their proportions in the essential oil of different cultivars also observed to be a reliable marker for the identification of betelvine cultivars. (Ravi Sankar, et. al. 1996). MAPA-9701-0581.

·        The keto-acids present in 3-mold light and dark grown leaves of Piper betle were extracted and characterized by standard procedures. The keto acids found were pyruvic acid, a-keto-glutaric acid, pyruvic acid and b-keto butyric acid in dark grown and levulinic acid, pyruvic acid, phenyl-pyruvic acid, a-keto glutaric acid and glyocalic acid were found in light grown leaves. (Bhuyan et. al., 1996).    

·        Ten varieties of pan leaves were taken and the extraction of keto acids from leaves was done using 2, 4 dinitrophenylhdrazine. The keto acids identified were: pyruvic acid, alpha-keto glutaric acid, ascorbic acid and beta-keto butyric acid. (In dark grown leaves). Levulinic acid, pyruvic acid, phenyl pyruvic acid, alpha keto glutaric acid and glyoxalic acid were found in light grown leaves. (Kar and Missa, 1997).

·        Betel leaves of 10 different varieties were kept in a polythene container covered with fresh banana leaves with arrangement for aeration and drainage. It was found that the ascorbic acid content and nitrogen of the leaves decreased on storage. Whereas, vitamin A and phosphorus content increased on storage. (Bhuyan et. al., 1997).

·        From Piper betle beta-sitosteryl palmitate was isolated for the first time. (Poonam et. al., 1998). MAPA-9802-1106.

·        From Piper betle, b-sitosteryl palmitate was isolated for the first time from genus Piper. (Parmar et. al., 1998).

·        The main compounds safrole (39.9%), eugenol (9.0%) allopyrocatechol monoacetate (8.5 %) and tlepinen-4-ol (6.3%) were identified. Other phenolic compounds, like chavibetol, eugenol and allopyrocatechol derivatives were found besides mono-and sequiterpenes. (Jirovetz, L., et. al; 1999). MAPA-2000-01-0604.

·        The essential oil from vines and leaves of Piper betle Linn. (Piperaceae) was obtained by stem distillation, the oil content was 0.2% (wt./wt.), on a wet basis. Chemical constituents of Thialand betel vine oil were analyzed by GC and GC - MS.58 of the 64 compounds detected have been identified, accounting for 99% of the oil, their main components are acetyl eugenol (31.768%), trans-Isoeugenol (28.322%), 4-Allyl phenyl acetate (8.053%), beta-Cayophyllene (3.063%), Germacrene-d(2.917%), alpha-Amorphense (2.520%), alpha-Cadinol (2.436%), and Chavicol (1.994%). The essential oil has medical effect. (Lio and Caolin, 2000). 


Pharmacology:

·        Extract of plain betel (Piper betle) leaf with betel nut. (Areca catechu) with and without autoclaving were tested for surface and infiltration anesthetic activities using rabbits and guinea pigs. Betel leaf showed dose dependent infiltration anesthetic activity comparable with xyloquinone. As a surface anaesthetic, the onest was quick as zylocine and the duration was shorter than xylocanie. Betel nut significantly reduced the infiltration activity and abolished the surface anesthetic activity of betel leaf. Betel leaf has potent anesthetic action both by surface and infiltration techniques. This effect is reduced by the addition of betel nut but not lost on autoclaving. (Kris The alcoholic extract of talk of leaf of [P. betle] (Known as Jhal pan) was fed orally to adult male albino rats at dose level of 800-1500 mg/kg body weight per rat per day for sixty consecutive days. This resulted in cessation of spermatogenesis, degenerative changes in the seminiferous tubules, epididymis, seminal vesicles, ventral prostate and coagulating glands. Reduction in the weight and fluid content of the accessory organs was also observed. It was found that the average number of implantation sites in female rats which mated with treated males reduced with dose and duration of treatment. Similarly, the cauda epididymal sperm count and motility also decreased. The treatment also resulted in accumulation of glycogen and cholesterol in the serum and testes and increased activities of alkaline phosphatase content. Delta-5-3-beta-HSD and 17beta-HSD were decreased significantly in testes. These result suggested anti-spermatogenic and anti-androgenic effects of the alcoholic extract of the plant. (Chatterjee et. al., 1994). MAPA-9502-0845.    

·        Oral administration of crude alcoholic extract of leaf stalk (1.25 and 1.5 g/kg daily for 30 consecutive days) manifested anti-fertility effects in male rats, leading to decrease in relative organ weights with 0-33% fertility rate, reduction in sperm count and sluggish motility. Significant increase (P<0.01) in cholesterol (adrenal and testis) was also observed. (Rastogi and Mehrotra 1990-1994)a.

·        Extractives of P. betle containing mixture of unsaturated amides and longuminine isolated showed anti-fertility properties when tested on mice. The oil of leaves has antibacterial properties. (Banerji, A.; et. al., 1994-95). MAPA-9603-1379.

·        The influence of two varieties of betel leaf P. betle, namely the pungent Mysore and non-pungent Ambadi, was examined on digestive enzymes of pancreas and intestinal mucosa and on bile secretion in experimental rats. The results indicated that while these betel leaves do not influence bile secretion and composition they have a significant stimulatory influence on pancreatic lipase activity. Beside, the Ambadi variety of betel leaf has a positive stimulatory influence on intestinal digestive enzymes; especially lipase, amylase and disaccharides. Both the betel leaf varieties have shown decreasing influence on pancreatic trypsin and chymotrypsin activities. (Sandhya Prabhu, M. et. al., 1995). MAPA 9601-0209.

·        Piper betle inflorescence extracts contain eugenol (6.2 percent) and safrole (78.9 percent). IV injections of water extracts of Piper betle inflorescence (PBE), eugenol, and safrole resulted in hypotensive and and tachycardiac effects. Effects of intra-arterial injections of PBE on blood pressure were inhibited by the pretreatment with substance P (SP) antagonist (1 nmol, i.t.) and clonidine (2.5 microg, i.t.), while heart rate was only inhibited by the pretreatment with SP antagonist (1 nmol, i.t.). In addition, the trachycardia resulting from intrathecal injections of PBE was inhibited by pretreatment with propranolol (0.3 mg/kg, i.v.). Eugenol and safrole induced the same pattern on blood pressure and heart changes as PBE in rats after various treatments. Acute administration of betel inflorescence extracts by different routes may activate C-fiber-evoked parasympathetic and sympathetic cardiovascular reflexes in rats. (Chen et. al., 1995).

·        Piperbetol, methylpiperbetol, piperol A and piperol B isolated from Piper betle, selectively inhibited the washed rabbit platelet aggregation induced by platelet activating factor (PAF) in a concentration-dependent manner. The inhibitory potency of ginkgolide B was about 2.8, 1.2, 22.8 and 1.4 times higher than those of piperbetol, methylpiperbetol, piperol A, and piperol B. the EC50 of PAF was increased by these compounds. The compounds also inhibited the binding of {3H}-PAF to washed rabbit platelets. correlating with the inhibitory potency for platelet aggregation, the inhibitory potency of ginkgolide B for binding of PAF was about 3.8, 1.9, 48, and 2.4 times higher than those of piperbetol, methylpiperbetol, piperol A, and piperol B. No effect of these compounds was noted on CAMP. (Zeng et. al., 1997). MAPA 9706-3588.

·        Extracts were prepared by extracting with distilled water ethanol (95%) or chloroform at 370 or 500. Streptococcus salivarius, S. sanguis, S. mutans, Neisseria sp., Salmonella sp., Staphylococcus aureus, Yersinia enterocolitica and Listeria monocytogenes are used as the test organisms in this study. Results revealed that all the ingredients of betel quid tested contained anti-microbial substance. Under the test conditions, ethanol and chloroform extract of Piper betle was found to exhibit the most profound anti-microbial activity. Analysis with GC-MS revealed that these extracts contained eugenol and hydroxychavicol which possessed the anti-microbial activity. (Yang et. al., 1997).

·        Calotropis gigantea (Erukku) and   Piper nigrum (Milagu) were mixed with Piper betle juice. The paste made out of the above was made into pills. (Weighing 200mg). Eg. Withania somnifera case of bronchial asthma in the age group of 25-60 years of both sexes were given two pills a day with yew decoction. (Thaleesapathri Kasayam) for 45 days. The observation made proved that the selected compound drug had a high potential in the treatment of bronchial asthma.

·        The anti-allergic effects of Piper betle were tested in rat models and discussed with regard to its uses as traditional chinese medicine. (Yamaharo and Joji, 1998).

·        Extracts from Piper betle exhibited activity against Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Pseudmonas aeruginosa and Klebsiella sp. E. coli exhibited higher resistance to antibiotics and plant extract. (Rajendhran, J.; et. al., 1998). MAPA-9902-1118.

·        Betel leaf extract can be both antiperoxidative and peroxidatve in nature. The effects of betel leaves. (Panda and Kar, 1998).

·        The effects of betel leaf extract (0.10, 0.40, 0.80 and 2.0 g kg-1 day-1 for 15 days) on the alterations in thyroid hormone concentrations, lipid peroxidation, (LPO) and on the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) were investigated in male Swiss mice. Administration of betel leaf extract exhibited a dual role, depending on the different doses. While the lowest dose decreased thyroxine (T4) and increased serum tri-iodothyronine (T3) concentrations, reverse effects were observed at two higher doses. Higher doses also increased LPO with a concomitant decrease in SOD and CAT activities. However, with the lowest dose most of these effects were reversed. These findings suggest that betel leaf can be both stimulatory and inhibitory to thyroid function, particularly for T3 generation and lipid peroxidation in male mice, depending on the amount consumed. (Panda and Kar, 1998).

·        The effects of betel leaves Piper betle on lipid peroxidation (LOP), superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) activities have been studied using adult albino rats. The results indicate that betel leaf extract exhibits dose-dependent actions. At low concentration it is anti peroxidative, whereas at higher concentrations it induces oxidative damage to biological membranes. (Panda and Kar, 1998). MAPA 9903-1794.

·        An anti-allergic agent comprises extracts of leaves of Piper betle. The leaves were extracted with water or organic solvents and the extracts contain dimethyl eugenol.  (Yamaharo and Joji, 1999).

·        An extract from the leaves of Piper betle (Piperaceae) to be one of the most promising species for cancer chemoprevention. (Murakami et. al., 2000).

·        According to native take it can treat the AIDS in Thialand. (Lio and Caolin, 2000).

·        The crude aqueous extract of Piper betle leaves (Pb, Cr) at dose of 1-10mg/ml caused a moderate spasmogenic effect in isolated guinea-pig ileum and this activity was concentrated in the aqueous fraction, which was found to be about 5 times more potent. Pretreatment of the tissue with atropine (1 micro M) but not hexamethonium (100 micro M) completely abolished the contractile effects of the aqueous fraction indicating a cholinergic (muscarinic) mechanism. In isolated rabbits jejunum preparations Pb. Cr did not produce a significant increase in the spontaneous contractions, but instead produced a dose dependent (0.03-3.0 mg/ml) inhibition of spontaneous activity. The plant has been suggested to contain cholinomimetic and possible calcium channel antagonist constituents, which are concentrated in the aqueous and ethyl acetate fractions, respectively. (Gilani, A.H.; et. al., 2000). MAPA 2001-01-0201.

Extract of plain betel (Piper betle) leaf, with betel nut. (Areca catechu) with and without autoclaving, were tested for surface and infiltration anesthetic activities using rabbits and guinea pigs. Betel leaf showed dose dependent infiltration anesthetic activity comparable with xylocaine. As a surface anaesthetic, the onest was quick as xylocaine and the duration was shorter than xylocanie. Betel nut significantly reduced the infiltration activity and abolished the surface anesthetic activity of betel leaf. Betel leaf has potent anesthetic action both by surface and infiltration techniques. This effect is reduced by the addition of betel nut but not lost on autoclaving. (Krishnakumar et. al., 2001).

Pharmaceutical and Toxicology:

·        The alcoholic extract of stalk of leaf of [Piper betle] (known as Jhal Pan) was fed orally to adult male albino rats at dose level of 600-1500 mg/kg body weight per rat per day for sixty consecutive days. This resulted in cessation of spermatogenesis, degenerative changes in the seminiferous tubules, epididymis, seminal vesicles, ventral prostrate and coagulating glands. Reduction in the weight and fluid content of the accessory organs was also observed. It was found that the average number of implantation sites in female rats which mated with treated males reduced with dose and duration of treatment. Similarly the cauda epididymal sperm count and motility also decreased. The treatment also resulted in accumulation of glycogen and cholesterol in the serum and testes and increased activities of alkaline phosphatase in the serum and testes and decreased activity in acid phosphatase content. delta5-3beta-HSD and 17beta-HSD were decreased significantly in testes. These results suggested antispermatogenic and antiandrogenic effects of the alcoholic extract of the plant. (Chatterjee et. al., 1994).

·        Physiological effects of cardioacceleration, a slightly drunk feeling, sweating and salivation occurred during the chewing of betel quid (a mixture of areca fruit, Piper betle inflorescence and red lime paste) a mixture of areca fruit and red lime paste). Both induced much more basal catecholamine secretion from adrenal chromaffin cells than did other ingredients and combinations of ingredients. It was evident that the responses in the sympathetic model system were closely correlated with the physiological feeling of the well-being. The inhibitory effects of all the chewing juices on catecholamine secretion evoked by carbachol and a high concentration of Potassium (K+) showed that they perhaps affected the calcium influx through voltage- sensitive channels or the steps involved in secretion after calcium entry to stimulate basal catecholamine secretion from chromaffin cells. (Wang et. al., 1997).   

·        Studies on pre-clinical toxicity was undertaken to determine the haematological and biochemical changes after administration of alcoholic extracts of leaf stalk of Piper betle in rats and mice. Acute and chronic toxicity experiments were performed on mice and rats according to WHO protocol. Different doses of plant extratives were administered for toxicity studies on short and long-term basis. Haematological, biochemical profiles and enzymatic studies. (transminases and phosphatases) indicated that the plant extractives were devoid of toxicity. (Sengupta et. al., 2000).
