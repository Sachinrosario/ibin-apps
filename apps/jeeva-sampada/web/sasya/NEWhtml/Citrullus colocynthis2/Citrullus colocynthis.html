DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:
Perennial trailing herb, branchlets hirsute; leaves alternate, simple petiolate, ovate or narrowly triangular, deeply trilobed, lobes pinna tifid, triangular, chartaceous rigid; tendril simple, slender, petiole upto 3 cm; male flower 1 cm across, yellow; female flower ovary ovoid; fruits globose, striped green and white when young, yellow when ripe.


REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:   
Male Flower: Peduncled ; calyx hairy, campanulate, teeth lanceolate, lobes 5; corolla 5-partite beyond the middle, pale yellow, segments ovate apiculate, stamens 3, filaments short free, anthers scarcely cohering, the cells linear, flexuose  ; Female Flower: calyx hairy, campanulate, teeth lanceolate, lobes 5; corolla 5-partite beyond the middle, pale yellow; ovary ovoid, stigma short, style short, ovules many horizontal ; Fruit: Globose, fleshy, indehiscent; Seed: Many, oblong, compressed . .
Floral and seed biology:

Mode of pollination and seed dispersal:
Flowers are pollinated by insects. Seeds are dispersed by wind or animals.
 
Genetic variability: The reported chromosome numbers =22, 24.

Economic Potential and Uses
UTILIZATION:
Uses:
 Fruit, seed and root- purgative; root- antirheumatic, in jaundice, ascites, urinary troubles; fruit and root- antidote to snake poison.


Chemistry:
Sterols A, mp. 153°C and B mp. 265°C. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1960-1969)a.

A bitter oil ‘citbittol’ isolated. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1960-1969)b.

Glucose and a-spinasterol from fruits and seeds respectively. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1960-1969)c.

Anticancer glycoside - a-elaterin-2-D-glucopyranoside – in addition to a-elaterin (Cucurbitacin E, fig. 1a) and citrullol from pulp. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1960-1969)d.

Glycosides in fruit composed of elaterin, elatericin B (cucurbitacin I, fig. 1b) and dihydroelatericin B (cucurbitacin L, fig. 2). (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1960-1969)e.

A new substance from unripe fruit. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1960-1969)f.

Cucurbins B, E, I and cucurbitacin E-2 glucoside isolated from leaves and fruits. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979)a.

11,14-dimethylhexadecan-14-ol-2-one and 10,14-dimethyl-hexadecan-14-ol-2-one from fruit peels. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979)b.

Heptacosane (5.0), octacosane (2.19), nonacosane (16.37), triacontane (5.67), hentriacontane (48.23), dotriacontane (3.32) and tritriacontane (10.46%) from peels. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979)c.

Lauric, myristic, palmitic, hexadecenoic, stearic, oleic, linoleic, and arachidic acids, hexadecanol, octadecanol, eicosanol, docosanol, tetracosanol, hexacosanol, methylheptenone, citronellol, citronellal, phenylethyl alcohol and methyleugenol isolated from fruit peels. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979)d.

Detection of myristic, palmitic, stearic, myristoleic, palmitoleic, oleic and linoleic acids in fixed oil of seed by GLC. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984)a.

A new tetracyclic triterpene – citrullonol (fig. 3) isolated from seed oil and its structure elucidated. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984)b.

Isolation of hentriacontane, elateridine, hexanorcucurbitacin I and its 16-O-acetyl derivatives from fruits. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989)a.

Quercetin and kaempferol isolated from leaves and flowers. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989)b.

New cucrbitacin T (fig. 4) isolated from fruits of Sri Lankan plant and characterized; cucurbitacins I and J also isolated. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989)c.

Isolation and characterization of a new glycoside as 2-O-b-D-glucopyranosyl-22,23,24,25,26,27-hexanorcurcurbitacin I (fig. 5); 2-O-b-D-glucopyranosylcurcurnitacin I, 2-O-b-D-glucopyranosylcurcurbitacin E and 2-O-b-D-glucopyranosylcurcurbitacin L also isolated. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989)d.

Hentriacontane, n-octacosanol and 1,26-hexacosanediol isolated. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994)a.

Linoleic (51.3), oleic (24.1), Palmitic (12.8), and stearic (9.8%) acids determined in seed oil by GC-MS. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994)b.

Isolation of 1,11-undecanediol monoacetate and noyl hexadecanoate from roots and their characterization. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994)c.

Hydrocarbons, a-spinasterol, b-sitosterol in addition to pentacyclic triterpenes, ursolic acid and b-amyrin were isolated from the unsaponifiable matter of Citrullus colocynthis. Tetracyclic triterpenes were isolated and identified as cucurbitacins D, I, B, E, L from the chloroform extract of seed. (El-Gengaihi et al, 1998). C.A. 1998, 128, 287786b.

Fruit contains a glycoside- colocynthin, its aglycone-a- elaterin; citrullol. Plant contains colocythetin. Frit juice contains a- elaterin, citrullin, citrullene and citrulluic acid. Unripe fruit contains P-hdroxybenzyl methyl ester. Roots contain a-elaterin and hentriacontane.

Pharmacology:

Citrullus colocynthis seed extracts have an insulinotropic effect which could at least partially account for the antidiabetic activities of these fruits. (Nmila et al., 2000)

Glycosidic extract significantly lowered the fasting glucose levels after 2 and 3 h and highly significant after 6 h. The effect was more pronounced with saponin extract, the saponin significantly lowered the fasting glucose levels after 1 and 2 h and highly significant (P<0.001) after 3 and 6 h. Graded doses (10, 15 and 20 mg/kg) of saponin extract, when given orally to alloxan diabetic rabbits, produced a significant reduction of plasma glucose concentration. These results suggest that the aqueous extract of the rind of C. colocynthis possesses a hypoglycaemic effect and its hypoglycaemic action could be attributed for more extent to the presence of saponin in addition to the presence of glycosidic components. (Abdel-Hassan et al.,2000).

Ethnobotany:
Medicine for abortifacient (Root, Lal and Lata, 1980); Galactagogue (Fruit, Shekhawat and Anand, 1984); Purgative (Fruit, Shekhawat and Anand, 1984; Singh, 1945; Fruit + Seed, Shah et al, 1981); Stomachache (Fruit, Sen et al, 1985); Edible (Grain Shekhawat and Anand, 1984; Bhandari, 1974; Kanodia and Gupta, 1968)
Pharmaceutical and Toxicology:
Acute toxicity and histopathological effects of saponin (extracted from the plant Citrullus colocynthis) on mice in order to assess its safety. The median lethal dose (LD50) of the saponin was 200 mg/kg. The histological changes were confined to the small intestine, liver and kidney, whereas the stomach, large intestine and heart appeared normal. The changes in the small intestine included haemorrhage and erosion of the mucosa. In addition, hepatorenal damage resulted from necrosis of liver cells and renal tubules. (Diwan et al.,2000).

The toxicity of diet containing 10% of Citrullus colocynthis fruits for rats treated for 6 weeks was determined. Dullness, ruffled hair, decreased body weight gains and feed efficiency, and enterohepatonephropathy characterised treatment with C. colocynthis alone. Diarrhoea was a prominent sign of C. colocynthis poisoning. (Al-Yahya et al., 2000).

Citrullus colocynthis extract was found to be free of hepatotoxic effects in concentrations up to 100 microg/ml incubation mixture when liver slices were incubated in William's medium E for 22 hours. All viability parameters used were not influenced by the extract of Citrullus colocynthis. Carbon tetrachloride induced hepatotoxicity could not be prevented or alleviated. Moreover, the damage was sometimes enhanced by higher extract concentrations. ( Barth et al.,2002)

Body weight loss, inefficiency of feed utilization, diarrhoea, and enterohepatonephropathy characterized C. colocynthis toxicosis in rats.( AL-Qarawi and  Adam, 2003).
