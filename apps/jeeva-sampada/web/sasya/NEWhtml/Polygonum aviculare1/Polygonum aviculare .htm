Taxonomic Details
Correct Name : Polygonum aviculare L.                                               
Family : polygonaceae
Synonyms : Polygonum littorale (Auct. pro parte),; Polygonum heterophyllum (Lindm.)
Common Names :
English:  Allseed, Beggar-weed, Bird's Knotgrass, Bird's Tongue, Bloodwort, Cow-grass, Hogweed, Iron-grass, Crab-grass, knot weed.
Sanskrit:  Miromati, Nisomali.
Hindi:  Bannalia, Bigbund, Hunraj,
Misc: Punjab: Banduke, Kesru.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Prostrate stems & branches with flowers throughout their length. Leaves nearly sessile, narrowely lanceolate, sometimes glandular dotted. Flowers small, green tipped with white or red , axillary clusters. Nutt 3 angled, minutely wrinkled.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
The flowers are hermaphrodite (have both male and female organs)

Breeding System :

Phenology : 
It will be in flower from June to October, and the seeds ripen from August to October.

 Pollinators: 
It is known to pollinated by insects.

Seed Biology :

Seed Germination:
Seed - sow spring in a cold frame. Germination is usually free and easy. When they are large enough to handle, prick the seedlings out into individual pots and plant them out in the summer if they have reached sufficient size. If not, overwinter them in a cold frame and plant them out the following spring after the last expected frosts.

Division in spring or autumn. Very easy, larger divisions can be planted out direct into their permanent positions. We have found that it is better to pot up the smaller divisions and grow them on in light shade in a cold frame until they are well established before planting them out in late spring or early summer.

Cultivation:
 Succeeds in an ordinary garden soil but prefers a moisture retentive not too fertile soil in sun or part shade. Repays generous treatment, in good soils the plant will cover an area up to a metre in diameter. Prefers an acid soil. Dislikes shade.

Knotweed is a common and invasive weed of cultivated ground. It is an important food plant for the caterpillars of many species of butterflies. It also produces an abundance of seeds and these are a favourite food for many species of birds. Plants seem to be immune to the predations of rabbits. The flowers have little or no scent or honey and are rarely visited by pollinating insects. Self-fertilization is the usual method of reproduction, though cross-fertilization by insects does sometimes occur. The plant also produces cleistogomous flowers - these never open and therefore are always self-fertilized. The plant is very variable and is seen by most botanists as an aggregate species of 4 very variable species, viz. - P. aviculare. L.; P. boreale. (Lange.)Small.; P. rurivacum. Jord. ex Box.; and P. arenastrum. Box.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used: 
Plant, root, Leaves , Seeds and Tea.

Uses

  1. Young leaves and plants - raw or cooked. Used as a pot herb, they are very rich in zinc. A nutritional analysis is available. The leaves are a tea substitute.

  2. Seed - raw or cooked. Rather small and fiddly to utilize, they can be used in all the ways that buckwheat (Fagopyrum esculentum) is used, either whole or dried and ground into a powder for use in pancakes, biscuits and Pinole. 

  3. In Chumba, the dried root is applied externally as an anodyne.
  4. In Kashmir, the seeds are used as an emetic & purgative.

Other Uses
  1. Knotweed is a safe and effective astringent and diuretic herb that is used mainly in the treatment of complaints such as dysentery and hemorrhoids. 
  2. It is also taken in the treatment of pulmonary complaints because the silicic acid it contains strengthens connective tissue in the lungs.
  3. The whole plant is anthelmintic, astringent, cardiotonic, cholagogue, diuretic, febrifuge, haemostatic, lithontripic and vulnerary. It was formerly widely used as an astringent both internally and externally in the treatment of wounds, bleeding, piles and diarrhoea. Its diuretic properties make it useful in removing stones. An alcohol-based preparation has been used with success to treat varicose veins of recent origin. The plant is harvested in the summer and early autumn and is dried for later use.
  4. The leaves are anthelmintic, diuretic and emollient.
  5. The whole plant is anthelmintic, antiphlogistic and diuretic.
  6. The juice of the plant is weakly diuretic, expectorant and vasoconstrictor. Applied externally, it is an excellent remedy to stay bleeding of the nose and to treat sores.
  7. The seeds are emetic and purgative.
  8. Recent research has shown that the plant is a useful medicine for bacterial dysentery. Of 108 people with this disease, 104 recovered within 5 days when treated internally with a paste of knotweed.
  9. Yields a blue dye that is not much inferior to indigo. The part used is not specified, but it is likely to be the leaves.
  10. Yellow and green dyes are obtained from the whole plant.
  11. The roots contain tannins.
