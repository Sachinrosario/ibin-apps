Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Oil palm is cultivated in the tropical regions of Africa, Asia and America. The oil palm is indigenous to West Africa. It occurs wild along the African coast and is common throughout the Congo basin and extends as far east as Uganda and Tanganyika. It is also found in British Guiana, Brazil, Peru, Venezuela and West Indies. It is cultivated extensively in Malaya and in Indonesia, particularly in Sumatra. The palm has spread from 16°N latitude in Senegal to 15°S in Angola and eastwards to the Indian Ocean, Zanzibar and Malagasy. Now introduced and cultivated throughout the tropics between 16°N and S latitudes. Sometimes grown as an ornamental, as in southern Florida. It was introduced into India nearly 20 years ago, and a few palms are found in the botanical gardens at Calcutta, Bombay, Poona, Baroda, Bangalore and other places. Some attempts have been made to cultivate the palm on a commercial scale in Travancore, now it is gaining economic importance in India (1).

Oil palm thrives in regions with an annual rainfall of 50-100 inch. Conditions of drought even for short periods affect the yield of fruits. It requires a humid climate and deep rich soil with a pH of 4-5.

Several varieties of the oil palm differing in the size and colour of the fruit, amount of pericarp and thickness of the seed shell have been recognised. Based on fruit characters, especially shell thickness, 4 economic varieties have been recognised:

(1) var. macrocarya (Congo type) with shell 50% of the fruit weight and pericarp, 0.75-2.5 mm. thick.

(2) Var. dura (Deli type) with shell about 30 % of the fruit weight and pericarp, 2-6 mm. thick

(3) Var. tenera (Liscombe type) with shell 10% of the fruit weight and pericarp of varying thickness and

(4) Var. pisifera with shell absent and fruit small (1).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A stately, erect, monoecious palm growing to a height of 20-80 ft. with a dense crown of pinnate leaves, 10-17 ft. long. It is a large feather palm having a solitary columnar stem with short internodes (2). It is unarmed except for short spines on the leaf base and within the fruit branch. The irregular set of leaflets on the leaf gives the palm its characteristic appearance. Spadices interfoliar, arising below the terminal bud, 6 or 8 at the same time, male ones always preceding the females by several weeks. Mature leaf paripinnate, up to 7.5 m long, petiole about a quarter of length of leaf, bearing spines, lower of which are formed from the base of fibres from leaf sheath and upper ones are more irregular and are modified small pinnae from which the vestigial laminae have been torn away leaving the midribs. Leaflets linear, 250-300 in number, inserted alternately in upper and lower ranks, longest in middle of leaf where about 120 cm long, swelling at base but with no motor function, semixerophytic, with thick cuticle and lignified tissue; stomata, about 145 per m2, confined to lower surface, guard cells thin-walled, closing at midday under conditions of water stress (8).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
An inflorescence primordium is produced in the axil of each leaf at the time of leaf initiation. Inflorescence reaches the central spear stage in two years and a further 9-10 months is required to flowering and anthesis. Each flower primordium is a potential producer of male and female organs, but one or the other usually remains rudimentary to produce either a male or female inflorescence. Hermaphrodite flowers and inflorescences are occasionally produced. The staminate inflorescence may consist of 200 spikelets with each spikelet bearing 700 to 1,200 florets. It may produce 3 ounces of pollen. The pollen is released over a 5-day period and most of it on the third day after flowering starts, the pistillate inflorescence may have as many spikelets but only five to 30 florets on each. The pistillate floret is larger than the staminate one and bears an ovoid or nearly cylindrical three-celled ovary. The florets take about a week to open, the individual floret being receptive 36 to 48 hours (3). The compound spike or spadix has a stout peduncle, 30-45 cm long and a central rachis with spirally arranged spikelets, 100-200 in number in different palms. The inflorescence is tightly enclosed in an outer and inner spathe until about one month after full emergence from base of leaf petiole and six weeks before anthesis, when outer spathe begins to open, inner spathe splitting 2-3 weeks later, both spathes then fray and disintegrate and inflorescence pushes its way out.

Male inflorescence has fingerlike spineless cylindrical spikes, 10-20 cm long, each with 700-1200 closely packed small male flowers sunk in tissues of rachis, each flower has a triangular bract, perianth of 6 minute segments, tubular androecium with 6 anthers, and rudimentary gynoecium with 3 projections corresponding to trilobed stigma.

Female inflorescence reaches a length of 30 cm or more before opening, female spikelets thick and fleshy in axils of spiny bract and with terminal spine of varying length, flowers arranged spirally in shallow cavities in rachis, with 12-30 flowers on central spikelets and 12 or less in lower and upper spikelets. Each female flower usually with a small male flower on either side, which normally abort, female flower with 2 bracteoles, 6 sepaloid tepals, 2 cm long, in 2 whorls, rudimentary androecium of 6-1 0 short projections, tricarpellary ovary, of which only one carpel usually develops, and sessile stigma of 3 heavy lobes. Hermaphrodite inflorescences occur occasionally, sometime between male and female inflorescence, and are common in young palms (8).

Breeding System :
Pollen must be transferred from the staminate clusters to the pistillate ones. There is no parthenogenetic development. Pisefera nuts are often partially sterile. The oil palm male and female inflorescences open at different times on the plant; thus, rarely is the plant self-fertilized. The palms, however, do not breed true owing to the normal occurrence of cross-pollination. Breeding of heavy yielding types with fruit containing a thick pericarp rich in oil, and high female to male sex ratio have been attempted. Attempts have also been made to select types with a 'dumpy' (low-growing) habit with a view to economise in the cost of harvesting (3). The total number of inflorescences produced per palm is dependent upon the number of leaves produced and the number of inflorescences which reach maturity without abortion. Abortion usually takes place when the inflorescence is 6-12 cm long and is just beginning to develop at a rapid rate 4-5 months before anthesis.

Phenology :
Oil palms start bearing after 4-6 years and takes 12-15 years for full bearing. After pollination, fruits require 6 months for ripening.

Pollinators:
The oil palm is obligatorily cross-pollinated. Both insects and wind contribute to pollen transfer. Since they produce large amount of pollen, flowers to be largely are wind pollinated and is observed that the pollen is distributed at least 55 feet from the original source. In Africa, specialists generally assign the principal pollinating role to insects, whereas in Asia wind pollination is considered most important (3).

Seed Biology :
The fruit is drupe, which is borne on a large compact bunch. Fruits sessile, 1-2 inch. long and 1-1.5 inches in diameter ovoid-oblong to obovoid with a pointed apex and clustered together in large heads, yellow, orange, red or shining black in colour, with a pulpy pericarp; each fruit contains usually 1, sometimes 2 or 3 seeds. Seeds hard shelled with whitish kernel (1).

Seedling Germination :
Naturally distributed seeds do not germinate readily and posses dormancy. A high temperature is required for satisfactory germination and stored seeds germinate more readily than fresh seeds. Although some form of heat treatment was used in West Africa, a germination of 25-60 per cent only was usually obtained until the physiological implications had been studied. The seed has certain moisture, temperature and oxygen requirements for germination. The temperature factor is satisfied by a heat pretreatment at a specified moisture content prior to germination, and the oxygen requirement is met by supplying the optimum moisture for germination after the seed has been brought to ambient temperature

On germination the thin layer of smaller endosperm cells above the embryo rupture and a disc of endosperm, testa, fibre plug and the germ-pore plate are extruded from the germ pore. The radicle then emerges, followed by the plumular projection. The lower end of the cotyledon remains in the seed and develops into an haustorium, which becomes convoluted along its long axis to provide a greater surface for absorption. After about three months, the spongy yellow haustorium has absorbed all the endosperm and completely fills the seed cavity. At this stage the seedling has produced three simple leaves and some adventitious roots (8).

Cultivation:

Propagation is from seeds specially collected from fresh ripe fruits. Seeds are sown in nursery beds and take 2-3 months to germinate. Seedlings are transplanted when 6-12 months old or 1-3 ft. high in prepared pits, 2 ft.X 2 ftX 2 ft., the distance between plants (triangular planting) being 29 or 30 ft. Green manure or catch crops may be grown to prevent soil wash. Pruning of spent up leaves is periodically carried out to facilitate harvesting of fruit-heads. Palms in bearing have to be periodically manured to ensure sustained yields. The dominant fertilizer requirement is phosphoric acid. Nitrogenous fertilizers may be added occasionally, potash fertilizers do not appear to produce any response. The palm begins to bear when 4-6 years old and reaches full bearing at 12-15 years. The yield gradually declines after the age of 30. The fruit takes 6 months for ripening after pollination. Artificial pollination has been attempted to secure regular and uniform crops, but as the ability of the palms to develop and sustain fruits is limited by its assimilation capacity, there is a risk of overstraining the palm by adopting such practices.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

pests:

Damage to oil palm caused by the beetles Oryctes rhinoceros L. and Rhynchophorus ferrugineus appears to be severe in some areas and is controlled by collecting and burning old leaves. Other important insects attacking oil palms in are Strategus aloeus, Setora nitens, Orthocraspeda trima, Pimelephila ghesquierei, Thosea asigna, Mahasena corbetti and Lansdownia bifenestralis. Important insects attacking oil palms in Africa are Oryctes monoceros.


Diseases:

Some of the most important diseases are nutritional in origin. Mineral deficiencies are generally followed by secondary invasions of species of Pestalotia, Helminthosporium, Helicosporium etc. Bud and crown rots affecting the palm produce symptoms reminiscent of those of the coconut palm caused by Phytophthora palmivora. The most serious disease of the oil palm is wilt (Fusarium oxysporum). Other diseases affecting the oil palm are Cercospora leaf disease (Cercospora elaeidis), Trunk rots caused by (Armillaria mellea and Ganoderma lucidum) Stem rot due to Fomes noxius, Bunch rot caused by Marasmium palmivora and Blast disease of the root in which (Pythlum splendens and Rhizoctonia lamellifera) appear to be causally involved (4).

Economic Potential and Uses

Two kinds of oil are obtained from this palm, Palm oil and Palm kernel oil. Palm oil is extracted from the fleshy mesocarp of the fruit which contains 45-55% oil which varies from light yellow to orange-red in colour, and melts from 25° to 50°C. Palm oil is extensively used for edible purposes. Palm oil used in the manufacture of vanaspathi, margarines. It is also used in cooking, shortening formulations, confectionery, bakery products and in the preparation of ice creams. The high grade oils are used for the production of margarine and vegetable shortening. A large part of the oil is used in the manufacture of soaps and candles. In the tin plating industry, the oil is employed as a finishing flux to assist in the distribution of tin evenly over the iron plate surface and as a protective coating during the process of cooling. It has been used as a fuel for internal combustion engines, in the manufacture of lubricants and in the cold reduction process of making sheet steel. Palm oil is a rich source of carotene and appears to be the only oil of vegetable origin, which can serve as a potent source of vitamin A, comparable to the better grades of cod liver oil and about 10 times richer than butter. It can be used in place of cod liver oil wherever a deficiency of vitamin A has to be corrected. Palm kernel oil is extracted from the kernel of endosperm, and contains about 50% oil. Similar to coconut oil, with high content of saturated acids, mainly lauric, it is solid at normal temperatures in temperate areas, and is nearly colourless, varying from white to slightly yellow. This non-drying oil is used in edible fats, in making ice cream and mayonnaise, in baked foods and confectioneries, and in the manufacture of soaps and detergents. Press cake, after extraction of oil from the kernels, used as livestock feed, containing 5-8% oil. The principal uses of fatty acids in palm oil are in the manufacture of metal stearates, fatty amines, esters and fatty alcohols. Palm oil can be used in the fermentation substrate for the production of protein and antibiotics. Palm oil and palm acid oil is also used in animal feed (5,9, 10, 11, 13).

Parts used

Uses

The sap ferments quickly, and is an important source of Vitamin B complex in diet of people of West Africa. Leaves used for thatching, petioles and rachices for fencing and for protecting the tops of retid walls. Refuse after stripping the bunches used for mulching and manuring, ash sometimes used in soap-making (10). The apical bud or cabbage is used as vegetable. The mid ribs of the leaflets and leaf stalks are used for making baskets and brooms, while the fibre from the base of the leaves is used for stuffing cushions. The shells of the nuts are carved into ornamental objects. They are used also as fuel. The shell charcoal, like coconut charcoal, possesses decolourising and gas adsorbent properties. The toddy collected by tapping the stalk of the immature inflorescence or the apical bud of the palm is used in West Africa for making arrack or sugar. The sap contains about 4.3 g/100 ml of sucrose and 3.4 g/100 ml of glucose (1). Palm oil and olein, because of the oxidative stability, are widely used in filled milk and liquid coffee whitener. Palm oil on its own or blended with palm kernel oil is also used as a fat. phase in non-dairy ice cream for chocolate coating for ice cream lollies and bricks, and in sandwich cream. Hydrogenated palm oil and palm stearin are also used in dried soups and powder mixes (13).

Harvesting Practices

Plantation practice varies but frequently a team of a cutter, who cuts the bunches from the palms, using in young palms a short pole and chisel and in older palms a bamboo pole and curved picking knife, and a gatherer who collects the fallen bunches and loose fruit. Tall and wild trees must be climbed. Using a machete the climber hacks through the thick stems close to the trunk and lets the heavy bunches fall to the ground. They are usually harvested when 5 -8 fruits fall from the bunch. The time of harvesting is critical since both the oil content and the free fatty acid the oil increase with ripeness. Hence, harvesting under-ripe fruits results in reduced yield whilst over-ripeness results in an oil of impaired quality. Ripeness is indicated by a change in colour from purple or deep violet to orange red, the apex only retaining its dark purplish colour. Processing should not be delayed as the bumping involved in harvesting intiates enzyyme activity resulting in high free fatty acid content (4).

The fruits are harvested when they are fully ripe and the oil content of the pericarp is at its maximum. The cultivated palms yield 4,000 nuts per bearing palm has been reported from experimental plots in South Kanara (1).

After harvesting, the separation of fruits from the bunches is carried out by repeated shocks of the bunches against the wall and the shock energy should be enough to break the binding resistance of the fruit to bunch. Shocks are given inside units called threshers. These are horizontal cages of large diameter and length which rotate at slow speed. The sterilised bunches are dropped inside at one end. Then, they are carried upwards by rotation from where they fall down by gravity. A suitable device is pushing forward the bunches, drop after drop, till they reach the other end of the cage from where they are discharged. The cages are manufactured with inner bars which spacing allows the separated fruits to drop out from the cage into a conveyor while preventing the passage of the stalks (12).

Produce and Products

Palm oil and palm kernels represent approximately 20-24% and 2-4% of the harvested fruit bunch, respectively. Therefore, vast quantities of palm press fiber (PPF) and highly polluting effluents are produced annually as byproducts of the palm oil industry. Palm press fiber is characterized by low protein, high lignin and low available energy contents. It is mostly used as fuel for boilers or as manure. The effluents of palm oil extraction can be used as substrates for the production of good quality microbial proteins. Oil palm nutshells through destructive distillation can produce high quality activated carbon, creosote, phenol, methanol and acetic acid (5).

The percentage of component fatty acids in the oil is: Myristic 0.5-5.9, Palmitic 32.3-47.0, Stearic 1.0-8.5, Oleic, 39.8-52.4 and Linoleic acid 2.0-11.3.

The component glycerides are: oleodipalmitins 45%, palmitodioleins 30%, oleopalmitostearins 10%, linoleodioleins 6-8% and fully saturated glycerides, tripalmitin and dipalmitostearin 6-8% (11, 13).

Processing Techniques

Palm oil

Semi-commercial scale processing methods are divisible into 2 groups.

  1. Manual processes, unaided by any mechanical appliances, such as hand sqeezing, ‘soft oil’ process and ‘hard oil’ process.

  2. Machine assisted processes like cage press and hydraulic hand press.


Of the manual methods may be mentioned the ‘soft oil’ process in which:

  1. The pulp is softened by boiling the fruits in water and then pounding them to disintegrate the pulp.

  2. The mass is then treated with a large volume of water, the pulp squeezed, the oily layer skimmed off and heated strongly to expel water and separate the oil from the suspended matter (conditions are unfavourable for the action of fat splitting enzymes and only small amounts of free fatty acids are present).


The ‘hard oil’ process:

  1. Softening of the pulp by fermentation in wooden troughs.

  2. Fermentation takes place in successive stages alternating with moisturizing and pounding for several days.

  3. Oil mixed with water and vegetable tissues drains out and is collected, boiled with water and the oil skimmed off. Considerable amounts of free fatty acids are formed leading to the formation of ‘hard’ palm oil.


Machine assisted processing methods are divided into the following steps:

1. Sterilization of the fruit bunches- treatment with steam or boiled in water, to destroy enzymes responsible for deterioration of the oil.

2. Separation of the fruit from the bunch by hand or mechanically.

3. Digestion of the fruit- which consists of stirring the fruit while maintaining a high temperature, renders the mass homogenous, detaches the pulp from the nuts and facilitates kernel removal.

4. Recovery of oil using centrifugation or by expression. Pressure may be discontinuous or continuous. Solvent extraction and water displacement are not often applied in modern palm fruit processing.

5. Clarification- general methods of processing the palm are largely composed of basic processes of neutralizing, earth bleaching and heat bleaching in various combinations.

6. For edible purposes the oil is deodorized by passing steam through it.


Palm kernel oil

(1) In West African villages the traditional method of cracking the nut with a stone is now being replaced by mechanical nutcrackers.

(2) On plantations of the East Indies and Malaysia, decortication is carried out by machines. Two types-

(i) Nuts are fed to the centre of the rotor and flung out against a heavy steel housing and broken by impact

(ii) A special type of hammer mill.

(3) After cracking, the nuts are dropped into rotary screens where some separation is obtained. Two methods of separation (i) dry method using inclined belts provided with sharp projections which move continuously upwards (kernels roll down and shells are caught on projections) (ii) brine method where the kernels, float, and the shells sink)

(4) Kernels are cleaned by passing over screens and magnetic separator

(5) Kernels are ground in a hammer mill or preferably crushed between flaking rollers (4).

Other Uses

Oil is used as a liniment for indolent tumors. Reported to be anodyne, antidotal, aphrodisiac, diuretic, and vulnerary, oil palm is a folk remedy for cancer, headaches, and rheumatism (10). The pulp residue after palm oil extraction is used locally as fuel (5).

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

In India palm oil is becoming popular as an edible oil. Interest in growing oil palms in the country began in the sixties with concern over the amount of vegetable oil being imported of which oil palm formed a sizeable chunk. In actual extraction it has been found that a fifth of the weight of the fruits can be got as oil-palm and two per cent more from the kernel. With such properties, the oil palm is said to have the highest yield of all oil-bearing plants. Oil is used in cooking, shortening formulations, confectionery, bakery products and in the preparation of ice creams. Industrially it is used in the manufacture of soaps and candles. We are unable to achieve self sufficiency. Demand for oil are met through imports. Self-sufficiency can be achieved in terms of higher output, cost and higher yields, through scientific and research. oriented management of plantations.

Miscellaneous Information

Commercial plantations of Oil Palm in India

The cultivation of Oil Palm on a plantation scale was attempted in India in the forest lands on the basis of feasibility studies conducted by Oil Palm experts from abroad. Two plantations were accordingly set up in the public sector on a commercial scale, one in Kerala and the other in Little Andamans (6).

Oil Palm plantations in Kerala: A large scale planting of Oil Palm was launched from 1971 to 1982 in Kerala covering an area of 3645 ha with imported seeds from Malaysia, Nigeria, Papua New Guinea and Cote de Ivoire. The plantation units were established on deforested land where the soil is lateritic and shallow. The area is also characterised by having a prolonged dry spell of not less than four months in a year. Though the initial performance of the plantations was far below the expectations, the yield level was brought up to 2.7 tonnes of oil per ha in certain areas of plantation units. However, on the whole, the present average yield is below one tonne of oil per ha per year. It should be possible to increase the yield if proper manuring soil and moisture conservation and summer irrigation are adopted in all the plantation units. There are presently two processing units for the extraction of crude palm oil of which one is of two tonnes capacity. Since these processing units are outmodern and not adequate to process the available FFB. efforts are being made to install a modern mill of 20 tonnes FFB/hour capacity.


Oil Palm plantations in Little Andamans: The Andaman Forest and Plantation Development Corporation raised Oil Palm in 1563 ha in Little Andamans between 1976 and 1985 with the planting materials received from Nigeria, Malaysia, Cote de Ivoire, Papua New Guinea and Zaire. These plantations were initially not managed properly. Consequently, the average productivity obtained was very low. Recently, one processing unit of 4.5 tonnes/hour capacity with screw press was put up for ensuring timely processing. There is good scope for getting at least 20 tonnes FFB/ha/year from these plantations if managed well.


Oil Palm in Karnataka: A small scale plantation of 4.5 ha of Oil Palm was planted by Forest Department of Karnataka at Sampaje Reserve Forest near Sulliya hills (Dakshin Kannada) during 1969 with dura population. The palms nearer to water source had good size bunches. However, no attempt was made to manage the plantation and left as forest crop (6).
