Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
A large deciduous tree, distributed  throughout the sub-Himalayan valley up to 1,200 m in Assam, West Bengal, Bihar, South India and the Andamans.

In Indonesia, Falcata is famous with the local name 'Sengon Laut' that grow alongside Java island, South Sumatra, South Celebes, Mollucas islands and Papua.

In Estates planted, along streams, bunds of fileds, outskirts of Wet Deciduous forests in lower ghat, dry deciduous forests, Slopes, moist deciduous forest.

Found in Malayasia, India, S.E.Asia, Sri Lanka, Indo-Malaysia, South-East Asia.




Availability :  
Distributed in Bengal , Punjab, UP, Assam to Burma ; it lies scattered in Panchmahals, M.P, Mysore, Kerala, Eastern Andhra , Andamans often cultivated.
Natural range is in Tropical southeastern Asia.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Tree up to 20 m tall. Leaves with 4-14 pairs of pinnae, leaflets 20-30 pairs per pinna, asymmetrical with the midrib strongly eccentric near one of the margins, 6-10 mm long, 2-3 mm wide, apex acute, with a nectary on the rachis just below each pair of pinnae, stipules up to 1.5 cm long and 3 cm wide. Flowers 8-12 mm long (central one larger), in heads 20-25 mm in diameter (excluding stamens), these usually in wide terminal panicles, sometimes reduced to 3-4 peduncles clustered in the leaf axils; corolla white; stamens up to 30 mm long.  Pods flat, 10-17 cm long, 1.7-3.5 cm wide, indehiscent. Seeds ellipsoid, laterally flattened, ca. 7 mm long and 4-5 in diameter, distinctly median (remote from sutures), with a small basal pleurogram ca. 1 mm in diameter."

Phenology :

The tree is evergreen, in North India it is deciduous, the leaves commencing to fall about January, shriveling up and falling by single leaflets or by whole pinnae.  The trees are usually leafless in February-March, the new leaves appearing in March early April.

The masses of fragrant feathery yellowish white flowers appear from April to June amongst the rich green of the new foliage, and the tree is then particularly handsome.  The pods while developing are pale reddish green; they commence ripening about November-December and continue hanging in quantity through the leafless season.  They commence falling in  by about March and continue falling during the hot weather months, being blown to some distance from the trees.  A few old empty pods may sometimes be found on the trees as late as September.


Pollinators:

Seed Biology :
Best time for collection of pods off the trees by lopping branches is January.  Seed weight is variable; it is reported as 60,000/kg by some and 31,000 by some researchers, pods are dried in sun, then threshed and winnowed to get clean seed.  Seeds can be stored for atleast one year, in gunny bags, till required for sowing.

Presowing treatment is not necessary; however, 48hr soaking in water hastens germination.  Germination commences in about 2 months and is complete in 6 months.  Germination capacity is 50%.
Seedling Germination :
Through Seed.

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Wood, Bark, seeds

Uses

  • A. chinensis yields an insoluble gum which is used in Nepal for sizing paper. The plant extract is reported to have piscicidal and oxytonic properties.
  • The bark yields 22% tannin, its infusion is used as a lotion for cuts, scabies and other skin diseases.

Other Uses

  1. Sapwood is large, white, heartwood is brown, soft not very durable. It is used for box-making especially tea-boxes, packaging cases and for matches. It is also used for planking, dugouts, well-curbs, small turnery articles, combs, spoons, yokes and agricultural implements.
  2. The tree is extensively cultivated for shade  in coffee and tea-gardens.     
  3. The seeds and leaflets are fairly toxic to melon worm larvae and cotton stainer adults. The petioles are toxic to melon worm only. Leaves  and twigs are lopped for fodder.  
  4. Important browse tree in hilly areas; also grown for shade on plantations and for the manurial value of the fallen leaves. The branches are used for fodder at the end of the growing season when the leaves are past their succulent stage. Leaves in the early stages may be toxic to animals as they contain saponin, and prolonged feeding even of mature leaves may cause toxic symptoms.
  5. Wood yields 47.8-49% pulp by the sulphate process. It is suitable for making writing and printing paper after it is mixed with suitable long-fibred pulp.
