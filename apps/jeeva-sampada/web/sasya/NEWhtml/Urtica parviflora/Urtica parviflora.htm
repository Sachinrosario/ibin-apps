Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:
Perennial crop. It is not frost tender found in the temperate Himalayas from Kashmir to Sikkim, in Darjeeling, West Bengal, Mishmi hills in Arunachal Pradesh and in the Nilgiri hills in the South.Forests and amongst taller herbaceous vegetation, 1700 - 2800 metres.Found in Woodland, Sunny Edge, Dappled Shade, Shady Edge.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A slender, sparingly branched perennial, up to 3 m. tall and copiously armed with stiff stinging hairs, abundantly found in the temperate Himalayas from Kashmir to Sikkim, in Darjeeling in West Bengal, Mishmi hills in Arunachal Pradesh, and in the Nilgiri hills in the South. Stems obtusely angled; leaves ovate or ovate-cordate or lanceolate; flowers small, monoecious, green, clustered on lax axillary cymes; achenes small with persistent sepals.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used:
Leaves, roots, stem and inflorescence.

Uses: 

  • Young leaves - cooked and eaten as a green vegetable, A very nutritious food, high in vitamins and minerals, it makes an excellent spinach substitute and can also be added to soups and stews. Only use the young leaves and wear stout gloves when harvesting them to prevent getting stung. Although the fresh leaves have stinging hairs, thoroughly drying or cooking them destroys these hairs. 
  • The formation of galls on the leaves, petioles and stems of Urtica  parviflora (apparently mistaken for U. dioica in the original) by Puccinia caricis Rebent. var. himalayensis Barclay apud Padwick & Azmatullah Khan is common in Naini Tal just after the onset of rains. The infected parts of the plant are deformed and very much hypertrophied; they become soft and yellowish green. These hypertrophied portions of the plant are sweet and delicious; they are called Sishun kakri and are relished by the hill-tribes. 
  • The plant is sometimes fed to cattle, though cases of dermatitis have been reported  The roots are employed for the treatment of fractures and dislocations. 
  • The leaves of Urtica  hyperborea Jacquem. ex Wedd. (LADAKH - Latud, stokpo), a low, tufted herb, occurring in the alpine regions of the central and eastern Himalayas, are eaten as a potherb .
  • Tender shoots cooked as vegetable.
  • Nettle beer is brewed from the young shoots.

Harvesting Practices
The crop is susceptible to lodging, if the rain is followed by wind; lodged plants strike roots from the nodes and such plants are difficult to uproot or cut while harvesting. The crop is ready for harvesting in 75 days. Harvesting is done on clear days; if there is a likelihood of rains, the harvested crop is heaped up and then threshed in the sunshine. Threshing is done on a clear sunny day under the feet of bullocks. After winnowing the seed is ready for consumption.
Storeage: The grain can be and is often stored for years for use during scarcity. It does not suffer much damage in storage.
Yeild: The crop is largely grown for grain in the Panch Mahals (8,300 ha.; 8,900 tonnes) and Banas Kantha (7,800 ha.; 5,600 tonnes) districts of Gujarat as a kharif crop. Besides these districts, it is also grown on a small scale in Sabar Kantha, Mehsana and Vadodara districts.

Other Uses
Compost; Dye; Fibre; Hair; Liquid feed; Repellent.
  1. The leaves and inflorescences are prescribed as tonic and as a cleaning agent after parturition. A decoction of the herb is given in fevers. The stems of this plant also yield fiber which is used for ropes. It is said to be superior to that of jute in tensile strength and length of staple. However, there seems to be confusion between the fibres of the Himalayan hemp and this nettle 
  2. stem yields fiber, roots used for the treatment of fractures and dislocations, leaves and inflorescences are prescribed as tonic and as a cleaning agent after parturition
  3. A strong flax-like fiber is obtained from the stems. Used for making string and cloth, it also makes a good quality paper. It is harvested as the plant begins to die down in early autumn and is retted before the fibers are extracted. 
  4. The plant matter left over after the fibers have been extracted are a good source of biomass and have been used in the manufacture of sugar, starch, protein and ethyl alcohol. 
  5. An oil obtained from the seeds is used as an illuminant. 
  6. An essential ingredient of 'QR' herbal compost activator. This is a dried and powdered mixture of several herbs that can be added to a compost heap in order to speed up bacterial activity and thus shorten the time needed to make the compost. 
  7. The leaves are also an excellent addition to the compost heap and they can be soaked for 7 - 21 days in water to make a very nutritious liquid feed for plants. This liquid feed is both insect repellent and a good foliar feed. 
  8. The growing plant increases the essential oil content of other nearby plants, thus making them more resistant to insect pests. Although many different species of insects feed on nettles, flies are repelled by the plant so a bunch of freshly cut stems has been used as a repellent in food cupboards. 
  9. The juice of the plant, or a decoction formed by boiling the herb in a strong solution of salt, will curdle milks and thus acts as a rennet substitute. This same juice, if rubbed into small seams of leaky wooden tubs, will coagulate and make the tub watertight again. A hair wash is made from the infused leaves and this is used as a tonic and antidandruff treatment.
  10. A beautiful and permanent green dye is obtained from a decoction of the leaves and stems. A yellow dye is obtained from the root when boiled with alum.
  11. The leaves are used as a source material for the manufacture of copper chlorophyll, which was found to be exceptionally suitable for toilet and cosmetic preparations.

Miscellaneous Information

The leaves of the plants have stinging hairs, causing irritation to the skin. This action is neutralized by heat so the cooked leaves are perfectly safe and nutritious. However, only young leaves should be used because older leaves develop gritty particles called cystoliths which act as an irritant to the kidneys.
