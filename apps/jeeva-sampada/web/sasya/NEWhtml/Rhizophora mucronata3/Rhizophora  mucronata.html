Other Biological Details
HABIT & HABITAT

An  evergreen tree,  occurring all  along the tidal shores and creeks of India , Burma and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. 

More tolerant of sandy and firmer ground than other Rhizophoras, R. mucronata grows well in soft mud too and is believed to be among the few that can survive complete daily inundation.

Estimated to range from Tropical Moist to Rain through Subtropical Moist to Rain Forest Life Zones, Asiatic mangrove is estimated to tolerate annual precipitation of 10 to 80 dm, annual temperature of 20 to 26°C, and pH of 6.0 to 8.5.  Of the Asian species, this one is most likely to be found in deep soft mud.

Availability :  
Old World tropics from South and East Africa to Madagascar, Seychelles, Mauritius, southeastern Africa to southern China, found throughout Malaysia to north-eastern Australia, Melanesia and Micronesia. Not widely introduced in Hawaii

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

Evergreen tree 25(–30) m high, 70 cm in diameter, with numerous branching arching stilt roots. Bark brown or blackish, smooth with horizontal fissures. Leaves opposite, elliptical to oblong, 8–15 cm long, 5–10 cm wide, acute, entire, without visible veins, thick and leathery, glabrous, black-dotted beneath. Petiole 3–5 cm long. Stipules paired, leaving ring scar. Flower clusters axillary, 2–3 times forked, with 3–8 flowers ca 15 mm long. Bell-shaped hypanthium with 4 pale yellow, pointed leathery sepals and 4 cream-colored petals 9 mm long. Stamens 8, stalkless, anthers 6–8 mm long, 4 opposite sepals and 4 opposite petals. Ovary half-inferior, conical, 2-celled, with 2 ovules in each cell, 2-lobed style. Berry ovoid or conical, 5–7 cm long, brown, leathery. Seed 1, viviparous, becoming cigar-shaped, to 40 cm long and 2 cm in diameter.
Chromosome no.:(2n = 36)

Seed Biology :

Seedling Germination :
The tree is a prolific and early seed bearer, the seeds germinate when the fruit is still on the tree. Hypocotyl is dart-like and develops into a long pendent radicle. On falling from the tree, the seedling establishes itself in shallow water, the sharp point of the radicle penetrating into the mud. The seedlings are buoyant and if not immediately established.

Cultivation:
Unlike some other mangrove trees, new growths from Rhizophora trees only emerge from branch tips and not the trunk. So they can be killed by excessive collection of branches for firewood or other uses.

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Wood, bark, fruit

Uses

  • Bark contains 25-36% tannins used  in leather industry. The tannin is  astringent, and also used for the treatment of haemorrhage, haematuria and angina. 
  • Leaves, unripe and ripe fruits also contain 9.1%,  12.0%  and  4.2%  of  tannin respectively.  A black to chestnut dye is obtained from the leaves.
  • Bark is said to be used to give a chacolate dye; it is also used in tanning.  This tan recommends to be used as a preliminary preparation for cheap leathers.  The leather is only half prepared in India and exported to Europe in that condition, to be redone and have the colour improved by myrabolans or other tanning materials.  Mangrove bark has been exported to Europe, but leather prepared with it solely is always inferior in colour and quality.  Except, therefore, as a preliminary tan, or in the preparation of cheap leathers it is not likely to become an article of European trade.  Instead of the bark, which is bulky, an extract should be prepared for the purpose of exportation.  It would, however, have to be made in an earthen vessel, since any contact with iron would make the leather prepared by the extract, brittle and discoloured.

Chemistry
Wood contains 4.4% resin, 63.4% cellulose and 1.5% ash  . Tannin may vary in dry bark from about 13–50%, leaves contain 9.1%, green fruits 12.0%, and ripe fruits 4.2%. Spent mangrove bark, after tannin extraction, can be used as a source of furfural . Spent bark from North Borneo yields an ash assaying 18% lime (70% CaCO3).

Back to Top


Processing Techniques
Bark is stripped from the tree and is grounded or shredded, extracted with hot water and  the extract evaporated in  film evaporator, and finally dried to a solid. Solid extract can also be prepared by boiling the powdered bark in copper extractors and evaporating the extract to a dark red solid.

Back to Top

Other Uses
  1. The timber is heavy, difficult to saw and not durable unless it is dried for a long time. But it is used for construction, to make fish traps,  pilings and poles. It also used in heavy construction, bridges, buffers, brake blocks, tool handles, post in huts, reinforcing mud-walls, and for frames of houses. Fuel wood, yields high quality charcoal. Pulp from the wood is suitable for blotting and corrugating paper.
  2. Fruits are sweet;  fruit juice is made into a light wine.   Fruits may be eaten, after scraping off the skin and boiling with wood ashes.
  3. Young shoots are cooked and eaten as vegetables.
  4.   Pulp from the wood is suitable for making  blotting and corrugating paper.
  5. They are planted along coastal fish ponds to stabilise the banks.
  6. It is used as an astringent and to treat angina, haemorrhaging (extracts from the seedlings in Indochina); diarrhoea (China, Japan): diabetes, dysentery, hematuria. A poultice of the leaves are used to relief armoured fish stings. Old leaves and roots are used during childbirth (Malay). Bark is used to treat blood in the urine (Burma).
