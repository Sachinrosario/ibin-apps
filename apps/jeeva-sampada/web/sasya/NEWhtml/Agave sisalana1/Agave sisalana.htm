Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Sisal is a perennial, short-stemmed herb. It originated in Mexico. In India it is cultivated in Andhra Pradesh, Assam, West Bengal, Bihar, Orissa, Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra and Karnataka. Sisal is drought resistant and grams well where many other plants fail.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Sisal plant is upto 120 cm tall. The bole or stem is upto 20 cm in diameter and bears a rosette of dark green, thick fleshy rigid leaves 10-15 cm in length and 10-15 cm in width at the broadest portion. The leaves are generally without spine. Flowers are yellow in colour. Bulbils and suckers are present.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Sisal is a perennial monocarp because it flowers only once during a life of several years after which it dies. The inflorescence or pole is a terminal panicle. The sisal flowers are epigynous, yellow and have a regular perianth divided to six narrow segments; united at the base of corolla and have versatile 2-lobed speckled anthers supported or long thin copper coloured filaments. The style is awl-shaped and has 3 lobed stigma. The anthers are protoandrous and protrude about 6 cm from the flower. The stamens dehisce and shed their pollen 2-3 days before the style has become fully elongated, and its stigma has produced a sticky exudate and becomes receptive. The style soon withers after pollination. (1)

The fruit or seed capsule is as big as a walnut being 3 cm long and 2 cm in diameter. It is green fleshy during the formative stage, but dries out, turning black on ripening. Fertile seeds are thin, triangular-rounded, black and papery, while the unfertilized ovules remain white and papery. There are often more than three hundred ovules in a fruit, but only half of these may be fertilized.(1) It shows polygonum type of embryo sac and nuclear endosperm.(2)

Breeding system and varietal improvement:

Phenology: Sisal flowers only once during a life of several years after which it dies, the fruit ripens about six months after the pollination of the flowers.(2)


Pollinators:Pollination is effected by flying insects, esp. bees which love the nectar and mob the pollen or else by wind carrying the yellow, dusty and strong smelling pollen downwards to the exposed stigmas on a lower branch.(2)

MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:

Sisal is fortunately is not plagued with many insect pests or diseases.

Pests:
Sisal weevil (Scyphoporus interstitialis Gyth). Is the only major insect part of Sisal. Weevil damage to sisal plant is attributable to the adult or to the larva. The commonest sign of an attack by adult weevils is the presence of brownish-grey speckled patches, usually elliptical or rounded form 3-10 cms in length, on the under surfaces of the leaves, mostly in the proximal half. There is no doubt that the gravest losses inflicted by the weevil are those that occur in young sisal as a result of subterranean damage arising out of larval feeding. Losses of plants in young sisal fields due to larval feeding may amount to 60% or more, in the aggregate during a bad year.

The best course to adapt for controlling weevil is to carry out sanitation measures and this implies the destruction of breeding sites such as senescent plants and rotting boles. Another favourite haunt of the adults is in green poling plants and spraying these plants will help keep down the population of weevils. Certain insecticides such as aldrin and dieldrin which persist in the soil for long periods have been effectively used in the preventive measures directed against egg-laying in the basis of the sisal plants.

Round reddish scale insects, Aonidiella orientalis (New St.) and Aonidiella andersoni have been recorded on sisal leaves causing pin-point depression in the epidermis but of no economic importance. A species of Lepidosaphes sp.n., or mussel scale, which is a light cinnamon-brown slipper-shaped scale, about 2mm long also is common on sisal leaves but is not serious. Certain wild animals and some kinds of vermin can wreck have in sisal plantations, particularly in young fields. Male rats (Heliophobius spalax) grow bole of plant from below. Porcupines (Hystix galeata) scoop out the tops of boleu of half grown plants, destroying entire meristem. Locusts nibble the margins of sisal leaves, usually near the tip end. Snouts (Gastropoda sp.) rasp off epidermis in young plants.(2)

Diseases:

Bole rest of sisal is caused by the black spored fungus Aspergillus niger var Teigh. Bole rot is a fatal disease of sisal, and once a plant is infected it cannot be saved. The control measure is to avoid cutting during wet weather as far as is practicable. Botryodiplodia theobromae Pat., a common fungus has been found in sisal leaves and is responsible for rolling of boles of agave hybrids. Anthracnose due to the fungus Colletotrichum agaves Cav. may occur on any part of a sisal plant, although not serious.

Other fungi such as Cucurbitaria agaves, Hypocarpa austroamericana (Speg.) sacc. var. agavis-americanae Speg., Microdiplodia agaves (Niessl) on leaves and a Podospora sp. In stumps and Fusarium solani hasalso been reported.

Sun-scarch of sisal leaves is a condition resulting from the bursting of the chlorophyll-bearing cells of the epidermis, after which they collapse on to the peripheral fibers. Sun-scorch is not infectious and cannot be transmitted to healthy plants. Occasionally, sisal growing on exceptionally fertile land, or some types of termite wounds, has drooping leaves which bend over in the distal half as if the plants were growing under shade.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
fiber

Leaf fiber is used for making twines, ship cordage, webbing, sacking. It is used as a substitute for jute in making mats, rugs, sacks for coffee, wagon covers, floor coverings, making brushes. In Kenya it is used for polishing cloth, polishing metal components prior to plating and painting.

PULP

Used for making heavy duty multi wall bagging, currency notes, tear and scuff resistant papers, stencil paper, tea-bag paper, kraft paper, paper board, cheap twine and upholstery.

FOOD AND FEED

The edible inner stem of agaves is boiled and eaten by poor people during famine. In some countries the stem is tapped for the sweetish juice which is converted into an alcoholic drink by fermentation. Pulque feer of spaniards is prepared by fermenting flower sap.

The inner stem of sisal is a favourite food for pigs.

Parts Used:

Leaves, stem, flower


Harvesting Practices:
An agave leaf is ready for harvest when it is mature enough to have attained an essentially horizontal position. In Africa, first cutting is generally in third year. The leaves are cut at the base by hand with a machete. The first cutting is generally done about two and a half to three years after planting. Thereafter successive crops of 25 to 50 or more leaves can be harvested once or twice a year. The sisal plant lives for ten years. Leaves are cut at the base by hand with a machete. At least 25 leaves should be left after the first cut and 16 to 18 leaves at subsequent cut. Over cutting results in the production of smaller leaves with reduced fiber yield and also entails higher harvesting cost. The green leaves weigh between 500 to 700g each and contain between two and five percent fiber, or about 1000 individual fibers. After the removal of the terminal spine, the leaves are tied into bundles of 30 and transported.

Produce and Products:


Chemistry:
Sisal fiber has a high percentage of cellulose (72.0%) and a relatively high proportion of lignin (14.5%) (Matthews, 1947); waxes-2%; Hemicellulose, pectin and other carbohydrates - 10%, ash - 1%. The ash of sisal fiber is mainly CaCO3, K and Mg salts and some Si.

The pentosan content of sisal fiber constitutes a drawback to use in the production of dissolving pulp for rayon manufacture and the yield of 90% pure L-hemicellulose pulp is only about 60%, compared with 91% L-cellulose in dissolving wood pulp.

Processing Techniques:
The fibers are extracted from the remainder of the leaf tissues by decortication. Decortication is now done entirely by machinery. In this process, a rapidly revolving bluntly bladed wheel cleans one end of the leaves against a breakplate with limited clearances as they are passed by it while held in a moving rope or other grip by the undecorticated end of the leaves. If adjusted correctly and the leaf sizes are uniform, the machine will completely free the fibers from other leaf tissues on the first pass. The next step involves drying the fibers by placing them on racks in the sun or by centrifugation followed by dehydration in an artificial dryer, or sun-drying. Proper drying is of great importance, since the quality of the fiber prodcued depends largely on its moisture content. Artificial drying has been found to result in generally better grades of fiber. The dry fibers are the machine combed and sorted into various grades, largely on the basis of the previous separation of the leaves into size gro8ups in the field. Short and broken fiber are removed and classified as tow.(3)
Other uses:
Sisal waste from leaves yield wax, sodium pretate, furfurqal, sap from inflorescence stalk contains hecogenin, a source material for the synthesis of cortisone. Waste pulp from processing is returned to the field as fertilizer.

Leaf juice may be used as an abortifacient. A row of Agave plants also makes an impenetrable living fence.(4)

Miscellaneous Information

 Plant regeneration and establishment via somatic embryogenesis in cultures of immature leaves from in vitro grown multiple shoots of Agave sisalona Perr ex. Engelm are described. Somatic embryogenesis is now being vigorously pursued for crop improvement because it ensures fidelity among regenerants by efficient cloning at low cost and less time; also allows efficient plant transformation, somatic hybridization, in vitro selection, production of artificial seeds and important secondary metabolites in vitro and studying various stages of somatic embryogenesis at the most fundamental level (de Jong et al., 1997; Thorpe, 1988; Parrot et al., 1995; Dudrils et al., 1991).

The prospects of genetic improvement in A. sisalana by conventional plant breeding techniques appear almost nil (Nikam 1997).(5)
