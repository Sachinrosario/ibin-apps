Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT                                                                                                    

Nutmeg is the seed of is an evergreen slow growing, branching, nutmeg tree with a shallow root system. Branches appear in spatially arranged whorls in the main stem and grow outwards. The entire tree has cone like appearance. Growth of the main stem and branches is intermittent. Two types of branches viz. plageotropic fruiting branches and orthotropic erect growing branches are present in a tree. Usually male and female trees are separate. Nutmeg is grown either as an intercrop in old coconut gardens or as a monocrop. Mace of commerce is the dried aril and the nutmeg is the dried kernel of the seed, often called nut (1). Both spices are strongly aromatic, resinous and warm in taste. Mace is generally said to have a finer aroma than nutmeg, but the difference is small. Nutmeg quickly loses its fragrace when ground. In many European countries, the name of nutmeg derives from Latin nux muscatus "musky nut; moschate nut"; the Middle English form is notemugge. Mace goes back to Greek makir, which was used to denote an oriental spice, though it is not clear whether this was identical to mace.

Climate and soil:
Requires a warm and humid tropical climate with an annual rainfall of 200 cm, a temperature range of 22-32oC, altitude range of sea level to 1000 m above MSL and average rainfall of 2000 to 3000 mm annually. It requires a pH range of 6.5 to 7.5 and thrives well in well drained, clay loam, sandy loam and red laterite soils. Nutmeg is a shade loving crop. Young trees should be protected from sun. Mature trees also need partial shade. Does not tolerate heavy drought and water logging (3).

Availability :

Known only from cultivation but most probably originated in Indonesia from the southern Moluccan islands, especially Ambon and Banda. At present cultivated in Banda and nearby islands, northern Indonesia, India, Sri Lanka and island of Pinang (3). Main producing countries today are Indonesia (East Indian Nutmeg) and Grenada (West Indian Nutmeg); the latter is regarded as inferior.
Conservation/ Threat Status : Cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

A moderate sized dioecious, aromatic, evergreen tree, 5-15 m tall, cone-shaped if free growing, with greyish black bark having lenticular spots on outside and exuding a sticky red sap when injured (4). Twigs slender, 1-2 mm in diameter towards the top. Leaves small, dark green, thinly coriaceous, shiny above, dull beneath with pointed ends and having sebaceous glands on the upper side, alternate, simple, extipulate, chartaceous; petiole about 1 cm long; blade elliptical to lanceolate, 5-15 cm x 3-7 cm, base acute, margin entire, apex acuminate, aromatic when bruised. Nutmeg is a slow grower, but can continue up to 60-80 years; full production is reached in 15-20 years (3).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:
Inflorescence axillary, in umbellate cymes, male ones usually many-flowered, female ones 1-3 flowered; flowers fragrant, creamy yellow, glabrescent with sparse, very minute tomentum, pale yellow with a 3-lobed perianth; male flowers with a slender pedicel less than 1 mm thick, a usually narrow perianth at the base, 8-10 stamen adante to a column; female flowers with a superior sessile, 1-celled ovary with a single basal ovule, normally anatropous to hemi-tropous (3).50% of the seeds will produce female trees. Male to female tree ratio: 1 male to 10-15 female trees.

Identifying sex: 1. By observing the flower features. Female trees produce only 1-3 flowers in a branch; male flowers appear in groups of 8-10 per bunch. Male flowers have a distinct staminal column formed by the fusion of anthers. In female flowers ovary is distinct.
2. Sex test using Ammonium molybdate.
3. Process of calcium oxalate crystals in the leaves of male seedlings.
4. Farmers feel that lengthy nuts gives rise to male trees and round nuts produce female plants.
5. Seedlings with small broad leaves and having early branching habits may be females.

Nutmeg is not strictly dioecious. Male trees show different degrees of femaleness, varying from no fruits at all to as many fruits as a good female tree (1).

Fruit:
Fruit peach shaped, berry or drupe like, 5-8 cm long, broadly pyriform or globose, periparp fleshy, yellowish, splitting open 2 halves when ripe, containing one seed.
Rate of fruit set: 30-35 of the female flowers can be realised as fruits. However, only about 8-10% of female flowers are harvested as fruits/seeds due to heavy fruit drop in nutmeg.
Fruit maturity: 7-8 months (1).

Breeding system and varietal improvement:
Sexual: Through seeds.
Vegetative: Layering (not very successful), budding (most common patch budding), grafting (inarching, veneer grafting, side grafting etc.), epicotyl grafting - very successful (> 90%).

Grafting
An efficient method for commercial propagation of nutmeg is by epicotyl grafting using M. fragrans as rootstock. The grafting has to be carried out on 20 day old seedlings. Though grafting can be performed throughout the year, grafting in August gives the best with 80% success. This technique is used for large-scale multiplication and distribution of elite nutmeg. Grafting of nutmeg on M. fragrans, M. malabarica, M. beddomeii, Gymnocranthera canaria and Knema attenuata was done by wedge grafting and approach grafting. In K. attenuata only approach grafting was successful.

Scions for grafting of nutmeg are to be collected from high yielding female trees. Graft successes of 63.3% and 62.2% was obtained up to 10 days of storage when the scions were stored in sealed polythene bags and in moist coir dust in sealed polythene bags, respectively.
Primary, secondary and tertiary shoots from different positions of the tree can be used for grafting. Among these primary shoots produce canopy with less branching compared to scions taken from tertiary branches. A softwood grafting technique with 100% success is standardized in situ for converting male trees into female trees for rejuvenation of unproductive old female trees. It involves detopping male trees at 1 m height and grafting the newly emerged shoots with scions from high yielding female trees by wedge grafting. This method is now being widely adopted by farmers (4).

Advantages of vegetative method: Produce true-to-type mother trees, reduces the juvenile period and hence quick yield, sex problem can be avoided.
Female plants can be produced easily. Male trees can be converted to female trees by budding /grafting etc.
Note: Select only best trees as mother trees. Scions from erect shoot will grow as erect trees and scions from lateral shoots (plageotropic) grow bushy.
Breeding: Mother tree selection (1).

Biotechnology
In vitro proliferation of mace tissue was achieved and a 10-fold increase in fresh weight of the tissue within 2 weeks was observed. The proliferated tissue retained the colour and flavour of original mace. Gas chromatographic analysis of mace oil extracted from the cultured tissue showed that it was similar to that of original mace. This technique, if refined further, has tremendous potential for industrial production of mace tissue and in vitro production of myristic acid (4).


Phenology: Trees come to flowering usually during June to October in Kerala. They start flowering from 6th-7th year onwards. Grafts come to flowering during 3rd-4th year.

Pollinators:
Male and female flowers take 80 to 148 days respectively, for anthesis from the date of emergence of flower bud (4). Anthesis is usually 3-5 AM. Cross pollinated. In South India it is pollinated by insects, especially beetles (ant mimicking flower beetle) and moths. Heavy monsoon rains during the peak flowering season may adversely affect pollination. Many local farmers believe that male trees should be in the fields as a source of pollen, while others are of the opinion that these are not obligatory. It has also been suggested locally by many that the local wasp "mibone" plays a role in pollination. The literature presents various views. According to Cruickshank (1973), Deinum wrote that pollination was effected by a moth, Peryl concluded that M. fragrans may be able to produce seeds without pollination, while Flach is of the view that cross pollination is obligatory and the team of Duncan and Ferguson suggested a cross-pollination mechanism (7).
Artificial pollination: Collecting pollen from undehisced flowers of male plants and pollinating the female flowers enhances fruit set. Pollen can be applied in dust form or in liquid by mixing in 6% sucrose solution (1).

Floral and seed biology:
Seed Biology:
Seed ovoid, oblong or obtuse, 2-3 cm long, with a shiny dark brown, hard and stony, furrowed and longitudinally wrinkled shell, surrounded by a laciniate red aril which is attached to its base; kernel with small embryo and ruminate endosperm which contains many veins containing essential oil (3).
Related taxa:
Papua nutmeg - Myristica argentea Warb. (wild and cultivated in Irian Jaya and Papua New Guinea), M. castaneifolia A. Gray (in the Fiji islands), M. cinnamomea King (Malaysia, Singapore, Sumatra, Borneo and Philippines), M. crassa King (Thailand, Malaysia, Singapore and Sumatra) , M. dactyloides Gaertner (in Sri Lanka), M. elliptica Wallich ex Hook.f. & Thomson (the swamp nutmeg, widespread in west Malesia and Thailand), M. fatua Houtt. (Kalimantan, Sulawesi, Moluccas and Philippines), M. malabarica Jain (Bombay nutmeg in west coast of peninsular India), M. muelleri Warb. (Australia, north eastern Queensland), M. succedanea Blume (the Halmahera nutmeg, wild and cultivated in northern Moluccas) and M. womersleyi J. Sinclair (New Guinea) etc (3).
Myristica fragrans, M. fatua var. magnifica*, M. malabarica, M. beddomeii, M. andamanica, M. attenuata, M. prainii, M. amygdalina, are the different species present and Gymnocranthera canaria, Knema andamanica are related taxa (4).

Myristica malabarica Lam.

Common Names
English : Bombay mace, false nutmeg
Hindi : Bombay jaayphal; Van jaayphal
Sanskrit : Gosthani
Malayalam : Pashupashi, Kattujathi, Patri
Tamil : Pattiri, Kattu jatikkai
Telugu : Rampatri, Vanajathi
Kannada : Kanagi

Distribution
Throughout south India, in evergreen forests of low elevations up to 300 m.

Diagnostic Features
Moderate sized evergreen tree with greyish black, smooth bark and whorl of branches; leaves simple alternate, elliptic-oblong, sub-obtuse, thinly coriaceous, shiny above, dull beneath, nerves about 10 pairs; flowers small in a dichasial cymes, unisexual, male flowers in slender cymes, females more globose and larger than males; fruits elongate, densely rugose-tomentose, seeds arillate, aril redish yellow, irregularly lobed, extending up to the apex of the seed.

Parts Used
Seed, aril

Medicinal Uses
Aril of the seeds is cooling, febrifuge and expectorant, and is useful in the vitiated conditions of vata, cough, bronchitis, fever and burning sensation. Fat obtained from the seeds is good for vitiated conditions of vata, myalgia, sprains and sores (6).

Germplasm
A very high yielding clone (A-9/4) of nutmeg, which is dwarf and bushy with flower in groups of 2-3, almost in every leaf axil, yielding from fifth year of planting is recommended for release in Kerala. This selection yields an average of 800 fruits/tree per year during the first 4 years of bearing. Eleven nutmeg selections namely, A-9/20, A-9/22, A-9/22, A-9/25, A-9/69, A-9/79, A-9/86, A-4/12, A-4/22, A-4/52, A-11/29 and A-11/70 have been identified on yield evaluation of open pollinated seedlings of germplasm collections (4).

CULTIVATION ASPECT:
Released varieties:
Konkan Sugandha and Viswashree. Many local cultivars are also there.

Chromosome number:
2n=44

Selection of planting material:
Fresh seeds from mature fruits are used for planting.

Planting:
Seeds should be planted immediately after collecting, before they dry out. Seed in the shell takes 4-6 weeks to germinate; without shell, half the time is only required. Seeds are collected from mother trees (Stable yielding 15 yr. old or above (up to 60 yrs.) trees producing at least 2000 fruits/year and having good amount of mace. Select only heavy nuts of naturally split, heavy fruits from the tree itself in June-July and sow immediately. Collected nuts can be stored for 15 days in moist sand/coir dust/saw dust without loosing viability much. Raised sand beds of convenient length, 1 - 1 ½ m width and 15 cm height are prepared under shade. Seeds are sowed in pits of 2 cm depth at 10-20 cm apart. Treat seeds with 200 ppm. Giberellic acid for 24 h before sowing, which increases germination percentage. Mulching, regular watering and weeding the beds is necessary. Germination starts with 17-20 days of sowing and continue up to 2-3 months. Usually 90% seeds germinate. About 20-30 day old sprouts are transferred to polythene bag (40 x 20 cm) containing a mixture of soil, sand and decomposed cowdung (3:3:1) from the beds and arrange the bags in a shaded area and nurture them. Alternative: Seeds can be also directly planted into the polythene bags avoiding the nursery beds. Planting in the main field: 1 to 1 ½ year old seedlings are planted in the main field with a spacing of 7.5 - 8 m and pit size 90 x 90 x 90 cm. Pits should be filled with compost/FYM and top soil and 1½ year old seedlings or grafts should be planted in the centre of the filled pit and the seedlings should be giving adequate shade. Seedlings are planted on the onset of South West monsoon (2).

Management after planting:
Shade requirements: 70-75% shade during the first 3-4 years 50% shade afterwards. Nutritional requirements: Organic - Starting with 10 kg compost or FYM during the first year and increasing progressively as the tree grows to reach 50 kg compost or FYM for a 15 year old tree. Inorganic - 20:18:50 g NPK/tree during the first year; double the dose during the second year. Increase every year the dose - 500:250:1000 g NPK per tree during the 15th year. In channels of 40 cm width opened about 15 cm away from the tree in the first year. As the tree grows this distance can be increased to 50 cm. Organic manures during May-June. Inorganic manures in 2-3 split doses during May-June, September-October and December-January.
Irrigation: Young plants should be watered daily during the first year, and weekly once during the subsequent 4-5 years. Mature trees need irrigation during summer months once in a week.
Aftercare: Ensure proper drainage in the gardens, regulate the shade tree growth, cover the exposed roots with soil, prune off the dead shoots (1).


MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
Insect pests are
Leaf eating beetles (Asticus crisochloris)
Control: Spray quinalphos 0.05%.
Stem borers (Phloeoginus ribortus, Xyleborus fornicatus, Xyleborus myristicae etc)
Control: Apply a paste of 50% BHC or carbaryl on the main stem and branches.
Scale insects black scale (Saissetia nigra), white scale (Pseudaulacaspis cockerelli) and shield scale (Protopulvinaria mangiferae) (Homoptera). They infest young plants in the nursery and field.
Symptoms: Infest tender stems or leaves causing wilting of shoots.
Control: Spray monocrotophos (0.05%) (4).
Root worms (Loknosterna sp.)
Control: Apply 10% BHC powder in the tree basin.
White ants, etc.
Control: BHC 10%.

Stored product pests: (Araecesus fasciculatus, Tribolium spp. Silvanus sueinamensis, Caspophilus spp.).
Control: Use CO2 before packing the aril in tins or smoke the nuts using methyl bromide. Dry the produce properly (2).

Diseases:

Diseases are not very serious in most of the areas.

Fruit rot: Caused by Diplodia natalensis, Colletotrichum gleosporioides, Phytophthora meadii. Both wet and dry fruit rot are noticed in many of the plantations. Phytophthora meadii causes wet rot during May-June, resulting in heavy fruit drop. Apart from these diseases there appears to be a complex where immature fruit fall is noticed which might due to physiological imbalance.
Symptoms: Dark brown drying occurs starting from the stalk of the fruit and extends showing depressed necrotic patches. D. natalensis also causes die back. The symptoms of wet rot are also similar to dry rot except that the lesions will not be sunken and the lesion enlarges fast leading to fruit rot.
Epidemiology: Dry rot is invariably seen during drier periods from December onwards. Whereas Phytophthora fruit rot is seen only during monsoon period.
Control: No detailed investigations have been done since it is very minor. However, Spraying the trees with Bordeaux mixture (1%) would check this disease.

White thread blight: Caused by Marasmius pulcherima
Symptoms: White thread blight is usually seen on the shoots spreading along the stalk of the leaves in a fan like fashion underneath the leaf, leading to leaf blight.
Control: Prune the affected twigs and spray Bordeaux mixture (1%).
Seedling wilt: Caused by Phytophthora sp.
Control: Pruning off of the affected branches and drenching the nursery bags with 0.2% copper oxychloride.
Horsehair blight: Caused by Marasmius equicrinus.
Symptoms: Horsehair blight is noticed on the foliage leading to drying of twigs. Fruiting bodies of the fungus with long silky horsehair like structures are seen hanging from the infected branches.
Control: Prune the affected twigs and spray Bordeaux mixture (1%) (4).
Nursery mould: Caused by Pharmocapinus affecting leaves of seedlings.
Control: Spray 1% Bordeaux mixture or any other copper fungicide.
Fruit rot (dry): Caused by Stigmina myristicae, Colletotrichum gleosporioides etc.
Control: Spray 1% Bordeaux mixture or any other copper fungicide.
Die back: Caused by Diploidia sp.
Control: Remove the dead branches. Apply Bordeaux paste on the cut end. Spray 1% Bordeaux mixture during April-May, August-September and January months.
Leaf spot: Caused by Alternaria citri.
Control: Spray 1% Bordeaux mixture.
Leaf burning: Caused by Botryodiplodia theobroma
Wilt: Caused by Rosellinia pepo.
Control: Isolate the infected plants by opening deep furrows around the basin. Apply systemic fungicides like benlate. As a last resort, destroy the affected plant.
Fruit fall of nutmeg: A major problem in certain areas - causes nonspecific most important cause is moisture stress. Other reasons are hard subsoil strata and physiological and nutritional problems. No specific pathogen is involved. Copious irrigation and nutrient application is useful (1).

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Nutmeg products, dry shelled seed (nutmeg) and dried aril (mace) are sold as spices whole or ground. In most countries grated nutmeg is used in small quantities to flavour confectionery. But in western Europe it is also used in meat dishes and soups. Mace is preferably used in savory dishes, pickles and ketchups. Both seed and aril are common spices and are used in cooking, especially with non-vegetarian dishes. Essential oil and extracts are often used in major food products, flavouring liquors and in perfumery, canning industry, in soft drinks and in cosmetics. Nutmeg quality BWP (broken, wormy and punky) and mouldy nutmegs are often used for distilling essential oil. Maximum level of oil permitted in food is about 0.08%. Oil has insecticidal, fungicidal and bactericidal properties. Young husks (pericarp) are made into confectionery (jellies, marmalades, sweets, jams and preserves). Old husks can be used as substrate to grow popular edible mushroom 'kulat pala' (Volvariella volvaceae (Bull. ex Fr.) Sing), which possess a light nutmeg flavour. Nutmeg butter, a fixed oil obtained by pressing seeds, is used in ointment and perfumery (3). In Indonesia, the (woody and very sour) pulp is used to make a delicious jam with fine nutmeg aroma. In Malaysia the fleshy outer husk is crystallized or pickled and then sold as a delicious snack.

Parts Used:

Seed and aril of fruit.

Harvesting Practices:
Harvesting is possible year around in non-seasonal climates; fruits are harvested when the pericarp splits open and exposes the red coloured aril and the brown seed. Such individual fruits are either handpicked, allowed to fall and collect or harvested with a small basket on a long pole, to which a sharpened piece of iron is attached. Fruit yield/tree: 50-5000 nuts/tree (3).

Post Harvest Handling:
After harvesting, the seed is removed from the fruit and subsequently the mace is separated from it. Seed is dried under shade and nut in the sun for 4-6 days or above a slow burning and smoking fire. Fire deters insect attack. Under sun there is danger of overheating as a result of which the fat inside the seed may melt, resulting in broken kernels at shelling. When properly dried, the kernel rattles inside the shell; crack the shell to free the kernel. Nutmegs are graded according to quality, size and weight. 110-287 fruits give 1kg nutmeg nuts. 300-400 seeds give 1 kg dried mace. Mace is also dried in the sun, stored in dark to change its original red colour to orange yellow, scaled in tins under carbon dioxide injection. Mace is also sorted into different qualities, mainly whole, broken and fine (3). Methyl bromide fumigation of the nuts is also done to avoid mould attack in godowns.
Caution: Never use smoke for drying mace and nutmeg seeds.

Grading: Unbroken, whole, uniform nuts are separated and packed for the market (different countries follow different grading systems) Based on the number of good nuts required to make one pound a sort of grading is practiced in some countries. Indonesia grades the nuts, irrespective of their size as ABCD. The BWA, BIA, ETEZ, BSLC grade nutmegs of Indonesia are low quality nuts used for extracting oil. 'CC' grade of Indonesia is the lowest quality produce comprising all broken and damaged nutmeg parts. In Granada mace is classified as 'Whole Pale Mace No.1' and 'Broken Mace' (1).

Produce and Products:
Produces: Nutmeg (seed) and mace (aril).

Products
Nutmeg oil: Obtained by steam distillation of nut and mace. Oil content varies from 7-16%. Low quality nuts are used for extracting oil. Nutmeg oil is pale yellow in colour or almost water white mobile oil of a light, fresh, warm spicy and aromatic odor, a distinctly terpene topnote and a rich, sweet spicy, warm bodynote. Its quality improves upon storage. Nutmeg oil contains monoterpene hydrocarbons, oxygenated monoterpenes and aromatic ethers. Nutmeg leaves and stems also give low quality oil on distillation.
Nutmeg butter: Low quality nuts are powdered, subjected to high pressure and cooled. This will give an orange coloured, non-edible, hard butter like substance. It is used in ointments, soap industry etc. and in medicine and as a mild external stimulant. Nutmeg seeds contain 30-40% butter.
Nutmeg oleoresin: Solvent extraction of powdered mace and nuts using organic solvents like benzene, petroleum ether etc. yields oleoresin. Nutmeg contains 30-37% oleoresin.
Mace essential oil is colourless to pale yellow, much resembling nutmeg oil; produced in very small quantities.
Mace oleoresin is an amber to reddish-amber coloured clear liquid.
Pickles and jellies: Fallen immature fruits can be used for pickles whereas the fleshy pericarp of mature fruits can be used for making jellies and jam.

Medicinal uses
Nutmeg and mace are bitter, acrid, astringent, sweet, thermogenic, aromatic, aphrodisiac, anti-inflammatory, anodyne, anthelmintic, deodorant, digestive, carminative, stomachic, expectorant, diuretic, emmenagogue, antispasmodic, febrifuge, narcotic, stimulant, ophthalmic, anticonvulsant, antiseptic, constipating and tonic. Useful in vitiated conditions of kapha and vata, inflammations, cephalalgia, helminthiasis, halitosis, dyspepsia, flatulence, colic, cough, asthma, catarrh, diarrhoea, vomiting, strangury, amenorrhoea, dysmenorrhoea, ulcers, hepatopathy, splenopathy, ophthalmopathy, impotency, skin diseases, insomnia, hyperdipsia, cardiac disorders, fever and general debility. The burnt seed kernel powdered and mixed with buttermilk forms a very specific remedy for diarrhoea and vomiting in children (5). Nutmeg is a narcotic in excess quantities. It is astringent, a stimulant and an aphrodisiac.

Chemistry:
Per 100 g edible portion of nutmeg contain approx.: water - 10 g, protein - 7 g, fat - 33 g, essential oil - 5 g, carbohydrates - 30 g, fibre - 11 g, ash - 2 g (Ca - 0.1 g, Fe - 4.5 mg, P - 0.2 g). Nutmeg butter is highly aromatic, orange-red to red-brown fat with the consistency of butter at ambient temperature; can be obtained by pressing nutmeg under heat; contains mainly trimyristin and a high proportion of essential oil which is difficult to separate. Nutmeg essential oil is a pale yellow to almost water-white with a fresh, warm spicy, aromatic odour; contains about 10% essential oil, which is mostly composed of terpene hydrocarbons (pinenes, camphene, p-cymene, sabinene, phellandrene, terpinene, limonene, myrcene, together 60 to 90%), terpene derivatives (linalool, geraniol, terpineol, together 5 to 15%) and phenylpropanes (myristicin, elemicin, safrol, together 2 to 20%). Of the latter group, myristicin (methoxy-safrol) is responsible for the hallucinogenic effect of large nutmeg dosages (typically, one or more nuts). Oil of mace (up to 12% in the spice) contains the same aroma components in slightly different amounts. Differences in oil components influence the nutmeg flavour considerably: East Indian oils have a stronger nutmeg flavour because of greater proportion of myristicin and safrole than the West Indian oils, which are richer in elemicin. Composition of nutmeg oil: 34.1% sebinene, 13.8% a-pinene, 10.7% ß-pinene, 6.8% terpinen-4-ol, 4.8% myristicin, 4.0% ?-terpinene, 3.7% lemonene, 2.5% a-terpinene, 2.4% ß-phellandrene, 2.1% myrcene, 2.1% elemicin, 1.4% terpinolene, 1.4% safrole, 0.9% d-3-carene, 0.9% para-cymene, 0.7% a-phellandrene, 0.6% a-terpineol, 0.6% ß-caryophyllene, 0.6% methyl eugenol, 0.6% trans-sabinene hydrate, 0.5% a-copaene, 0.4% linalool, 0.4% cis-sabinene hydrate, 0.3% geranyl acetate, 0.3% cis-p-menth-2-en-1-ol, 0.2% camphene, 0.2% a-terpinyl acetate, 0.2% d-cadinene, 0.2% a-cubebene, 0.2% trans-p-menth-2-en-1-ol, 0.2% eugenol, 0.1% bornyl acetate, 0.1% a,p-dimethylstyrene (Source: Analytical Methods Committee, 1988)(8).

Nutmeg oleoresin is a pale to golden brown viscous liquid, clear and oily or opaque and waxy, becoming clear on warming to 50°C, graded on volatile oil content (ml/100 g) as 25-30, 55-60, 80 and 80-90.

Per 100 g edible portion of mace contain approx.: water - 16 g, essential oil - 10 g, fat - 22 g, carbohydrates - 48 g, P - 0.1 g, Fe - 13 mg. Red pigment in mace is lycopene and is identical with red colourant in tomato. Mace oleoresin is graded on volatile oil content as 8-24, 40-45, 50 and 50-56.

The hallucinogenic properties of nutmeg have been ascribed to aromatic ethers of safrole, elemicin and myristicin. In US, the regulatory status 'generally regarded as safe' (GRAS) has been accorded to nutmeg (GRAS 2792), nutmeg oil (GRAS 2793), mace (GRAS 2652), mace oil (GRAS 2653) and mace oleoresin (GRAS 2654) (3).

Economic potential to the country

Nutmeg cultivation is prevalent in Ernakulam, Thrissur, Kottayam and Thiruvananthapuram districts of Kerala and also a few districts in Tamil Nadu and Karnataka (4). Cultivation: About 4775 ha and 1420 t production. Main producing states are Kerala and Tamil Nadu, Most of the produces are consumed domestically.
Yield: 297 kg/ha (source: Spices Statistics, Spices Board, 1995-96).

Export potential:
Major producing countries: Grenada, Indonesia and New Guinea.
