Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Prunus armeniaca is a large to moderate sized deciduous tree considered to be native of China and central Asiatic region, from where it has spread to India and Iran by the way of Armenia. It is widely distributed and is found in Europe, Western Asia, Baluchistan, the northwest Himalayas and Western Tibet up to 12,000 ft. The wild apricot is a common fruit of the hills in northern India, comprising the major parts of Jammu & Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh and the hilly areas of Uttar Pradesh. In Himachal Pradesh, the wild apricot is commonly found in mid-hills, ranging from 1,100 to 1,700 metres above the a mean sea-level. Apricot cultivation is not successfull in south India. In its natural habitat apricot grows successfully between 350 and 430 N latitude at an elevation of 70-1500 m where annual rainfall is about 50 cm and minimum temperature does not go below -330C. Apricots are quite hardy to grow in most of the soils which are deep and well drained. A porous, light but rich loamy soil is said to be very suitable (1, 2, 3, 4).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A deciduous, spreading and hardy tree with dark-brown to black bark and glabrous twigs. Height of the tree is around 10 metres, may go up to 15 metres in some cases; girth of the tree is 82 cm, wood, hard and durable. Leaves are ovate to round ovate, broad, sometimes subcordate, abruptly short pointed, mostly obtusely serrate, pubescent on veins beneath. Petiole 24 cm long (petiolate), alternate, having reticulate pinnate venation, length 6.2 cm; breadth, 6.1 cm. Fruit is a drupe, velvety when young but nearly smooth at maturity, round to oblong, 2.5 to 2.6 cm in diameter. The fruit with a thin outer skin enclosing the yellow flesh (mesocarp), the inner layers becoming woody and forming the large, smooth, compressed stone, the ovule ripening into the seed (1, 3, 4, 5).

Flowers are about 1 inch in diameter, white to pinkish in colour and borne solitary in leaf axils at a node on very short stems. Flowers are simple, sessile, pentamerous, perigynous, actinomorphic, complete, hermaphrodite, 2 to 5 flowers per cluster. Calyx, gamosepalous, companulate, with five sepals, light red, having 3 to 5 mm long lobes of sepals. Corolla, polypetalous with 5 petals, rosaceous, valvate, actinomorphic, angular, imbricate, 1.5 to 2.0 cm long, white to light pink. Androecium, polyandrous, with 35 stamens, dorsifixed, anther-lobes, yellow, episepalous, bithecus. Gynoecium, monocarpellary, perigynous. Ovary, unilocular, having basal placentation (1, 3, 4, 5).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

 

Breeding System :
P. armeniaca is self-fertile. The literature on pollination mechanism of apricots is meager and is not well understood. Cook and Green (1894) reported that the best set of fruit was obtained from bagged flowers, with the next best from flowers in bags with honey bees, and the lowest set in the open. They did not comment on the activity of the bees either in the bags or on the open flowers. Cullinan (1937) stated that the apricot is self-fruitful. He did not indicate whether he meant the flowers would pollinate themselves or that they would set only if pollinated with their own pollen. The rather meager data indicate that some apricot cultivars must be cross-pollinated and other cultivars are benefited by cross-pollination (1, 2, 6).

Phenology :
The flowering season was observed to continue from the first week to the last week of March. The fruits start ripening from the middle of May and continue till the first week of June (3).

Pollinators:
Sticky pollen grains needs the help of insects to carry it from the stamens to the stigmatic surface of the flower. Honey bees are the primary pollinating agents (6).

Back to Top

 

Seed Biology :
The apricot seed represents about 15% of the fruit and kernel represents about 34% of the seed. Stone in the fruit is 1.7 to 2 cm long, 1.6 to 1.7 cm broad and 2.5 g in weight. 2 kernels per seed which are 1.5 cm long, 1.2 cm broad and 879 mg in weight.

Seedling Germination :
Apricot seeds require cold stratification for a period of 72 days for 100% germination. The cold treatment at 6°C is better than 4°C. The growth inhibitory activity of the seed-coat disappears with continuous cold treatment and accumulation of growth promoters occurs. The germination of apricot seeds can be hastened by removal of kernel from shell, scarification and gibberellic acid or kinetin treatment. The seeds are soaked for 24 hours in 500 ppm GA3 or 5 ppm kinetin solution before planting (2).

Cultivation:

Apricots are commercially raised by vegetative methods of propagation, such as grafting and budding. The multiplication through cuttings and seed propagation is rarely done.

June budding, fall budding and tongue grafting are generally adopted for apricot multiplication. The seedlings which attain pencil thickness in the month of June are budded and the other good seedlings are utilized for fall budding in September. T -budding (shield budding) is the usual method followed in apricot. The rootstock should be more than 0.6 cm in diameter with a bark separating readily from the wood. The stock may be budded at a height of about 10-25 cm above the ground level in a smooth bark surface. About a week after insertion of a bud, the rootstock can be cut back about 10 cm above the bud. After a fortnight of budding operation the rootstock portion just above the bud is to be removed completely. When the bud starts growing, the union becomes strong enough and then the wrapping material should be removed. Some of the June budded plants become ready for permanent planting in the dormant season while fall budded can only be utilized in the next dormant season. The graft union is considered better in budding than in grafting.

The seedlings which do not make enough growth for budding are usually grafted in the following dormant season by whip or tongue grafting. The grafting in apricot is more successful when it is done late in the season at the beginning of sap flow and the buds become active. A high percentage of success is obtained in apricot both in budding and grafting.

Planting: Undesirable trees and shrubs should be removed from the soil during its initial preparation by digging and ploughing. The natural slope of the soil is left undisturbed in order to ensure good drainage. The pits having one metre dimension in length, breadth and depth are dug in winter season and properly filled with a mixture of soil and well decomposed farmyard manure. The pebbles, roots of the plants and stones, if any, found on the sides of pit, are removed. On a flat land a regular planting pattern layout, such as, square, rectangular or triangle is followed, but on hill slopes contour layout is the general practice. The spacing of plants varies with the soil, climate and vigour of the cultivar to be grown. In a fertile soil and humid climate more distance between plants is provided. Square planting system with a distance of 6 x 6 metre is traditionally adopted. In comparison to traditional wide spacing (8 x 6 and 10 x 6 m) a closer spacing (7 x 5 m) gave higher yield in apricot.

Pruning: Apricot grafts usually have numerous lateral branches unlike the straight whips of apple, pear and cherry. If the laterals developed in the nursery are not properly spaced, cut off the main stem while planting, about 50 to 75 cm above the ground level to promote the growth of new laterals. During the first summer, remove all unwanted laterals, leaving three to five well-placed branches to form the framework. Head-back the scaffold branches breast-high next winter to get secondary scaffold branches. Leave the upper branches longer than the lower ones, as the latter grows faster and crowds the upper branches. By the end of the second growing season, the tree produces a large number of laterals on the scaffold branches and trunk, which should all be removed, except a few short growths (7 cm to 12 cm long) on the trunk and the main branches. Retain only five to seven secondary scaffolds. In subsequent years, thin only the branches which are either crossing or crowding one another. This practice admits light into the centre and encourages the growth of spurs.

The pruning of old trees should aim at producing new spurs to replace those broken during picking. The kind and the amount of pruning depend upon the bearing habit of the variety. Light to moderate thinning of branches and the shortening of new wood back to the laterals is the usual practice. If new growth is less than 40 to 80 cm each year, resort to severe pruning.

Manuring: Genarally, complete NPK fertilizers are applied. A dose of fertilizers to supply 55-65 kg of N, 55-65 kg of P, 110-135 kg of K may be applied. Application of organic manure is necessary for good yield on poor sandy soils along with the fertilizers providing NPK.

Varieties: The cultivated varieties of apricot are mainly exotic and they grow successfully at varying elevations. The following varieties are recommended : Shipley Early, Kaisha, Halman, New Castle, Nari, St. Ambroise,Moorpark, Shakarpara, Nugget and Royal.

Shipley Early: Fruit medium sized, roundish. Skin creamy white with red flush on the sunny side. Flesh meshy, juicy and of average quality. Ripens in mid May. Earliest of all varieties.

New Castle: The fruita are round, medium sized and barium yellow in colour. Flesh is very sweet and juicy. It is apopular desert cultivar for warmer locations. Ripens in the month of May.

Kaisha: The roundish fruits are medium in size having yellowish skin with red blush. The flesh is orange-yellow, sweet and tasty. It is a prolific bearing cultivar ripening in early June. The kernel is bitter.

Nugget: It is rather a recent introduction which has been found to be one of the best cultivars for mid hills in respect of fruit quality, regularity in bearing and yield. It is the earliest cultivar to flower in the season. Fruits are medium to large in size and round in shape. The skin is glabrous, deep orange with vermillion blush. The flesh is deep yellow in colour, firm and juicy with fine texture. Both flesh and kernel are sweet in taste. The fruit is ready to harvest in the last week of May or the first week of June.

Halman: Halman is the most important dessert and drying cultivar of Ladakh. The fruit is medium-sized and golden in colour. The flesh is very sweet but not very juicy. It is harvested in early August.

St. Ambroise: It is a mid to late season cuItivar ripening from the last week of June to the first week of July. The fruit is oval, large and orange-yellow, having tiny dots of pink colour. The flesh is deep yellow, firm, juicy and very sweet. This cultivar is not suitable for the lower mid hill region.

Nari: Small to medium in size, oblong, depressed at both base and apex ends. The skin is smooth and pale yellow in colour. The flesh is light yellow with slight fibre having sweet pleasant aroma. The kernel is sweet like almond. The fruit is ready to harvest in the last week of May.

Royal: Royal is a popular cultivar of mid hills. The fruits are large in size, yellow in colour, ripening in the middle of June. The flesh is yellow, firm, juicy and sweet in taste. It is a good dessert cultivar and the fruits are suitable for canning. It can be utilized as a parent in breeding for heat resistance (1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 8).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

1. Indian Gypsy Moth (Lymantrio obfuscata) : This destructive pest has been recorded in all apricot growing regions. The damage is caused by caterpillar which are gregarious and nocturnal in habit. In case of severe attack the trees are completely defoliated as the larvae eat up the entire lamina, leaving the hard veins behind. The moth can be controlled by dusting with 10% BHC in March-April. The egg masses having yellowish brown hairs should be located in August and March and destroyed.

2. Peach Stem Borer (Sphenoptero lafertei) : It is a polyphagus pest attacking trunk and branches of apricot. Sometimes the entire tree dries up due to boring of grubs into trunk. Infested inner bark becomes a powdery mass like sawdust when, mixed with frass, oozes out from the splits on the surface. The pest undergoes three generations in a year by overwintering as larva. Heavily infested branches and trees are to be removed and burnt. The adult beetles should be killed by spraying 0.15% BHC in the month of March and it should be repeated after a fortnight again.

3. Peach Fruit Fly (Dacus spp) : The flies lay eggs on fruits and feed on pulp rendering them unfit for human consumption. Pupation takes place in soil under infected trees. In April-May, on the appearance of flies on leaves the bait comprising 0.1 % malathion and 1 % sugar (2 ml cythion 50 EC + 10 g sugar in 1 lit of water) should be spayed on leaves and other resting places of the flies. Two sprays, first one month before and second a fortnight before the commencement of fruit harvest, with fenthion (250 ml lebaycid 1000) or fenitrothion (500 ml sumithion/folithion/accothion 50 EC in 500 lit water) should be done.

4. European Red Mite (Panonychus uini) : Depending upon weather condition and fruit exposure, highest incidence of apricot damage at harvest is associated with this mite. The best control is obtained by applying carbophenothion at 58g /100 lit in the middle of October when maximum hatching of eggs takes place.

5. Apricot Chalcid (Eurytoma samsonavi) : This is a serious pest of apricot. Eggs are laid inside the tender fruits. On hatching, the grub feeds inside the kernel causing drop of premature fruits. Grubs, which are translucent white, feed during April-May and remain quiescent from June to February. Thereafter, they pupate inside the fruits. All the infested and fallen fruits should be collected and destroyed to control this pest.

6. Leaf Roller (Archips micaceana) : The leaves and petioles are cut and rolled up by caterpillars and then they feed inside the soft tissues. After puncturing the ripening fruits, the caterpillars also eat up inner pulp. The affected fruits are not fit for human consumption. The leaf roller can be controlled by spraying 0.05% endosulfan or fenitrothion. The rolled leaves and webbed flowers should be destroyed after collecting (1, 2).

Diseases:

1. Brown Rot (Moniiinia iaxa., M. fructigena, Sclerotinia spp) : This disease attacks both blossoms and fruits. In case of severe infection, blossoms turn brown and die. After infection, tiny brown spot enlarges to a large, soft, brown area within a few days. The pathogens overwinter in fruit mumies, in twig cankers and in rotten fruits. The penetration takes place via cuticle and stomata. Treatment with 0.3% zineb and 0.1 % benomyl during anthesis for effectively control the disease.

2. Bacterial Canker and Gummosis (Pseudomonas syringae) : The Pathogen attacks all parts except root. The bark and outer sapwood show symptoms of circular to elongated water-soaked lesions. The affected bark later turns brown, gummy and sour-smelling. Sunken black lesions are found on the infected fruits. Bordeaux mixture can be sprayed to prevent the infection. Mashobra paste (stearic acid 25.5 g, morpholine 9.0 ml, water 340 ml, lanolin 136 g and non-sterile dihydrostreptomycin sulphate 3.1 g) has been found effective as a control measure.

3. Powdery Mildew (Sphaerotheca pannosa) : The fungus infects young leaves and shoots while the older ones are fairly resistant. The white, powdery appearance is caused by great number of conidia produced in chains arising from the wefts of hyphae on the leaf surface. The infected young leaves get curled and narrowed as they expand. The disease checks the growth of the affected plant parts. Three sprays of 2% wettable sulphur at different stages, i.e., before flowering, at petal fall and two weeks thereafter are necessary to control the mildew.

4. Apoplexy (Pseudomonas syringae and Cytospora cinta): Sometimes an apricot tree or a part of it begins wilting and dies suddenly due to a syndrome known as apoplexy or permature die back. The infection increases cellwall thickness and severe cambium infection inhibits the new tissue development. Badly affected plants die within one or two years but in some cases within ten to fifteen years. Several control measures include pruning in spring instead of winter season, spraying of plants with Bordeaux mixture before and after pruning and removal and burning of all infected wood. Mechanical injuries to the plant should be avoided. Planting of resistant and tolerant cultivars is also suggested.

5. Wilt (Verticillium albo-atrum) : The leaves drop down from the affected limbs in early summer. The whole tree or a part of it may be affected. No external evidence of the disease except general stunting is visible. The infected tree is not always killed, but may remain stunted and unproductive for several years. This is a soil-inhabiting fungus and infects through the roots, obviously its control through protective sprays is not possible. Before planting the pit soil should be sterilized with formalin.

6. Corymeum Blight (Stigmina carpophylla) : The blight attacks twigs, leaves, blossoms, fruits as well as unopened buds. Small circular deep purple spots appearing on the fruit become raised and rough. The raised spots on twigs expand into necrotic cankers. Abscission of the diseased results in shot hole effect. Diseased buds become darker in colour than normal ones often show a glistening layer of gum. Copper oxychloride at 300g/100 lit water should be sprayed before leaf fall and at bud swell (1, 2).

Economic Potential and Uses

Apricot kernels yield by expression 40 to 50 per cent of a fixed oil. This oil is used for cooking as well as for burning. The oil is far less expensive and finds its use in cosmetics, for its softening action on the skin and pharmaceutical preparations. It is often fraudulently added with other seed oils and used in the manufacture of soaps, cold creams and other preparations of the perfumery trade. Since apricot kernel oil is rich in essential fatty acids (oleic acid and linoleic acid), it spreads easily on the skin and provides useful elements to its health. They also help to regulate moisturization and restructure the skin. Oil has moisturizing, nourishing and revitalizing properties, which are being used in body and hand products (body and bath oils) and products for baby skin, dry and mature skin. The cake left after oil extraction can be used as fuel and fertilizer. When the cake is digested with alcohol it yields as essential oil which contains a colourless, crystalline glucoside Amygdalin. The essential oil is used in confectionery and as a culinary flavouring (1, 2, 3, 4, 9, 10, 11).

Uses

The fresh fruits are generally eaten. An excellent chutney is made from the wild apricot fruits. These fruits can also be used for making jam. Chopped kernels are added to kheer in villages. The fruits of wild apricot are also sun-dried to make chalori, which is used as a souring agent in many food preparations. A chaat is also made from chalori after soaking it in water and mixing it with spices. An alcoholic drink is also prepared from the fruits of wild apricots in this area. The wood is hard and durable and is used for making agricultural implements, thatching mud houses and is used as fuel (1, 2, 3, 4).

Harvesting Practices
Fruits are plucked when they change colour from green to yellow, lose flesh firmness slightly depending upon the cultivar. The optimum stage of fruit ripening for machine harvesting starts when about 10% fruits are still green and ends when more than 10% of the fruits are overripe. It is also determined by measuring fruit flesh firmness, dry matter content and refractive index of juice and harvesting time is considered optimum on ripening of 70-85% of the fruits. An apricot plant starts bearing at the age of four or five years attaining a full bearing age at about eight or ten years when it yields about 50-80 kg fruit (1, 2, 4, 5).

Produce and Products

Fruits are utilized in the preparation of number of products such as such as, apricot jam, apricot nectar and apricot papad. In the preparation of papad, the fruit pulp is thinly spread, dehydrated and pressed to make papad. Apricot leather (kamaradin) is similarly prepared by drying the pulp after treating it with sulphur dioxide as preservative and mixing the same with sucrose and glucose syrup. Another popular beverage, apricot nectar, is made by converting the ripe fruits into puree and mixing it with sugar syrup and citric acid. Oil extracted from the apricot kernels has the industrial importance. Press cake is used as manure (1, 2).

Processing Techniques

Kernels after collection and drying are coarsely ground and heated with steam to the temperature of boiling water before pressing them in expellers and hydraulic presses. The expressed oil is refined by screening to remove the solid material, treating with sodium carbonate to neutralize free acids, decolourizing with fullers earth or activated carbon and finally heating with steam in vaccum (1).

Other Uses
The fruits are antidiarrhoeal, antipyretic, emetic, allaying thirst and not good for old people. The seeds are tonic and anthelmintic, used in liver troubles, piles, earache and deafness. Cyanogenic glycosides are found in higher concentration in apricot seeds. The drug laetrile is derived from extracts of apricot seeds. A controversial therapy for cancer. Apricot seeds were used to treat tumors as early as AD 502. In Tibet, the masticated wild apricots are applied in ophthalmia. In Afghanistan, the fruits are considered to be laxative and refrigerant in fever. Arab doctors used to recommend the oil extracted from apricot stones to cure haemorrhoids, aching noses and earache. Apricot oil was used against tumors and ulcers in England in the 1600s (1, 3, 4, 10, 11).

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

The apricot (Prunus armeniaca L.) is an attractive, delicious and highly nutritious fruit. It is particularly a very rich source of vitamin A and also contains more carbohydrates, protein, phosphorus and niacin than majority of other common fruits. Since apricot fruits are the first to reach the market, they sell at a premium. It ranks second next to plum among the stone fruits in India in area, production and popularity. Apricots are grown with minimum cultural care in the tracts which are otherwise unfit for cultivating other temperate fruit crops. It is a drought resistant, salt tolerant, hardy plant being rather less susceptible to pests and diseases. Apricot kernel oil is an important commercial commodity containing over 90 per cent of fatty acids as Oleic and Linoleic acids.

Miscellaneous Information

Breeding and Improvement: For any good breeding programme, the availability of wide genetic diversity in the gene pool is a pre-requisite which is abundantly present in apricots. Even then comparatively less breeding work has been done in this crop than other stone fruits. Although apricots are geographically widely distributed, yet a particular genotype of the fruit is ecologically less adapted. The damage due to spring frost is the greatest limiting factor in extending its cultivation. Therefore, the climatic adaptability has been the main objective of apricot breeding throughout the world. Depending upon the local requirements, the cultivars need to be developed for cold hardiness and for varying temperate climatic conditions, i.e., high and low chilling cultivars. Good pomological fruit qualities such as large size, good skin colour with blush, firm flesh, proper blend of sugar and acidity, good texture and high keeping quality and resistance to pests and diseases are common objectives of all the fruit improvement programmes (2).
