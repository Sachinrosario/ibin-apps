Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
This tree is believed to be native of India. It is common in deciduous forests of Western Ghats usually along the banks of rivers and streams. Trees grow on rocky and sandy soil and thrive on Deccan trap. Madhuca longifolia grows throughout the greater part of India except the temperate and arid areas and southern parts of the Indian Peninsula. It is a common tree of deciduous forests of Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra and Gujarat, central India, Indian Peninsula, Chota Nagpur. Orissa, extending to the sub-Himalayan tract between the Ravi and Gandak. It is a characteristic tree of dry plains sal forests. The tree is extensively cultivated near the villages because of its economic value in the rural areas. In its natural habitat the obsolute maximum shade temperature varies from 1060 to 1180 F, the obsolute minimum from 300 to 460 F, and the normal rainfall from 30-75 inches. The trees grows on a wide variety of soils, but prefers sandy soil. It can grow on shallow bouldery soil. It grows well on alluvail soil in Indo-Gangetic plains (1, 11, 12).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
It is a large tropical evergreen tree with a dense spreading crown found in south India, bark grey to dark brown with vertical cracks, scaly. Young leaves, pedicles and petiole pubescent and tomentose. Leaves coriaceous firm, clustered near ends of branches, linear lanceolate, 7.5-12.5 cm X 2.5-4.5 cm., tapering towards base, short acuminate, on petioles 1-1.5 inches long, main lateral nerves 10-12 pair glabrous when mature. Stipules subulate 0.5 the length of the petioles. Fruit an avoid berry upto 5 cm long, turning yellow when ripe (5, 7).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Flowers in dense clusters near ends of branches, many, small, calyx rusty, pubescent, corolla tubular, fleshy, pale yellow. 1.5 cm long, aromatic, caducous, berries ovoid, yellow when dry. Calyx coriaceous, densly clothed with rusty tomentum, sepals 4-5 ovate. Corolla cream coloured, tube ovoid, limb with 7-14, often 8 or 9, short erect ovate teeth. Stamens 20-30 (7, 9).

Phenology :
Under favourable soil and climatic conditions mahua tree begin to bear fruit at the age of 8-10 years and lasts for about 60 years. The brown flower buds appear at the end of the thick branchlets early in February, and the flowers open from the end of February to April. Alternate bearing is common. About ten years old trees start flowering (11, 12).

Seed Biology :
Seeds are compressed, straight on one side and curved on the other side, smooth and shining, with fairly thick and hard seed coat. The seeds are 1.25 cm long, ellipsoidal, light brown and moderately hard. The fruit is a fleshy, ovoid green berry, which is one to four seeded. The seeds are made up of brown testa, little endosperm and germ with two bulky cotyledons. The kernels weigh about 75 per cent of the whole seed and contain about 50 per cent of oil (5).

Seedling Germination :
Hypogeous. Germnination commences with the development of thick cotyledonary petioles. These petioles, which are not visible in the seed, reach a length 0.6-1 inch., and assist the radicle to make its way into thc ground and plumule to extricate itself from between the fleshy cotyledons, which are in close contact. The cotyledons remain underground, the testa breaking open as they swell. The seed germinates early in the rainy season, soon after falling. For successful germination it should be covered with earth or debris (2, 12).

Cultivation:

Natural reproduction takes place by seeds which germinate early in the rainy season, soon after falling. Exposure renders them liable to fungus attack and radicles may dry up or be eaten by insects; for germination to take place the seeds should become covered with earth or debris. Natural seedlings are thus found chiefly in small hollows into which earth is washed at the commencement of rains. Subsequent growth is 'slow, but is favoured by abundant light.


Artificial propagation may be done by direct sowing or by transplanting nursery raised seedlings. Fresh seeds should be sown during July-August and covered with earth. Direct sowing in prepared lines or patches is preferred to transplanting which is troublesome as the seedlings develop long and delicate tap-roots. For transplanting, seeds should be either sown direct in deep pots or baskets or seedlings transplanted into them from the nursery during the first rainy season a few weeks after germination; they are planted in the forest early in the second rainy season (1).

Direct sowing is done in July in well prepared lines. The seed is sown about 1.5-2.5 cm deep. Only fresh seed is used for sowing. The lines may be spaced about 4-5 m apart. The lines are kept weeded and clean of bushes which may suppress the seedlings. Irrigation may become necessary if monsoon rains fail. Mahua can be successfully raised with agricultural crops also which are cultivated in spaces between the lines. About 60 cm wide strips need, however, be kept clean along the lines so that agricultural crops do not suppress the seedlings. Effective fencing of the plantation areas to keep out cattle is also necessary.

Sowing in 30 cm3 pits dug during summer and refilled at the time of sowing has been reported to be a very successful method to raise this species. The pits are spaced about 9 X 9m. Four to five seeds are sown per pit, but only one seedling is retained per pit after the seedlings have established. Agricultural crops can be grown in intervening spaces. Sowing is done in July. Irrigation may be necessary if monsoon rains fail (12).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

The leaves are eaten by caterpillars of Achaea janata Linn., Anuga multiplicans wlk., Bombotelia nugatrix Guen., Metanastria hyrtaca Ciani. and a few species of Rhodoneura. two species of Acrocercops mine the leaves and blotch them.. Newly felled trees and green logs are attacked by the pin-hole borers, Xyleborus spp. and Polygraphus bassiae Beeson. The bark is destroyed by white ants (Odontotermes obesus Ramb.) and the bark borer, Xyloctonus scolytoides Eichh. The sapwood of dead tree is damaged by ghoon borers, like Schistoceros anobioides Waterh. and Xylocis tortilicornis Lesne.

M. longifolia is attacked by the sap sucker, Unaspis acuminata Green. And sometimes by white ants (Coptotermes ceylonicus) and Kalotermes sp. (1).


Diseases:

It is affected by rust, Scopella echnulata. The tree is also subject to White spongy rot (polystictus steinheilianus Berk. & Lev.), heart rot of stems (Fomes caryophylli Racib.) and root and butt rot (Polyporus gilvus Schw.)

Economic Potential and Uses

The fresh oil obtained is yellow in colour without unpleasant taste. But commercial oils are generally greenish yellow in colour with an offensive odour and disagreeable taste. High acidity, deep colour, unpleasant odour, and high content of unsaponifiable matter limit the use of mahua oil for edible purposes. Almost entire mahua oil is currently used in the manufacture of ordinary soaps. However, soaps made from this oil get rancid. The refined oil is used as a substitute for tallow and ghee in India. The fat is also used in chocolate manufacture. Mahua oil has emollient properties and is used in skin diseases, rheumatism and headache. It is laxative and is considered useful in habitual constipation, piles and haemorrhoids, it is also used as emetic.


The cake cannot be used as cattle feed because it contains about 4.6 % of a toxic and bitter saponin, mowrin, possessing digitalis-like as well as a hemolytic action on the heart. The cake finds its use as manure in sugarcane fields, grass plantations and lawns because of its vermicidal properties (3, 10).

Uses:

Berries and flowers are eaten raw or cooked. They are also eaten after frying or baking into cakes. Flowers are largely used in the preparation of liquors, vinegar. Mahua tree yields constructional timber.

Harvesting Practices
The fruits ripen in May-June, when they fall from the tree or dropped by vigorous shaking of the branches. The season for collecting the seeds is short and in the absence of organized harvesting, a considerable portion of the crop is lost during the monsoon. In some parts of South India, harvesting extends up to December. In northern India harvesting takes place between April and July. In southern India the harvesting period is between August and September. The seeds are separated from the fruit wall by pressing, and then dried and shelled to get the kernel. The kernels constitute the mahua seed of commerce (1, 10, 12).

Processing Techniques

The ripe fruits fallen on the ground are collected or manually collected from the tree and are depulped by pressing to obtain the seeds. The testa is removed from the seed by hand or by beating the seeds with a mallet. The seeds may be decorticated in a roll mill to crack the outer shell. The oil is extracted by crushing the kernels in a traditional expeller or by gasoline or benzene as a solvent. Alcohol refining improves the product, and hydrogenation of the refined mahua oil yields a product similar to mutton tallow and cocoa butter (1).

Pre-treatment: The seeds are separated by pressing and are then sun dried. During storage the kernels are susceptible to fungus and insect attack. Aspergillus flavus and Rhizopus have been recorded together with the insect pest Oryzaephilus surinamensis. To prevent extensive damage the kernels should have a moisture content of 8% and if possible be treated with a persistent insecticide. Oil quality is much related to storage conditions. The FFA content of fresh kernels lies between 1 and 2% but can rise to 30% in poorly stored material.

Oil extraction

In rural areas, the oil is extracted using traditional Ghanis. Industrial processing involves the use of expellers with or without solvent extraction of the cakes. The yields obtained by each method are shown below.

Other Uses
Flowers are regarded as cooling, tonic and demulcent. They are used in coughs, colds and bronchits. Leaves are astringent and used in embrocations, eaten by cattle, used as green manure. Latex from the bark yields rubbery product. Tannin from the bark is used for dying ,tanning, ulcers, itches, bleeding and diabetes.

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

It is estimated that India could produce 400 000 tons of oil per annum. Actual production (1976-1984) ranged from 19 000 to 30 000 tons per annum. The kernels are not edible as they contain poisonous saponins. Crude Mowrah butter is used as a fat for spinning wool, for making candles and soap. Mahwa flowers are either directly used for human consumption or as a raw material for the production of alcohol. The refined fat is used as an edible fat and vegetable ghee in India. The seed contains 55-60% fat (4, 9).

Export Potential

Prior to world war I, consiederable quantities of mahua seed (30,000 tons per annum) were exported to Europe. During the recent past, a small quantity of oil has been exported, mainly to Pakistan. In 1960, 519 cwt. of oil valued Rs. 51,225 were exported (1).
