Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Cotton grows naturally in relatively arid areas in tropical and subtropical regions of the Middle East, Africa, the Americas, Australia, and some South Pacific islands. Researchers have identified 31 species of cotton, 4 of which are domesticated, bearing seed fibers capable of being spun. Two of these cultivated species are indigenous to the Middle East, two are indigenous to the Americas. All cotton grown in the U.S. is from the New World group, characterized by 26 pairs of chromosomes. Cotton quality is measured by "grade," which is based on the color and purity of the lint; and by "staple," or the length of the fibers. The fruit of the cotton plant is called a boll (pronounced bowl) which splits open to reveal the fiber-covered seed. The price received by cotton producers is determined by both the quantity and quality of the harvested lint (1).


In India cotton is distributed through out the country. In its native habitat, cotton is a perennial that does not die in the fall. Instead, the plant becomes dormant during periods of drought and resumes growth with the return of favorable rainfall. Another growth characteristic of cotton is its indeterminate fruiting habit. Rather than flowering during a distinct period following vegetative growth, cotton simultaneously produces vegetation and fruiting structures (1). Cotton is crop of warm plains, grown commercially from sealevel to 1,200 m, with some perennial forms found at 1,800 m. A long-season plant, cotton requires a minimum of 180–200 frost-free days of uniformly high temperatures, averaging 21–22°C. Full sunlight is critical for proper development. Cotton is tolerant of a wide variety of soils, but thrives best on deep, friable, moisture-holding soils with good humus supply. Optimum pH is 5.2–7. In India, cotton is grown on black alluvial and red soils (10).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
It is an annual, highly self pollinated and grows to an height of 0.5 meters to 2 meters. Primary axis is erect and branched, the leaves on the main axis have a regular spiral arrangement and they petiolate and stipulate. At the base of each leaf, two buds may develop, one is true axillary and the other is an accessory bud, extra axillary in position. The axillary bud may develop either into vegetative/fruiting branch. If an accessory bud develops may give rise to fruiting branch which bears flowers. Inflorescence is a ‘calyx’ called as a ‘square’,and each flower is surrounded by an invocluere of three leafy structures, called as bracteoles.

30 to 35 days after emergence of the seedling, the first square (flower bud) will be formed on a fruiting branch arising from the axil (node) of the fifth to seventh true leaf. The fruit is called as a ‘capsule’.The plant will continue to produce additional fruiting branches in an orderly manner up the main stem. Each fruiting branch may produce several squares, but most of the harvestable bolls will be found at either the first or second position on a fruiting branch (3). Leaves variable in size, shape, texture and hairiness. Leaves spirally arranged on monopodia. Leaves on sympodia have appearance of being alternate in 2 rows. Stipules falcate, 10 x 4 mm, caducous, petiole long, lamina cordate, as broad as they are long, 7.5-15 cm across, usually 3-5 lobed for about half their length, lobes broadly triangular, acuminate, often with multi cellular stellate hairs, extrafloral nectaries usually seen on under-surface of 3 main veins (11).

Flowers are borne at each node of the fruiting branches and each flower is surrounded by an invoclure of three leafy structures, called as bracteoles. Calyx consists of a very short cup shaped structure at the base of the corolla. The rim of the cup is five lobed. The floral nectary forms a ring on the innerside of the calyx. Extra floral nectaries are found outside the calyx near its base. Corolla has five petals and to the interior they are attached to the base of the staminal tube. Colour of the petal varies from pale lemon to golden and white to creamy yellow. Generally a "spot" or "eye" may be found at the base of the petal, due to the presence of anthocyanin pigment. The stamens monodelphous and anthers are reniform. Gynoecium consists of three to five superior carpels united in the middle

Flowers 6–8 on each fertile branch, large, white or yellow, subtended by a reduced calyx and 3–4 large green fringed bracts, staminal column surrounding style made up of 100 or more stamens, ovary superior, 3–5-carpellate, fruit a dehiscent capsule, 4–6 cm long, spherical, smooth, light green, with few oil glands (11).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Cotton is highly self pollinated crop and grows to an height of 0.5 meters to 2 meters. Inflorescence is called as ‘calyx’. 

Since the lowest branch is the oldest, the lower bud on each branch is the first to flower and the uppermost the last Therefore anthesis is acropetal and centrifugal pollination takes place shortly after flower opening. Pollen grains immediately germinate and fertilization is completed in about 30 hours after pollination (3).

Breeding System :
Cotton is self pollinated crop but natural cross pollination occurs in the presence of insect pollinators. Selfing is one of the methods of breeding cotton. Tip of the matured buds are smeared with mud which makes the calyx not to open and will fall after the development of the boll. Alternately a small metallic ring is put on to the matured bud with a thread tied to the stalk of the bud, a few days later i.e. after pollination the calyx will fall off along with the ring. Selffed bolls are harvested at maturity.

Phenology :
A cotton fruit begins as a small flower bud or "square" which flowers about 21 days after it reaches the size of a pinhead (just visible to the naked eye). A new bloom is white the first day (which is when pollination occurs) and turns red by the second day.

Pollinators:
Cotton is a highly self pollinated species but natural cross pollination may occur depending upon the presence of insect pollinators to the extent of 0.1-0.4 %.

Seed Biology :
Cotton fruit is called as ‘capsule’. They are of variable size with 5-11 seeds per loculus (1). The seed is pointed, ovoid in shape, 8-12 mm long, varying in color from brown to nearly black. Seeds possess copious coat of lint hairs besides a thick coat of fuzz hairs, or naked with a small tuft at the end. The commercial cotton seed normally consists of about 10-15% linters, 35-40% hull and 50-55% kernels. For raising the crop in one hectare, 7-8 Kg of seeds is required (13).

Seedling Germination :
The radicle emerges through the micropyle, followed by the hypocotyl which becomes arched and appears above the ground in 5-15 days. The hypocotyl then straightens lifting out the much folded cotyledons which rapidly expand into broad heart-shaped leaves on short petioles. Seeds of some cultivars have a period of dormancy before germination of 2-3 months after harvesting. A good sample should give 85-100 per cent germination, but this is reduced by storing for 2 years or more under humid conditions and the seeds lose viability rapidly if the moisture content exceeds 10 per cent. Seed in which the moisture content has been reduced to 7 per cent can be stored in sealed jars for up to 15 years (11).

Cultivation:

For cotton production May- June sowing is the best for south India and June-July sowing is the best for northern India. Land should be free from weeds, soil should be deep, well drained and fertile. Land preparation is done with one deep ploughing and 2-3 harrowing followed by leveling. Soils should be well pulverized and levelled before planting. Apply basal dose of fertilizers in furrows and 10cm below the seed zone. Dibble 1-2 seeds per hill on the sides of the ridges. Gap filling can be done within 10-12 days and thinning may be done 10-15 days after sowing (3).

Manural Requirement:

Incorporate about 5 tonnes of FYM or well decomposed compost during land preparation at least 2-3 weeks before sowing. Apply 20 Kg N, 40Kg P2O5 and 40 Kg K2O per acre at the time of sowing. Spot application of fertilizers 8cm below the seed is preferred than line application. Top dress the crop after 45 days and 70 days of sowing with the remaining 20Kg N per acre in equal splits. Before top dressing the soil should be slightly moist. Fertilizers should be placed in the 'ring form' 4-5 cm deep, 10-15 cm away from the stem and covered with soil, so that the fertilizers should not come in contact with stem during earthing up. Irrigate the crop 2-3 days after every top dressing.


Irrigation:

Irrigate the seed plot once in 15 to 20 days depending on soil type and weather conditions. Heavy irrigation during crossing period should be avoided. Give light irrigation after picking of bolls started.


Weed Control:

Atleast 2-3 hand weeding and 2 intercultivations are required to keep the seed field weed free. Intercultivations done thoroughly both ways (along/across the rows) once at 30 days and 2nd at 60 days helps in the loosening of the soil plus simultaneous hand weeding keeps the seed plots free from weeds all the time (3, 10).

Economic Potential and Uses

The crop is mainly grown for the fiber purpose but it is also grown for oil since the seeds are the important source of oil. Seeds yield a semi-drying and edible oil. Cottonseed oil is used for culinary purposes, and the cake is a protein rich feed for ruminent livestock. The cottonseed oil contains 40-55% linoleic acid, 20-25% palmitic acid, 2-7% stearic acid, 18-30 % oleic acid and small proportion of myristic and arachidic acids with about 0.5 to 2% cyclopropenoid acids. Oil used in the manufacture of salad and cooking oils, shortenings, margarine and for protective coverings. To lesser extent it is used in the packing of fish and cured meats. A small percentage of cottonseed oil used is utilized to produce nonedible products like soaps. Only about 5% of cottonseed in India is processed for oil (2, 12, 13).

The cut plants can become excellent green manures. Among the oilseeds, cotton is considered to be an important commodity because of its use in food and feed throughout the world.

Uses

Cotton is the important raw material for the Indian textile industry. Cultivated primarily for its seed fiber, the raw material for a large volume of textile products, this species is considered the most important of the cotton-yielding plants, providing the bulk of commercial cottons. Linters are of intermediate texture and shorter than those of G. barbadense. Fuzz, which is not removed in ginning, become linters in felts, upholstery, mattresses, twine, wicks, carpets, surgical cottons, and in chemical industries such as rayons, film, shatterproof glass, plastics, sausage skins, lacquers, and cellulose explosives. Pigg (1980) reports that bread, made with cottonseed protein is an even better source of protein than enriched white bread, six slices of which provide 20% of the adult RDA. Low-grade residue serves as manure, bedding and fuel (14).

Harvesting Practices
Planting to flowering is 80–110 days with another 55–80 days until the boll opens. Hand-harvesting still accounts for the largest percentage of harvest in spite of advances in mechanization. Hand methods provide a higher grade of cotton and gets more from the fields. One man can pick about 50–110 kg of seed cotton per day. On the average a two-row mechanical picker can harvest 1,400 kg of seed cotton per hour (14). The time of picking is an important aspect for maintaining fibre and seed quality. Picking should commence when the cotton is fully mature, when the bolls fully open. Several pickings may be necessary depending on the variety/hybrid since bolls ripen over one to three months. Cotton picked from 1st and 2nd pickings should be used for seed purpose, as the quality of seeds of such bolls will be excellent. Pick the ripe and completely opened bolls along with carpels. Dry the seed cotton bolls under sun for one to two days until moisture content drops to 6 to 8.5 percent. Remove the kapas from the bolls and store in cool and dry place in gunny bags until supplied to processing plant for ginning. The seeds collected can either be used for oil purpose or for breeding purpose (3).

Chemistry

The component fatty acids of Indian cottonseed oil, as recorded by various workers, are: myristic, 1.4-3.3; palmitic, 19.9-23.4; stearic, 1.1-2.7; arachidic, 0.6-1.3; oleic, 22.9-29.6; and linoleic, 45.3- 50.4%. The glycerides present are: palmitooleolinoleins, 35-40; palmitodioleins, 20; tri- unsaturated (mainly oleo-dilinoleins) , 28; and oleo- or linoleo- disaturated, 12-13 %. The unsaponifiable fraction contains ß-sitosterol and ergosterol.

The minor constituents present in the oil are free fatty acids (0.3-5.6%), gossypol (0.05%), raffinose, pentosans, resins, wax, proteoses, peptones, phospholipins, inosite phosphates, phytosterols, phytosteroline, xanthophyll, chlorophyll and mucilaginous substances (7, 15).

Processing Techniques

Pretreatment

  • High-moisture cottonseed is passed through driers where hot air or combustion gases are blown through the seed, which must then be cooled before being sent to storage. (In cool weather, i.e. below 700F. (210C.) seed containing up to 16% moisture is satisfactorily stored; above this temperature it is considered unsafe to store seed in quantity if the moisture content is above 11-12%.)

  • Seed are cleaned

  • Delinting process- similar in principle to ginning and carried out by machines known as 'linters' which subject the seed to the action of fast revolving saws.

  • Decortication. Two types of hulling machines

(i) Bar huller -seed fed between a rotating cylinder and a stationary concave member, each having projecting cutting edges which split the seed coat

(ii) Disc huller -hulls split as seeds pass from centre to periphery of opposed discs with radial cutters.

  • Separation of the split hulls and meats is accomplished in an assembly of shakers, air separators etc.

  • For oil recovery by mechanical expression or solvent extraction the separated kernels must be rolled into thin flakes between heavy smooth. Iron rolls

  • Cooking- necessary before mechanical expression and sometimes before

solvent extraction. The flaked meats are subjected to a heat, treatment, often conducted in a series of superimposed ,steam jacketed pans through which the meats are dropped by gravity. For hydraulic pressing the cooking time ranges from about 90-120 minutes, the temperature varies from, 175-1950F (79-9100C) in the top pan to about 225-2350F(107-1120C.) in the bottom pan and the moisture content is reduced from 11-12% to about 5.0-5.5%.. For prepress solvent extraction the cooking and drying are usually less severe. For direct solvent extraction flakes must be first cooked and then cooled to surface dry the particles and put them in a more granulized state. Normally conditioned by mild heating 120-130 0F (49-54°C.) and adjusting the moisture (10-11%) and then passed through flaking rolls. (When cooking is omitted before extraction, the meal obtained must be heat processed to destroy free gossypol.

Extraction

Methods of removing oil from cotton seed

(i) Hydraulic press

(ii) Screw expeller press

(iii) Solvent extraction ,

(iv) Prepress solvent extraction

(v) Filtration extraction


Hydraulic pressing - the meats are discharged from the cooker into a mechanical cake former, slabs are formed and enclosed in a press cloth. The cake produced contains 5.6-6.5% residual oil.


Continuous pressing -cottonseed is crushed in screw presses. The oil is settled to remove the bulk of the 'fine', then passed through a plate filter press and pumped to storage tanks. The press cake is ground into meal and then ground cottonseed hulls are added to adjust the protein content to trade standards. The meal contains 2.5-4.0% oil.


Solvent extraction- the oil-solvent solution is filtered  and clarified, unless a percolation type extractor is used. The miscella is passed through a long tube extractor which recovers 90% of the solvent. The oil concentrate is then passed through a stripping column where sparging steam is applied to remove the remaining solvent. The meal from solvent extraction usually contains about 1% residual oil.

Crude oil is then refined. It is freed of most of its nonglyceride constituents by treatment with alkali and then subsequently bleached or deodorized (4, 15).

Other Uses
Cottonseed and roots have been used in nasal polyps, uterine fibroids and other types of cancer. Gossypol has shown anticancer activity. Mucilaginous tea of fresh or roasted seeds used for bonchitis, diarrhea, dysentry, and hemorrhage. Flowers diuretic and emollient, used for hypochondriasis. Leaves steeped in vinegar applied to the forehead for headache. Often used by early American slaves for abortion; apparently with no serious side effects. In Guinea, leaves and seeds considered emollient and roots emmenagogue. About 100 g root was boiled in about a liter of water until reduced by half. 50 g of the resultant witches brew was then drunk about every half hour. Root decocotion used for asthma, diarrhea, and dysentery. Root bark, devoid of tannin, astringent, antihemorrhoidal, used as an emmenagogue, hemostat, lactagogue, oxytocic, parturient, and vasoconstrictor. Gossypol is being used in China as a male contraceptive (14).

Miscellaneous Information

It contains a polyphenolic pigment, gossypol, which is toxic to mono-gastric animals including humans but not to ruminants. Gossypol is toxic in the free form. However, when it is bound to the protein it is harmless. Since gossypol binds to the protein mainly through the lysine groups, the nutritive value of the protein decreases due to a decrease in the available lysine content. Essentially, three approaches have been used to obtain edible cottonseed flour, low in gossypol content. They are:

(1) screw pressing of the seed under optimum conditions

(2) direct solvent extraction and

(3) physical separation of gossypol glands by liquid cyclone process.

In all the cases the dehulled seed/meat is used.

In the first process the seeds, after selection and careful cleaning, are subjected to cooking and expelling at the right moisture level and temperature for a minimum period. The conditions are so chosen that free gossypol enters the oil and is bound least to the protein (gossypol in the oil is removed during alkali refining of the oil). After grinding, the flour is subjected to sieving or air classification to reduce the fibre content.

In the solvent extraction method the cottonseed meal is extracted with either acetone or an azeotropic mixture of hexane, acetone and water, in which gossypol is soluble. This yields a cake with low gossypol content. However, the use of acetone leads to a catty flavour in the cake.

In the liquid cyclone process the cottonseed meat, in which as little rupture of the pigment glands as possible has occurred, is conditioned ,to a moisture content of 1.5-3.0 per cent and is suspended in hexane to form a slurry containing 15-20 per cent solids. This slurry is pumped under optimum pressure through the tangential opening of a stainless steel liquid cyclone to separate the two fractions. The over-flow contains finely divided protein flour essentia1ly free of gossypol while the underflow contains principally the pigment glands, coarse meal particles, etc. The flour is washed to remove the oil, desolventised and dried (16).

Species of glandless cottonseed, which is free from, gossypol, have been developed. These do not appear to have been used in commercial cultivation. They are reported to be highly susceptible to insect attack.
