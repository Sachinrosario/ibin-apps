Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

An erect, short lived biennial or perennial herb grown worldwide for its leaves.

Climate and soil

A temperate plant prefers cool climate. Grows best between 7-16˚C, above 600 m altitude and rainfall of 500 mm. Parsley should have a deep, moist fertile soil for ideal growth, and sun or a little shade (2). Well drained sandy loam to medium loam soil is best suitable with pH ranging from 4.9-8.2. Heavy clayey soils are not suitable for its cultivation (1).


Availability :

Native of Mediterranean region. Cultivated in most Mediterranean and temperate countries. In tropics it is cultivated only in small-scales. Old crop well known in classical Greece and Rome (1).

Conservation/ Threat Status : Cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES: 

Erect, copiously branched, biennial to perennial herb, 30-100 cm tall, aromatic in all parts, glabrous. Root system slender, fibrous, taproot up to 1 m long, white, sometimes thickened, with a radical rosette of leaves when young. Stem terete, sulcate, hollow. Leaves alternate, 1-3 pinnately compound, dark green, glossy, flat or curled, sheathing at base; petioles longest in lower leaves; pinnae long stalked, leaflets obovate-cuneate to finely linear, divided into acute segments, 2.5 cm long, higher leaves gradually less divided, topmost one consisting of a few acute segments only and forms basal clusters in first year (1).

  

Back to Top

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR: 

Inflorescence a terminal or axillary compound umbel; bracts 1-3 foliate, rather short; peduncle up to 12 cm long; primary rays 5-20, 1-5 cm long; bracteoles 3-8-foliate; secondary rays (pedicels) 3-15, 2-5 mm long; flowers small, yellow-green, bisexual; calyx obscure; corolla consisting of 5 petals, each one suborbicular to obovate, up to 1 mm x 0.5 mm, submarginate with an inflexed apical lobe; stamens 5; pistil with inferior, 2-carpelled ovary, each carpel with a thickened stylopodium, a style and a globose stigma (1).

 Fruit

Fruit a schizocarp, ovoid, 2-3 mm long, at maturity splitting into 2 mericarps (1).

 

Breeding system and varietal improvement: Propagated by seeds.

 
Phenology:
Flowering and fruiting: July – September.

Floral and seed biology:

Seed Biology

Seeds are tiny, black and each mericarp is with 5 narrow ribs (1).

 

Seed germination

Seeds are very slow to germinate and takes 25-30 days under field.

 

Seedling development

Early development is slow.

 

Local cultivars

Three main types (cultivar groups) are there:

cv. group Curly-leaved parsley (also referred to as var. crispum) – Has compact branches with curled leaf blades and is used to flavour food products, but especially as a garnish, and in the preparation of Arab salad dishes. Cultivars commonly grown are: ‘Afro’, ‘Perfection’, ‘Banquet’, ‘Forest Green’, ‘Decorator’, ‘Moss Curled’, ‘Deep Green’, ‘Improved Market Gardener’ and ‘Dark Moss Colored’. Fresh herb essential oil – 0.07-0.15%.

cv. group Flat-leaved parsley (including var. latifolium Airy-Shaw, var. filicinum Bailey with fern-like leaves, and var. neaploitanum Danert or Italian parsley) – Has more open branches with flat leaf-blades. It is stronger flavoured than the curly-leaved type and is mainly used in flavouring sauces, soups and stews. Cultivars commonly grown are: ‘Plain’ and ‘Plain Italian Dark Green’. Fresh herb essential oil – 0.04%.

cv. group Turnip-rooted parsley (also referred to as var. tuberosum (Bernh.) Mart. Crov. or Hamburg or Dutch parsley, var. radicosum Alefeld) – Has long fern-like leaves and is more robust. Cultivated for its thickened aromatic edible root. Leaves may be used for flavouring dishes. Cultivars are: ‘Early Sugar’, ‘Fakir’ and ‘Hamburg Thick Rooted’. Fresh herb essential oil – 0.05% (1).    

Chromosome number 2n=22

Selection of planting material

Seeds are the planting material.

 

Planting

Both transplanting and sowing directly into field is practiced. Being slow to germinate it is better to sown in green house/beds or nursery beds. Sowing rates range from 7-12 kg/ha when grown for fresh for market, 20-25 kg/ha for industrial purposes. 40-60 kg/ha for direct sowing is also reported. 2-3 ploughings are to be done and bring the land to fine tilth before sowing. Seeds are sown in October - in low hills, August to September - in mid hills, March to April - in high hills. In plains parsley is grown for leaves during September-November. Seeds are sown deep in double rows of 40-45 cm apart and seedlings thinning to 10 cm apart. Seedlings grown in nursery can be transplanted when they have 6 leaves or are 5-8 cm tall. For transplanting crop, seedlings are 5-8 cm length is planted in field at 10-15 cm apart during evening hours. Soaking seeds in water a day before or brief freezing hastens germination (1).

 Management after planting

Weeding should be done by hoeing, cultivation or mulching. Glyphosate and Chlorbromuron (herbicides) are recommended. Fertilizer can be broadcast before planting and N side-dressed after germination and after the first leaf harvest, to maintain a continuous production of leaves. 10-12 t/h well rotten FYM and NPK @ 100:80:40 kg/h, half dose of N and full dose of P & K should be applied as basal during soil preparation. The balance 50 kg N and topdressing in two splits at an interval of 30 and 60 days after germination. 4-8 irrigations are required when it is grown as winter crop in plains for foliage production. When grown on temperate crop irrigation is not essential of there are well distributed rains.


MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Diseases:

Cercospora blight – Caused by Cercospora apii.

Symptoms: Disease first appears on older leaves and subsequently on stem, causing circular brownish spots with concentric rings.

Control: Treat with Thiram or Captan or Bavistin 2.5 g/kg of seed or hot water dipping of the seeds at 48˚C for 30 minutes. Spraying of 0.2% copper oxychloride at 10-15 days interval.

Septoria leaf spot – Caused by Septoria apiicola.

Symptoms: Small circular water soaked areas, turning to black on the tips and margin of the leaves and spread to other parts of the plant.

Control: Spray Dithane 2.78 (0.2%) 10-15 days interval.

Damping off - Caused by Pythium sp.

Bacterial soft rot – Caused by Pseudomonas apii. Soil-borne disease.

Symptoms: Appear as circular reddish spots with pale yellow borders on leaves.

Control: Adopt crop rotation and regular spray of plamtomycin 100ppm.

Parsley mosaic – A viral disease transmitted by aphids causing leaf mottling in parsley.

Control: Use healthy seeds, spray malathion 50 EC (0.1%) to control vectors.

Other diseases are aster yellows (prevented by controlling leaf hoppers), stem rot (white mould), Texas root rot, heart rot (can be prevented by Ca application and careful watering) and several viruses (1).

 

Nematode Induced Diseases

Also infects parsley.



Pests:
Important pests are carrot fly (Psila rosae), aphids transmitting carrot mottle dwarf virus, several caterpillars and plant bugs (1).

Control: Spray malathion 50 EC (0.1%).

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Both dried and fresh leaves are aromatic and used as spice. Fresh leaves of curly-leaved cultivars are used for garnishing and seasoning, while those of flat-leaved cultivars are mainly used to flavour dishes, playing a role in western cooking similar to that of coriander leaves in India. Leaves are also eaten as ‘lablab’ with rice or in mixed vegetables and are sometimes fried in butter or oil. Dried leaves, known as parsley flakes, are used to flavour soups, meat and fish dishes, vegetables and salads. Fresh or dried leaf for sauces, potatoes, as part of bouquet garni, with egg dishes, for omelette, marinades, in casseroles added at start and near end of cooking. Tasty tea can be prepared and are ingredient of some types of savory biscuits. Taproot of turnip-rooted cultivars is eaten as an aromatic vegetable, their leaves are used as those of flat-leaved cultivars. Essential oil from the fruits and herb are used as a seasoning agent in all kinds of food products, especially meats, sausages and sauces. Oil is occasionally used in perfumery. The juice from leaves is used as an insecticide. Seed is used as spice (1). In cosmetics to prevent thread veins in the complexion, said to remove wrinkles. Domestically, used as a dye, to provide cream or shades of green depending on mordant (2).

Parts Used:  

Leaf, flowering top, seed.

Harvesting Practices:  

Leaves are ready for harvest in 90-100 days, when the plants are 20 cm tall. Leaves are periodically, cut off and marketed. Harvesting is done manually by grasping the stalks of the outer leaves, cutting them off with a knife and tying them together to maintain bunch integrity. New leaves develop from the centre of the plant for later harvests. Plants are also harvested by slicing off the top of the taproot. Roots of turnip-rooted cultivar are harvested by digging manually. Umbels are harvested 4-5 times depending upon maturity of umbels at the right stage usually just before the primary umbels shatter as ripe seed of parsley shatters easily. The seeds are harvested when mature. Seeds from king umbels are superior to subsequent stage umbel. The seeds are cleaned dried is stored in at room temperature in airtight containers or distilled immediately for recovery of more essential oil. The average yield is about 550 kg/ha, the genetic potential of the crop being 1800 kg/ha (1).

Post Harvest Handling

For fresh and crispy herb, parsley is washed and packed on ice in styrofoam boxes. For parsley flakes, fresh leaves are rapidly dried under controlled conditions of temperature and humidity to retain the fresh green colour and the essential oil content. Dried parsley are packed in polythene-lined corrugated fibre cases or well-sealed metal containers. They should be stored under cool, dry conditions. Steam distillation of the shoot yield parsley herb oil and that of mature fruits yield parsley fruit oil (1).

 


Produce and Products:  

Parsley leaves, seeds, herb oil, seed oil and oleoresins are the commercial products.

 Parsley herb oil is pale yellow to greenish-yellow liquid with an odour described as unusual, warm-spicy, very leafy, yet like the fresh herb. Flavour is warm, but slightly burning and bitter. It is used exclusively in food industry. Composition varies with its origin. Odour of parsley fruit oil is described as warm-woody, spicy and somewhat sweet-herbaceous, but not directly reminiscent of the fresh herb. Flavour is rich and deep, aromatic and warm-spicy, but rather bitter. Used in perfumery to impart a special note to a variety of perfumes, but main use is in food industry, to flavour meat sauces, seasonings, canned food, pickles and also to flavour tobacco (1).

 

Medicinal uses

Parsley has diuretic, carminative, emmenagogue and antipyretic properties and is effectively used to control hypertension. Intake of large quantities of fruit induces abortion. Used in products against halitosis. Milk secretion can be suppressed by applying leaves several times in a day to the breasts. Bruised leaves are used as a poultice for sore eyes and can be applied to insect bites and stings. Fruits used to get rid of lice and skin parasites. Parsley tea has carminative and diuretic properties and aids in digestion. Essential oil component apiol has been used to treat kidney ailments, menstrual problems, is an abortifacient. Large doses may cause paralysis and death while minute doses helps cure epileptic fits. People working with parsley are affected by dermatitis (1).  Fresh or dried leaf, dried root, dried seed contains V.T.C; as a tonic and stomachic, for flatulence, a diuretic and of considerable help in many kidney complaints (2).

 

Chemistry:

Leaves, stem and fruits contain a glucoside ‘Apin’. Two types of essential oils are extracted by steam distillation: fruit (seed) oil and herb oil. Fully ripe fruits give the highest yield of oil, ranging from 1.5-7.0%, main component being apiol. True odour and flavour is given by the herb oil obtained from flowering tops. The aerial parts contain furanocoumarins (mainly bergapten) and glucosides (derivatives of glucose and apiose with apigenin and luteolin as glucon parts).

 Maximum permitted level of leaf oil is about 1.5% in processed vegetables and of oleoresin about 0.05% in condiments. Dried parsley contains per 100 g: water – 9.0 g, protein – 22.4 g, fat – 4.4 g, carbohydrates – 41.4 g, fibre – 10.3 g, ash – 12.5 g (Ca – 1.5 g, Fe – 98 mg, Mg – 249 mg, P – 351 mg, K – 3.8 g, Na – 452 mg, Zn – 4 mg), ascorbic acid – 122 mg, thiamine – 0.172 mg, riboflavin – 1.23 mg, niacin – 7.93 mg, vitamin B6 – 1.00 mg, vitamin A – 23340 IU. The energy value is about 1155 kJ/100 g. fresh parsley loses 80-90% of its weight on drying. In US, regulatory status ‘Generally regarded as safe’ (GRAS) is accorded to parsley (GRAS 2835), parsley leaf oil (GRAS 2836) and parsley oleoresin (GRAS 2837) (1).
