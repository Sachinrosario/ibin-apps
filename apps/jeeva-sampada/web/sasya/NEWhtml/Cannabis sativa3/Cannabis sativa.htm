Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:

Hemp is annual, rough, strong selling plant. It is a native of central Asia. It occurs wild throughout the Western Himalayas, cultivated in valleys of Himalayas in Himachal Pradesh and plains from Kashmir eastwards to Assam. It is grown in UP, MP and Orissa. Wild growth found in Tamil Nadu, Rajasthan, Bihar and Kerala.
Availability : Plenty


Conservation/ Threat Status : Wild and cultivated.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Hemp is an erect plant with variable height from 1-5 mt. The stem is slender, angular and grooved. The leaves are 7-20 cm long, palmately compound, 3-11 partite, stalked, leaflets are sessile, narrow-lanceolate, serrate, upper surface scabard, lower slightly hairy. Male flowers yellow in short dense cymes uniting into a lax, foliate terminal panicle. Female flowers light green, solitary in the axils of small membranous bracts. Fruit is achene, smooth, shining, 4-5mm long.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Hemp is dioecious. The staminate flowers develop in small, drooping, branched panicles, which arise in the axis of leaves. The flowers of the panicle may occur singly or in slender pedicels or in group, and usually the branches bear these flowers, a median and one or two laterals which are subtended by bracts or stipules. The individual flowers are apetalous with a deep by parted calyx having fine greenish yellow or red lobes that are widespread at maturity. There are five stamens and the anthers are suspended from long thread like filaments. The staminate plants became yellow after the pollen is shed, and die, whereas the pistillate plants remain alive and green on additional month or more until the seed are fully matured.

The pistillate flowers are borne on short spikes on a more or less bushy and leafy shoot. The flowers are closely aggregated at the apex of the inflorescence and are produced in pairs on the axil of a leaf, each flower being subtended by a stipule. The pistillate flower is relatively simple in structure, consisting of a pistil surrounded by a transparent, more or less papery perianth. In its normal position, the green scabrous floral bract with its overlapping edges completely surrounds the mature flower except for the exerted stigmas.

The fruit is an achene which is light brown to dark gray and is often mottled. The fruit is smooth, somewhat compressed and articular or oval in form, but it varies in size, as does the enclosed seed.

Usually, the epichalazal megaspore is functional. The development of female gametophyte is of the polygonum type. Hypostoe is subterized .



Breeding system and varietal improvement:

Breeding System - Hemp is dioecious, and the number of staminate and pistllate flowers is relatively constant under normal conditions. Associated with the dioecious condition is a distinct vegetative dimorphism. The staminate plants are taller and more slender than the pistillate, and the staminate inflorescences have few leaves. The pistillate plants are shorter and more stocky, with a broad crown of leaves associated with the terminal inflorescence.

Although hemp is dioecious, it is not uncommon for an individual plant to bear both staminate and pistillate flowers. The occurrence of monoecious plants is more common when the daily exposure to light is short, and under experimental conditions it has been possible to modify and control to some degree the production of staminate and pistillate flowers .

Pollinators: The plants are wind-pollinated and large numbers of white, spherical pollen grains are liberated through terminal porus

Floral and seed biology:

Seed Biology :
Seedling germination - Hemp should be planted on well prepared land. Hemp is never sown on spring-ploured sod. Chemical fertilizers usually are applied to the soil in the spring just before final land preparation, and usually are applied broadcast followed by disking and leveling.

A review of early literature on hemp gives the impression that this crop requires very little fertilization, provided it is planted or good soil. Dewey (1913) states that hemp improves the physical condition of the soil and destroys weeds. When setted on the ground, as is the common practice, it does not exhaust the soil fertility.

Almost all hemp is planted with narrow-width seed drills, usually ranging from 8 to 15 cm apart. Hemp is planted as early as possible in the spring after the danger of heavy freezer is past. Since hemp is sensitive to photoperiod, the earlier plantings produce better crops. Delayed plantings result in lowered fiber yields. Depending upon the area and rainfall conditions, hemp usually is planted between early March and late May in the northern latitude.

Relatively few hemp growers treat hemp seed with a fungicide before planting because most of the hemp is grown by small holders. If hemp seed are treated with a fungicide prior to planting, there will be impoved germination and less plant mortality from attacks by Fusarium and other fungi.

Wilsie and Reddy (1946) found that hemp seed treated with Sperger, Ceresan, tetramethylthiuran disulphide (Arasan) and ethyl mercuric phosphate (Semesan Jr.) increased hemp stands 100% in one trial. Zhalmina (1969) states that hemp seed treated with a mercurial fungicide at the rate of 300 g/100 kg of seed or a noncommercial fungicide at 600 g/100 kg of seed markedly reduced the rate of seedlings killed by Fusarium sp., increasing the plant population and yields of stems and fiber.

High seeding rates (100-140 kg of seed per hectare) increases plant mortality, but closer spacing produces stems with a smaller diameter and high fiber content, which may be handled more readily with harvesting equipment. Lower seeding rates ranges from 40-50 kg/ha. Most hemp does not require cultivation after planting unless it is planted in wide rours or at low seeding rate


MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
One of he principal pests of hemp is the phaneroganic parasite known as brancheol broom rape (Orobanche ramose). Broom rape roots are parasitic on the roots of hemp, and plants that are attacked die before they reach maturity. The best control measures for branched broom rape are strict rotation and the use of clean seed.

The bulb on stem nematode (Ditylenchus dipsaei) is another pests that has been reported. Various types of dinibing venes or kind weeds sometimes cause considerable trouble in harvesting hemp. These include black kind weed (Polygonum convoluvulus L.) Veter (Vieia sp.) and morning glory (Convolvulus sepium L.).

Insect pests : The insect pests of hemp are few and scattered, being confined principally to leaf, eating larvae, most of which can be controlled by DDT. The main species are as follows : Lepidoptera; Sphinx atroposa L., Plusia gamma L., Mamestra persicariae r., Boty's silacealis Hb., Psyche cannebiella Doum., Heliothis armigera Hb. (seed eater); Coleoptera : Podagrica aerata Mish., Homoptera : Aphis cannabis Paso and Hemiptera : Psylliodes attenuata.


Diseases:

The principal disease found in hemp are caused by seed and soil borne fungi. These diseases are not widespread and acque sporadically. Tey normally may be controlled by seed treatment before planting with non-mercurial or organic material fungicides, the use of resistant variety, or by rotation. These diseases include : leaf spot (Septoria cannabis Lasch) Sacc); stripe disease (Pseudomonas canabina (Lutic and Dowson); gray mold (Botrytis cierea Fr); hemp canker (Sclerotinia sclerotiorum (Lib) Mass.); brown leaf spot (Macrosporium canabinum); damping off (Pythium sp); and wilt (Fusarium sp.)

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
fiberS -
Used for making soil-cloth, tarpaulins, canvas good twine, mixed with woolen yarn to make bed sheets, shawls and shoulder bags, made into rapes, fishing nets, non slippery shoes.

PULP - The cellulose pulp is sued for making printing paper and textiles.
MEDICINAL -
Used for dyspepsia, colid and diarrhoea. Also used in treating depression, high blood pressure, glaucoma, menstrual disorders, labour pains, infection of urinary tracts. It is used as sedative, analgesic, antispasmodic, pain relieving and antibacterial activities.

Ganja is used as pills with bran or grass to bullocks as a tonic to relieve fatigue and to impart additional staying power, good remedy against intestinal worms and footsore disease. Also used as an adjunct to conventional aesthetics

Parts Used:

Dried leaves, flowers, resinous matters.


Harvesting Practices:
Plant is ready for harvesting (for fiber) after 4-5 months. Harvesting is started from the periphery of the field. Plants are cut of 2-3 cm from the ground and they are spread on the floor to dry. Harvesting must be done when the male flowers are fully out.

Produce and Products:

Narcotics - Bhang, Charas, Ganja and Hashish
Chemistry:
Chemical analysis of hemp showed water (hygroscopic), 8.88; ash, 0.82; fat and wax, 0.56; aqueous extract; 3.48; cellulose 77.77; and intercellular matter and pectin bodies, 9.31%

Processing Techniques:
Hemp fiber may be freed by either pond or steam ritting. The later is quicker but more expensive and generally produces poorer fiber. In Korea, cut stems are tied in bundles and placed in a closed chamber where water is poured over hot stones to produce the steam. 2/3 hrs is sufficient to loosen the bark, which is then stripped form the stem tissues by hand. Pond setting on the other hand, requires 1 wreck or more and is satisfactory only when the water temperature ranges between 11 and 28oC. Following pond setting the stems are dried in the open, then crushed to pursuit shaking the stem core loose from the fibrous bark. Pond is preferred to steam setting
Other uses:
Seed oil is used as drying oil in paints, varnish, soaps, insect repellent.
Seeds are roasted and eaten in the hilly regions, added in food to improve taste and flavor
Seed cake can be used for feeding purposes .
In the southern part of eastern China, fiber from green, freshly harvested stems is used for paper-making .

In the Rif mountains of Morocea, the leaves are separated from pods or seeds, then cut up by a small hand sickle into very minute particles. It is then mixed with uncured tobacco before smoking .
