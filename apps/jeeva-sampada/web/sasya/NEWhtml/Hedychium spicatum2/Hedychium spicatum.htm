Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
A perennial rhizomatous herb growing to 1.5m by 0.7m. occurring in parts of
Western and Central Himalayas at an altitude of 3,500-7500 ft.

The plant prefers light (sandy), medium (loamy) and heavy (clay) soils. The plant prefers acid, neutral and basic (alkaline) soils. It cannot grow in the shade.  It requires moist soil.

Tough species, hardy to -15C and to 90-100 cm rainfall



Availability :  
Found in Nepal, Eastern Himalayas, Bhutan and Northeast India.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
An herbaceous, ornamental-leaved and flowering perennial.  Stems grow to about 4 ft tall with rather thin leaves. Winter dormant. The stems die down in late autumn. Those bearing seed capsules remain green till seeds are ripe around the middle of winter. Some plants exhibit a marked red/brown tinge on the back of the leaves and the intensity of the colouration varies. Flowers in summer, Rhizome horizontal, fleshy 1-2 inches thick. Leaves are glabrous  beneath and the white ascending flowers are borne in dense terminal spikes.

This species makes stocky growth and produces beautiful, individually large flowers. These open from primrose buds to reveal pure white petals with contrasting stamens of bright scarlet. The flowers look exactly like big white moths at rest. These are followed in mid autumn by startling fruits, which are very readily produced, and comprise apricot seed cases that split open to reveal a vivid orange waxy interior with deep crimson seeds.

Seed Biology :

 

Seedling Germination :
Hedychium spicatum can be easily raised from seed and if sown at 20C, lightly covered in a general seed compost, germination will take place within 3 - 4 weeks.  This can be sporadic with seedlings appearing for up to 6 months from the initial sowing.  As soon as the young plantlets have reached several cms. in height, they can be individually potted into 10 cms. pots using a good, loam based compost.  Young plants raised this way will need protection from frost for the first winter at least and should not be planted out until in full growth during the second summer following germination.

The main problem with raising from seed, is that it is very difficult to find and plants take at least 4 years to reach flowering size in a typical southern garden.  A better, more immediate solution is to divide clumps when they start into growth during late spring.  The entire plant should be carefully lifted and a sharp spade driven through the root mass, cutting it into portions containing at least one or two new growths.

Division as growth commences in the spring. Dig up the clump and divide it with a sharp spade or knife, making sure that each division has a growing shoot. Larger clumps can be planted out direct into their permanent positions, but it is best to pot up the smaller divisions and grow them on in a greenhouse until they are established. Plant them out in the summer or late in the following spring.

Cultivation:

Hedychium spicatum forms a most attractive, rather elegant clump of foliage for much of the summer, finally erupting into a blaze of orange scarlet at a time when flowers are few and far between.  Ideally, the plant should be allowed to develop into a bold clump and is most effective when given a prominent position where it can rise above its neighbours in the border.  If given a sufficiently large, thick-walled tub, this species will also add grace and an air of tropical luxuriance to any patio. Although this 'Ginger Lily' will tolerate light shade and even produce a few flowers spikes under such conditions, full sun is required to promote the brightest colouration and develop strong, ripe, stems which will bear substantial flower spikes. 

Ideal growing conditions are provided in free-draining, yet humus laden soils in a sunny, sheltered site.  Given these, Hedychium spicatum will grow away with the minimum amount of effort and start growth comparatively early, thus allowing the plant to achieve its greatest potential.  If planted on thin, poor soils, a good amount of well-rotted manure should be incorporated before planting and plenty of extra water supplied during hot, dry weather.  Even in mild areas, the clump will benefit from a thick layer of dried leaves or other insulating material during winter.

This species can be grown as an impressive specimen in the greenhouse where, given a little heat and a brightly lit spot, it will continue to grow throughout the year - new growths reappearing to flower after spent flowering 'canes' have been cut down.

Requires a rich moist soil and a sunny position. They can be grown in a sunny border as a summer sub-tropical bedding plant.

The flowers are sweetly scented, the scent being most pronounced towards evening.

Both the bruised and the dried root are very aromatic with a fragrant, somewhat pungent smell that is similar to orris root but more powerful.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests: 

Green fly will sometimes cause distortion of new growths and can severely affect early flower spikes.  These can be easily controlled with regular sprays of soft soap - mild washing-up liquid is ideal. 

Slugs and snails create havoc with the new shoots as they rise through the soil, care needs to be taken to ensure that they do not destroy all of the current season's growth.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts used
Rhizome, leaves

Uses

  • The root or rhizome has strong perfume and is used in dyeing scent cloth, also to scent tobacco and as a medicine.
  • Employed in the preparation of Abir , a fragrant coloured powder used during Holi and other religious ceremonies.  
  • Cosmetic powder used for promoting hair growth. Veterinary medicine.

Other Uses 
  1. Rhizome possess strong aromatic odour and bitter comphoraceous taste. They have  insect-repelling properties and used for preserving clothes. Stomachic, carminative, stimulant and tonic  and are used in dyspepsia in the form of powder or decoction.  
  2. The rootstock is carminative, emmenagogue, expectorant, stimulant, stomachic and tonic. It is useful in the treatment of liver complaints, and is also used in treating vomiting, diarrohea, inflammation, pains and snake bit.
  3. The root is used in Tibetan medicine, it is said to have an acrid taste and heating potency. It is digestive, stomachic and vasodilator. It is used in the treatment of indigestion and poor circulation due to thickening of the blood.
  4. The rootstock yields 4% essential oil. This oil, which has a scent somewhat like hyacinths, is so powerful that a single drop will render clothes highly perfumed for a considerable period.
  5. The dried root is burnt as an incense. Both the bruised and the dried root are very aromatic with a fragrant, somewhat pungent smell similar to orris root but more powerful.
  6. Oil used as perfume for soaps, hair oils and face powders and as incense.
  7. Leaves woven into mats.
  8. Dried fruit is reported to be added to soften meat and pulses during cooking  
  9. Fruit - cooked. Eaten in savory dishes with lentils. 
  10. The flowers are sweetly scented, the scent being most pronounced towards evening
