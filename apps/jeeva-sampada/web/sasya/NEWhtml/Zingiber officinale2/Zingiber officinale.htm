Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Zingiber officinale Rosc., a herbaceous, perennial monocotyledon, is a member of the Zingiberaceae family. The Zingiber genus consists of about 80-90 species all of which are said to be growing throughout tropical and subtropical Asia and North Australia (Willis 1966) (1). The generic name Zingiber is derived from the Tamil word ‘ingiver’ meaning root of ‘ingi’ or ginger. Ginger originated in South East Asia, probably in India (Burkill, 1966; Purseglove et al., 1981). The ginger of commerce is the dried rhizome of Z. officinale.

Availability :

Cultivated throughout India, run wild in some places of the Western Ghats.

Conservation/ Threat Status : Wild and cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES: Ginger is a herbaceous plant, 30-100 cm tall, the underground part of which consists of an elongated, creeping multi-branched, irregular, fleshy, pungent rhizome. Each rhizome is covered with scale leaves and many fine fibrous roots, which branch throughout the surface layers of soil. 


The rhizomes, which are grey-white to pale brown in colour, produce at intervals erect, leafy stems, which can grow to a height of 2-3 feet, are covered with sheathing leaf bases. The inner core of the rhizome is pale yellow with bluish tinge while the outer is light yellow. Adventitious roots and tubular roots arising from the nodes of the scales. The anxillary buds shoot up as leafy stem known as pseudostem which die out annually but the plant continue to live through its rhizome (Purseglove 1975, Daniel Sundararaja and Thulasidas 1976). The leaves, which are arranged alternately, are light green in colour, smooth, sessile and may be lanceolate, 8-12 in number. Each blade has a well developed central rib, the lamina being well supplied with parallel veins. Although the leaf, which is 6-9 inches long, is strongly pointed, it tends to fold inwards. The flowers are borne on a bracteal imbricated spike produced on a separate leafless stem which usually reached about 12 inches in height. The spike is condensed, oblong and cylindric with numerous bracts that are imbricate, persistent and each carrying a single flower. In some plants, it has been observed that the inflorescence terminates a leafy shoot. The inflorescence, which is a 25-35 inch long cane-like spike, is made up of a number of 0.5 inch long greenish-yellow bracts. Between these bracts, small flowers appear at intervals, usually one at a time (1).

Climate and soil
Ginger requires a warm and humid climate. Plant thrives well from sea level to an altitude of 1,500 m, and optimum elevation between 300 and 900 m. A well distributed rainfall (150-300 cm) during the growing season and dry spells during land preparation as well as before harvesting are required for large scale cultivation. In areas receiving less rainfall crop requires regular irrigation. It can grow on heavy laterite loam, clayey loam, red laterite, black soil etc. the crop prefers light shade for good growth, but, not absolutely necessary (Mudaliar, 424; Gupta. loc. cit., Kannan and Nair, lic. cit.) (6).  

In South East Asia, where ginger must have originated, it thrives in a hot, moist climate. In Kerala, the main ginger producing state in India, this crop is grown purely as a rainfed crop (April-May to December), in areas from sea-level to 800 m or more. Ginger is also grown successfully as an irrigated crop in areas of less rainfall in some of the northern states of India. It is also grown in areas high altitude (1200 to 1500 m) as in Himachal Pradesh and in the hill tracts of Assam and West Bengal. In North and Central India, it is grown as irrigated crop. In Eastern India planted in March (6).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR: The flowers are numerous, trimerous, bisexual, irregular, epigynous, pale yellow in colour and occasionally speckled with a dark purple lip which is less brightly speckled. The perianth segments, cylindric, are differentiated into short calyx tube and a corolla tube which opens at the tip into an unequal pointed trilobed structure. The stamen, which has two short anthers, is borne on a short filament. Anther cells are contiguous, produced into a long beak. The delicate, slender style, which passes in between the two anthers, is held by them. There is tricarpellary, three-celled, inferior ovary containing several ovules which are arranged in axile placentation. Stigma is small and sub-globose. The fruit, which is an oblong three-valved capsule containing several small black, angular, large, arrilate and perispermous seeds, is rarely produced (2).  

Breeding system and varietal improvement: Ginger is a sterile crop as it does not set fruit. Hence, it is purely a vegetative crop, propagated by vegetative means, through the rhizomes.

Different types of ginger
Jamaican ginger: Best quality ginger. Rhizomes are unbleached and devoid of outer suberised layers. Length of rhizome is 7-12 cm and thickness  cm. The rhizome is pale-yellowish to yellowish-orange externally. The fracture is short and uneven, mealy, fibrous and resinous. It is pleasantly pungent and aromatic.
Indian ginger: More starchy and pungent. It has a faint lemon-like odour due to the presence of a small quantity of citral (Wallis, 389; Youngken, 223; Claus et al., 213).
There are two types of Indian ginger, mainly
1. Cochin ginger, which comes from central Kerala, is peeled type, light brown to yellowish grey externally. 
2. Calicut ginger, from Malabar region, is orange or reddish brown, resembling African ginger. It is inferior to Cochin ginger.
Another type of ginger is also there but of less demand, Calcutta ginger, which is grayish brown to greyish blue externally.
African ginger: Mostly unpeeled, darker than Cochin ginger in bulk, appears discoloured, fracture is short or short-fibrous, odour strongly aromatic and taste pungent. 
Chinese ginger: It is white and free from fibre. Inferior in aroma to Jamaican and consists of rhizomes which are not fully ripe. The absence of fibre in rhizome makes this type very suitable for pickling.
Japanese ginger: Weakly pungent, aromatic and like Cochin ginger. Obtained from Z. mioga Rosc.
Martinique ginger: Obtained from Z. zerumbet Rosc. ex Sm. (Claus et al., 213).

Major diseases of ginger
Soft Rot: The most devastating disease of ginger is soft rot or rhizome rot. This disease is caused by one or more of the following fungi (Anon. 1965): Pythium aphanidermatum (Edson) Fitzp., P. vexans de Bary and Sclerotium rolfsii Sacc (Abbaiah and Subbayya 1978). In India P. aphanidermatum is the most common pathogenic fungus at normal altitudes, but at higher altitudes P. vexans becomes more prevalent. A heavy rainfall and continued dampness caused by inadequate soil drainage is considered to be optimum for soft rot fungal attack. Fungal spores are generally transmitted through the soil and seed rhizomes.

Symptoms: The early symptom of this disease is yellowing of the leaf margins, tip and sheath which gradually spreads through the entire leaf and petiole. Within a short time (Khan 1958), wilting sets in and the leaves dry up. Rhizome becomes soft, caused by the complete breakdown and decay of all of the inner tissue of the rhizome. The disease is often accompanied by a foul smell emitted from the decayed material. In early stages, an infected rhizome has ash coloured patches or a fuzzy growth on its surface and it begins to soften. In 1980, Joshi and Sharma reported that in addition to P. vexans and P. aphanidermatum other Pythium species have also been isolated from ginger with soft rot disease, e.g., P. butleri Subram., P. complectens Braun (Park 1934), P. deliense Meurs, P. gracile (de Bary) Schrenk, P. myriotylum Drechsler and P. graminicolum Subram. P. zingiberum Takahashi (Ichitani and Shinsu 1980) has also been found to cause damage to Japanese ginger. 

Control: (Kannan and Nair 1966): Select planting sites which have adequate drainage, Rotate crops, use only plump, firm healthy rhizome pieces from disease-free areas as planting material, treat seed rhizomes with fungicide Ceresan (0.25%) for 30 minutes and shade dry them before storage, apply the fungicide to the entire area at the time of planting, once the early signs of the disease are detected, drench the ginger beds with a chestnut compound fungicide @ 1.75 cc per ginger plant. Dithane M-45 (Mancozeb), Bavistin Benkate, Kitazin (Sharma and Joshi 1979), Aerofungin also controls storage rot.

Bacterial Wilt: The bacterial wilt of ginger is caused by the bacterium Pseudomonas solanacearum E. F. Smith (Haywood et al. 1967), a common disease forming bacteria of the tomato and potato. The wilt initially infects the lower leaves of the ginger plant and then spreads rapidly upwards.

Symptoms: Whole plant becomes golden brown and wilted. In more advanced stages (Ishii and Aragaki 1963), the base of the stems easily break away from the rhizome as it becomes quite watery. Affected rhizomes appear greyish-brown with transparent patches which when cut exude a milky substance.

Fusarium Yellows: According to Teakle (1965) this disease is caused by a distinct form of Fusarium oxysporum known as Fusarium oxysporum f. zingiberi (Schlect.) Synd. & Hans. 
Symptoms: 

Control: This disease is controlled by soaking the planting sets in a solution of 3 pounds of ethoxy mercury chloride in 4 gallons of water. This not only prevents rhizome rot but also stimulate shoot formation in treated seed rhizomes. 

Many of the other diseases are treated with fungicides such as Bavistin and/or Dithane M-45 as well as copper and mercury-containing soil drenches (1).

Leaf Spot: caused by Phyllosticta zingiberi T. S. Ramakr (Gupta and Verma 1966). Also caused by Colletotrichum zingiberis Sundar.
Symptoms: Oval water soaked spots occur on leaves, later become papery, and may coalesce, causing extensive discolouration and desiccation. The leaves are attacked here, forming round to light-yellow spots which may coalesce to form large discoloured areas, often drying up in the centre forming a hole. The plants become stunted and the rhizome development is adversely affected. 

Control: It can be controlled by spraying 1% Bordeaux mixture.

Other diseases recorded include thread blight caused by Pellicularia filamentosa (Pat.) Rogers, by Chattopadhyay (1967) in India. Root rot (Nectriella zingiberi Stev. & Ati.), dry black rot (Rosellinia zingiberi Stev. & Ati.) and leaf spot (Coniothyrium zingiberi Stev. & Ati.) are found to occur in Philippines (Maistre 1964) (2).  

Major pests of ginger
Shoot borer: Dichocrocis punctiferalis Guen. attacks the ginger in India. The larvae bore into the shoots. 
Symptoms: wilting and then death (Yegna Narayan Aiyer 1944). 

Control: Periodical spraying of Endrin (0.02%) at intervals of one month, starting from the second month after planting, controls the pest effectively (Paulose 1973).

Scale insects: Aspidiotus, including Aspidiella hartii (Targ.) and A. destructor (Sign.) have been reported to attack ginger plants in several countries. 

Other pests
Mealy bug (Pseudococcus sp.), Scolytid beetle (Stephanoderes trinitatis Hopk.), minute translucent mite (Rhizoglyphus sp.), etc are some of the other pests. In India, stored ginger is attacked by stored-products pest. Paulose (1973) gave a list of the stored ginger pests. They are the common drug store beetle (Sitodrepa panicea (L.)) and the tobacco borer beetle (Lasioderma serricorne (F.) (2).

Nematodes
The root knot nematodes Meloidogyne incognita Kofoid & White, M. javanica Treub and to a lesser extent M. arenaria are the major root damaging pests of ginger in India (Sharma and Jain 1977), Fiji (Kirby 1977), Australia and Hawaii (Colbran 1968), China (Cheng and Tu 1979), S. Africa (Milne et al. 1979) and other ginger growing countries. Because of the prevalence of these nematodes even in virgin land, which possesses many alternate hosts, precautionary measures have to be taken prior to planting. Colbran and Davis 1969, Milne et al. 1979 found that as the nematodes can rapidly spread in planting material, all seed rhizomes should be treated in hot water (48˚C for 20 minutes) prior to planting. Later Colbran (1972, 1974) suggested that a combination of pre-planting soil fumigation with ethylene dibromide at 50L per hectare followed immediately by soil spraying with Nemacur at 4.5 kg per hectare. After planting the hot water-treated seed rhizomes, the soil was mulched to a depth of 5-7.5 cm with sawdust. This procedure has found to be most effective control method for the root knot nematodes.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
In the culinary field, ginger finds wide use as a spice for flavouring a multitude of food products. Fresh and dried powdered ginger is the usual form of ginger. Fresh ginger is used to a large extent in the preparation of Oriental dishes. Whole ginger is used in pickling and in particular for the preparation of crystallized or preserved ginger. This latter product is made by pickling clean fresh ginger rhizome in a boiling sugar solution. As a spice, ginger is one of the most versatile of all because it can be used in both sweet and savory foods. For example, it is used in the preparing cakes (gingerbread), biscuits or cookies (gingersnaps), non-alcoholic beverages (ginger ale and ginger beer) and alcoholic beverages (ginger vine) (1). In India, in meat and fish dishes it is indispensable to make it palatable and digestible. Buttermilk containing crushed fresh ginger, green chillies, salt and curry leaf is a delicious drink and appetizer of South India. ‘Puliyinji’ a curry prepared from finely chopped fresh ginger and ripe tamarind fruit extract as main ingredients is unique in taste and is indispensable in social and festival feasts of the Malabar Coast (5). In addition the ground spice can be used in a wide variety of products from steamed puddings and sauces to chutneys, salad dressings and fruit pies. Ginger oil and ginger extract or oleoresin has some limited use in the food processing industry outside of the major products listed above. In addition, ginger oil also has a limited use in the fragrance industry, especially in the Oriental male fragrances. Ginger is a masking agent for mouth odor in dentifrice and oral hygiene products. The essential oil is used in perfumery (1).  

Parts Used:  

Harvesting Practices: Always propagated by portions of the rhizome known as seed pieces or setts (because ginger rarely sets seed). Carefully preserved seed rhizomes are cut into small pieces, 2.5 – 5 cm long and weighing 28-56 g, each having at least one good bud or growing point. The seed rate in Kerala and other Southern states of India is 1200 to 1400 kg of seed rhizomes for 1 ha. In parts of North India, especially in higher altitudes in the Himalayan regions, a higher seed rate of 2500 to 3000 kg per ha is adopted, using bigger seed bits. Planting of large sized seed rhizomes gives significantly higher yields than small pieces (2).  

Preparation and Planting
As a general rule, prior to planting, the land is cleared of all vegetation by burning and then all rocks, stumps and debris are removed. The planting site is then broken up to a depth of 6-12 inches by ploughing or hand digging and raking until it has been broken into a light tilth after which the planting bed can be prepared. In Kerala the land is prepared in March with the receipt of the pre-monsoon summer showers. Once cleared, the land is graded into raised beds of flats 10-20 feet x 3 feet wide and 6 inches high at a spacing of 12-18 inches in between the beds. When the planting site is sloped, the beds are generally small in size and are formed so that they follow the natural contour of the slope (1).  

Shade Requirements
Intercropping can provide some partial shading. Shading is helpful in retardation of water loss and general cooling of the plant. 

Fertilizer Requirements
In Kerala, once the ginger is planted, the flat beds are covered with mulch of green leaves (Anon. 1965), particularly one which rots quickly. This mulch covering helps in sustaining the moisture content of the soil, decreasing rain water erosion, providing a source of nutrients and organic matter, retarding weed growth and also helps to keep the young plants warm during the cool monsoon rains. As the inorganic fertilizers seem to be confined to protein crops, in other areas of India organic fertilizers such as pressed groundnut cake, mustard seed meal and paddy chaff, straw and sugar cane trash are used (1).

Water Requirements
Other than a few areas such as Kerala and certain areas in Sri Lanka in which ginger is raised under rainfed conditions, most of the ginger farmers rely on rill or furrow type irrigation (1). For irrigated crop in India, irrigation is given at fortnightly intervals, usually during the period from the middle of September to the middle of November Paulose (1973) (2).  

Weed Control
In most places, weeds are removed either by hoeing them out or by meticulous hand weeding. Sometimes Paraquat, a non-residual contact herbicide are sprayed directly onto the weeds (1).  

Crop Rotation
Although ginger is a perennial, it is generally cultivated as an annual crop. This makes its cultivation rather time consuming if a good job is to be done. Ginger is either grown as a pure crop or an intercrop. Crops used for crop rotation in Kerala under rainfed conditions are Ginger, rice, gingely, fallow, samai, ragi, chillies, maize, groundnuts, sesame and vegetables (Kannan and Nair 1965) (1).

Harvesting is done 7-9 months after planting at a time when the leaves have started to dry out and wither. The later the harvest the more fibrous the ginger would be. As a consequence, ginger which is to be used for the preparation of preserved or crystallized ginger is harvested 4-5 months after planting. In Kerala, ginger is harvested during the months of November and December, approximately 8 months after planting. Initial harvest occurs during September when only a small quantity is dug up as this is the ginger which has low fibre content and is used for preserving. Main harvest takes place in December. Finally, the seed rhizomes are harvested in February ready for sowing in April (1).

Drying and Processing
Cleaned ginger is dried in the sun. Ginger cleaned and dried with no other processing is known commercially as “rough” or “unbleached” ginger. Another type of ginger known commercially as “bleached” ginger, which is popular in Southern India, is prepared by cleaning and peeling the rhizomes to remove tissues up to the cortical parenchyma. After the ginger is peeled, it is soaked in clean water for 24 hours and dipped into a solution of lime water (Ca (OH)2) and sun-dried on a coconut mat. This process of liming and drying is repeated several times over a period of days until the ginger assumes a white colour from a uniform coating of lime. A product which is locally known as “black” ginger (Ridley 1912) is occasionally found in some areas of India. It is prepared by presoaking freshly cleaned and de-rooted rhizomes in hot water for 10-15 minutes before drying. Black ginger is not very popular except to a limited extent as a local condiment (1).

Produce and Products: There are three primary products of the ginger rhizome: fresh (‘green’) ginger, preserved ginger in syrup or brine and the dried ginger spice. 

Preserved ginger is prepared from the immature rhizome, while more pungent and aromatic spice is prepared from harvesting and drying the mature rhizome. It is used both for domestic culinary purposes and in the manufacture of processed foods such as jams, marmalades, cakes and confectionery. Succulent ginger rhizomes with very little fibre and less pungency are preferred for this kind. The raw material for preserved ginger has to be harvested within 195 days of planting. Fresh rhizomes with low fibre content but rich in aroma, fat, pungency and protein are preferred for green ginger purposes. 

Dried ginger is prepared by washing and removing roots and overground parts of the plant; killing the rhizome; preparing the rhizome into the form required for drying and finally drying. Dried ginger is used directly as a spice and also for the preparation of its extractives, ginger oleoresin and ginger oil. It is employed on a wide range of foodstuffs, especially in bakery products and desserts. It is traditional pickling spice (1).

Grading
Commercially available gingers are known by the country, area of origin and type. For example, ginger may be coated or uncoated, peeled or unpeeled, splits or peelings. The grading of ginger in India is determined against a set of AGMARK specifications (Anon. 1965) in a similar fashion to all of the other major spices. In general, ginger is graded according to colour, plumpness or boldness, weight damage, hardness, moisture content, cleanliness and freedom from extraneous matter such as insects, mildew or sand (1).

Ginger Oil
The volatile oil of ginger, obtained as a pale-yellow to light-amber mobile liquid, which is generally prepared by the steam distillation of dried comminuted rhizomes, is an important raw material of food, cosmetic and pharmaceutical industries. The various constituents present in ginger oil (Guenther 1952) are: alpha-pinene, camphene, limonene, methyl heptenone, 1,8-cineole, linalool, nonanal, decanal, citral, borneol, geraniol, zingiberol, zingiberene, farnesene, ar. curcumene, bisabolene etc (1).

Other uses: Ginger is one of the reputed drugs of Ayurveda and is employed in indigenous systems of medicine. The dry ginger is acrid, pungent, hot, antirheumatic, cooling, diuretic and aphrodisiac, thermogenic, emollient, appetizer, laxative, stomachic, stimulant, rubefacient, anodyne, expectorant, anthelmintic and carminative. It is useful in dropsy, otalgia, cephalalgia, asthma, colic, diarrhoea, anorexia, vitiated condition of vata and kapha, dyspepsia, cardiopathy, pharyngopathy, cholera, nausea, vomiting, elephantiasis and inflammations. It is also much used in several domestic preparations (8). It is used externally as a rubefacient and counter-irritant. Ginger helps digestion, warms the stomach, clears sight and is profitable for old men; it heats the joints and is therefore useful against gout. It has an impressive record in treating all kinds of ailments. It is said to help poor circulation, and to cure flatulence and indigestion; taken as a drink for coughs, nausea and influenza. It is. It promotes digestive power, cleanses the throat and tongue, dispels cardiac disorders and cures vomiting, fever, anaemia, constipation and elephantiasis. In traditional medicine, ginger is extensively used for its specific action in rheumatism and inflammation of liver (Aiyer & Kolammal, 1964; Kurupp et al., 1979) (4).

Economic potential to the country

Largest ginger producing country is India (about 50% of the world’s total) and is the largest exporter. Countries importing the biggest amount are the United Kingdom, US and Saudi Arabia (2). Ginger occupies maximum area in Kerala, followed by Orissa, Meghalaya, Himachal Pradesh, West Bengal, Andhra Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka, Mizoram, Manipur, Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra, Bihar, Tripura, Gujarat, Uttar Pradesh, Nagaland, Rajasthan, Haryana, Assam, Jammu and Kashmir, Sikkim and Arunachal Pradesh (3). Kerala is the most important ginger growing state, accounting for over 75% of total production (1). Area: 83,940 ha. Production: 3,06,960 t (2000-01). Productivity: 3657 kg/ha.

Export Potential:
In India, Kerala accounts for about 90% of India’s exports of ginger. India now mainly exports dry ginger, fresh ginger and preserved ginger is produced for domestic demand (2).
Export: 6580 t valued at Rs. 22.95 crores (2000-01).
