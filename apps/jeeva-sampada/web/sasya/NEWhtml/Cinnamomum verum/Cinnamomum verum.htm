Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Evergreen open pollinated tropical tree. Dried inner bark of coppiced branches is the cinnamon of commerce.

Climate and soil:
Requires a warm and per-humid climate with a well-distributed annual rainfall of 2000-2500 mm, and average temperature of 27°C. Grows best at low altitudes, without shade. Fine sandy and lateritic gravelly soils rather than rocky and stony substrates are best in Sri Lanka and India, but in Seychelles and Madagascar more loamy soils are preferred. Grows in a wide variety of soils, best on pure laterite soils and high mountain soils above 1000 m MSL with 1 m depth and acidic to neutral pH. C. verum is susceptible to salinity. Waterlogged and marshy conditions result in a bitter product (1).

Availability :

Occur wild in south-west tropical evergreen and semi-evergreen forests India, Western Sri Lanka and the Tenasserim Hills of Myanmar (Burma). It is cultivated in south India, Sri Lanka, the Seychelles and in Madagascar (1).
Conservation/ Threat Status : Wild and cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

An evergreen tree up to 18 m tall; bole low branching, up to 60 cm in diameter; buttresses 60 cm tall, 70 cm deep, thin, light pinkish brown; bark about 10 mm thick, strongly aromatic; the bark on young shoots is smooth and pale brown, on mature branches and stems rough, dark brown, or brownish grey; oil cells are located in phloem; wood of mature trees vary from light brownish-grey to grey or yellowish brown, without markings, more or less lustrous and faintly scented. Leaves opposite, variable in form and size, strongly aromatic; petiole 1-2 cm long, grooved on upper surface; blade ovate to elliptical, 5-25 cm x 3-10 cm, conspicuously 3-venied or 5-veined, base rounded, apex acuminate, glabrous, coriaceous, shiny dark green (1).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:
Inflorescence consisting of lax axillary or terminal panicles up to 10 cm long or longer, peduncle creamy white, softly hairy, 5-7 cm long; flowers small, 3 mm in diameter, with foetid smell, pale yellow, subtended by small ovate hairy bract; perianth 8 mm long, silky hairy, with short companulate tube and 6 persistent tepals about 3 mm long; fertile stamens 9, in 3 whorls, with 2 small glands at the base of the stamens of the third whorl; a fourth inner whorl consists of 3 staminodes; filaments hairy; stout; anther 4- or 2-celled, with a single ovule, style short (1). Adapted for cross pollination.

Anther dehiscence:
Anthesis takes place on the 40th day after flower bud emergence. The stigma is receptive when the flower opens but the anthers do not dehisce on the first day. The flower closes on the day and open again the next day when the stigma is dry and the anther dehisce (2).

Fruit:
Fruit a 1-seeded berry, ellipsoid to ovoid, 1-2 cm long, surrounded by enlarged perianth at the base (1). Time taken from flowering to fruit ripening varies from 145 to 170 days. Fully ripened fruits are fleshy 1.5-2 cm long, purple coloured and contain one seed inside (2).

Breeding system and varietal improvement:
Propagation is by seed, vegetative cuttings, air-layering or tissue culture methods.
Cuttings: Softwood cuttings of cinnamon are treated with various rooting hormones (IBA, IAA NAA) at different concentrations for rooting. Sand and coir dust (1:1) is also ideal for rooting.
Air layering: Air layering is carried out on softwood and semi hardwood cuttings of cinnamon. IBA at different concentrations can be applied to etiolated and non-etiolated shoots for rooting.
Protocols for in vitro multiplication of cinnamon were standardized. Multiple shoots from mature and immature nodal segments could be induced. Somatic embryos were produced from immature seeds (4-5 months old) of cinnamon. Synthetic seeds were also developed by encapsulating somatic embryos and shoot buds ( 2).

Phenology:

Flowering in cinnamon occurs during January-April and September-December, with most of the trees flowering during January-February (2).

Pollinators:
By insects especially flies, beetles, honeybees or by wind.
Floral and seed biology:
Seed Biology
Only a single seed is present.

CULTIVATION ASPECT:
Seed germination:
Fresh seeds germinate in 20-25 days after sowing and continue up to 5-6 weeks. Usually 90% seeds germinate (1).

Seedling development:
Seedling growth is rapid initially with formation of a well developed taproot, followed by numerous spreading laterals (1).

Related taxa :
Cassia is the dried husk (bark) of a small, bushy evergreen tree. Though botanically has close relations, cassia is considered inferior to cinnamon. There are different varieties of cassia, obtained from different species of Cinnamomum and originating from different sources. These include 'Chinese', 'Indonesian', 'Saigon', 'Vietnamese' and 'Indian'. Though there is a generic likeness in the aromatic properties of the various species of cassia, they do exhibit differences in some respects (10).
Indonesian cassia or Padang cassia: Cinnamomum burmanni (Nees & Nees) Nees ex Blume.
Saigon cassia, Vietnamese cassia or Saigon cinnamon: C. loureirii Nees.
Indian cassia (Tejpat): C. tamala.
Chinese cassia or Chinese cinnamon; C. cassia endemic to China, South Vietnam, Indonesia etc. Occurs in South China, Myanmar, Laos and Vietnam. Among the oldest spices together with cinnamon. Introduced into India, Sri Lanka, Indonesia, South America and US. Barks are used in soaps and perfumes. Cassia leaf oil or cassia oil is obtained by steam distillation of twigs and leaves (3).
Distribution: In India, cassia is grown in the North-Eastern states and in the evergreen tracts along the high ranges of the Western Ghats.
Uses: Cassia bark is a popular ingredient in foods, beverages, perfumery, toileteries and cosmetics, while the oil of cassia finds extensive application in liquors and beauty products. Cassia is often used as a less costly substitute to true cinnamon (10).
Useful part: Bark and leaves
Chemistry: Cinnamaldehyde is the major constituent both in bark and leaf oils.
Vegetative propagation
Cuttings: Terminal cuttings of 2 year old trees were treated with different rooting hormones at various concentrations and combinations for rooting.
Air layering: Air layering of cassia can be done during July and November. Most suitable is during July. The layers developed good rooting system (2).

Insect pests
Cinnamon butterfly: Chilasa clytia (Lepidoptera, Pyralidae) is the most serious pest of cinnamon especially in younger plantations and nurseries.
Symptoms: The larvae feed on tender and slightly mature leaves and sometimes the entire plant is defoliated and only midrib of the leaves with portions of veins are left behind.
Control: The pest is controlled by spraying quinalphos (0.05%).
Leaf miner: Conopomorpha civica (Lepidoptera, Gracillaridae) generally infests tender leaves of young cinnamon plants in the nursery.
Symptoms: The larvae feed on the tissues between the upper and lower epidermis of tender leaves resulting in mines leading to 'blister' like patches and drying of infested portions.
Control : Spraying quinalphos (0.05%) during the emergence of new flushes is effective for the management of the pest (2).
Other related taxa: C. camphora (Camphor tree) and C. malabatrum (Vayana, 'Edava') (3).

Different species of cinnamon
There are about 150-250 species, common ones are C. cassia, C. camphora, C. sulphuratum, C. riparium, C. wightii, C. tamala, C. perrottetti, C. macrocarpum, C. glaucens (2). Many south east Asian Cinnamomum species are used as substitutes or adulterants for the true cinnamon and 3 main cassias of commerce. Some are minor spices, others have greater economic importance as timber: C. iners Reinw. ex Blume, C. javanicum Blume, C. mercadoi S. Vidal, C. mollissimum Hook.f., C. porrectum (Roxb.) Kosterm., C. scortechinii Gamble, C. sintoc Blume and C. subavenium. Substitutes and adulterants of local importance in southern Asia are:
· C. bejolghota (Buch.-Ham.) Sweet (syn. C. obtusifolium (Roxb.) C. Nees), occurs from northern India to Indo-China, southern China and Andaman Islands).
· C. impressinervium Meissner, occurring in the Sikkim Himalaya.
· C. tamala (Buch.-Ham.) T. Nees & Eberm., the Indian cassia, distributed in India, Bangladesh and Myanmar (Burma). In India it is cultivated for bark and leaves ('tejpat'). Tejpat leaves are extensively used as a spice in India, as well as in ayurvedic preparations (3).

Released varieties:
Nithyashree, Navashree, YCD-1, Konkan Tej, RRL(B) C-6 and Sugandhini are the varieties released. Sri Lanka has some local cultivars/types such as sweet or honey cinnamon, snake cinnamon, camphor cinnamon, astringent cinnamon, 'pen karudu', 'rassekarundu', 'pat karundu' etc.

Chromosome number:
2n=22

Selection of planting material:
Planting units: Seedlings/rooted cuttings/seeds are selected.

Planting:
For raising trees from seeds, fully ripened seeds from elite trees are collected during July-August. Avoid mother trees growing in the vicinity of C. malabatrum or other wild species, as there is chance of cross-pollination. Seeds removed from fruits are washed free of pulp and sown immediately in pits of 2.5 cm depth (as seeds quickly lose their viability) in raised bed consisting of rich sandy soil (20 cm) of 1 m width and convenient length having a fine tilth and be lightly shaded. Seeds can also be sown directly into polythene bags containing a mixture of sand, FYM and soil (2:1:1). Seeds are sown close together. Clumps of seedlings are transferred into bags after 4 months in nursery and transplanted to field in another 4-5 months (1).

For vegetative propagation (by cuttings, layering or division of old rootstocks), cuttings with 2-3 nodes are planted in poly bags and placed under polythene cover; they are ready for field planting in 12-18 months. Old rootstocks can be divided. For this, old plants are cut down to within 15 cm of the ground, and suitable parts of the rootstock planted out with adhering soil. Field spacing of 0.9-1.2 m x 0.9 -1.2 m are recommended for commercial plantations, 3 m x 3 m spacing also practiced.

Planting is done either as intercrop in homestead gardens or as monocrop in virgin soils during June-July in pits - 60 x 60 x 60 cm, with a spacing of 3 x 3 m and number of plants/pits being 5-10 (1).

Mist chamber method layering: Application of IBA (50 ppm) at the cut end induces profuse rooting in the layers, giving about 40% success. Separated layers may be immediately planted in polybags/plots filled with sand, soil and cowdung mixture. Layers should be hardened for about 6-12 months before planting in the main field.

Management after planting:
· Mulch of green leaves/dried leaves should be given to the beds.
· Pandal for providing shade to the beds.
· Frequent irrigation is needed to maintain adequate moisture level.
· Seedlings transplanted in polythene bags containing potting mixture after 3-4 months in nursery. Such seedlings are planted in main field after another six months.
· Slashing/removing weeds 2-3 times a year during the early years.
· 5-10 kg compost/FYM at the time of filling the pits, organic manures @ 20 kg/plant every year during June-July months and 20:18:25 g NPK per tree during the first year in two equal splits during May-June and September-October is required, in tree basin or in furrows (15-20 cm depth) opened around the tree. Fertilizer dose can be increased to 200:180:250 g NPK/year for grown up plants of 10 years and above.
· Stems are kept straight by pruning. Plants are coppiced for the first time after 2 years, stem being cut to within 10-15 cm from the ground and covered with the earth, allowing 4-6 shoots per stool to grow for a further 2 years before harvesting. Every side shoot will also be cut back so that plant will ultimately assume a bush shape. The number of shoots per stool normally increases to a maximum at 8 years and decline after 10-12 years. A cinnamon plantation remains profitable for 15-45 years (1).


MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
Shothole borer (Xylosandrus spp.) cause damage to stem and bark. Spray BHC (50% W.P.) on the plants.
Cinnamon butterfly (Chilasa clytia) (Lepidoptera, Pyralidae) is the most serious pest of cinnamon especially in younger plantations and nurseries.
Symptoms: Larvae feed on tender and slightly mature leaves and sometimes the entire plant is defoliated and only midrib of the leaves with portions of veins are left behind.
Control: Spray 0.05% Quinalphos.
Leaf miners (Acrocercops spp., Phyllocnistis chrysophthalma) generally infest tender leaves of young cinnamon plants in the nursery.
Symptoms: Larvae feed on the tissues between the upper and lower epidermis of tender leaves resulting in mines leading to 'blister' like patches and drying of infested portions.
Control: Spraying quinalphos (0.05%) during the emergence of new flushes is effective for the management of the pest.
Arboreal ants/white ants (Oecophylla smaragdina) cause occasional damage.
Control: Dust BHC (10%) or Aldrin (5%) on the basin and affected parts.
Mole crickets or agrotid larvae (Gryllotalpa spp.).
Gall and leaf mites (Eriophyes bois, E. doctersi, Typhlodromus spp.).
Leaf Webbers (Sorolopha archimedias).
Larvae of Popillia spp. attacks roots.
Storage pests of cinnamon quills are Lasioderma serricorne, Pyralis farinalis and Sitodrepa panicea (1).

Diseases:

Pink disease: Caused by Corticium salmonicolor.
Symptoms: pink encrustations on stem with death of small shoots.
Control: Spray 1% Bordeaux mixture, prune off the affected twigs, regulate excess shade.
Seedling blight: Caused by Diplodia spp.
Control: Spray 1% Bordeaux mixture.
Leaf spot: Caused by Gloeosporium sp./Cephaleuros sp.
Control: Spray 1% Bordeaux mixture or 0.3% Fytolan.
Stripe canker: Caused by Phytophthora cinnamomi, damage trunks and branches of young trees.
Symptoms: Vertical strips of dead bark, particularly near ground level.
Root rot: Black rot caused by Rosellinia spp., brown rot caused by Phellinus lamaensis, and white rot Caused by Fomes lignosus.
Anthracnose: Caused by Glomerella cingulata
Rust: Caused by Aecidium cinnamomi (1).

Nematode Induced Diseases
Root knot nematode (Meloidogyne incognita) also attacks cinnamon.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Both the ground and the whole form find use in domestic culinary purposes and for flavouring processed foods (bakery products, sauces, pickles, puddings, beverages, confectionery, some varieties of wine, rice varieties, fruit masalas, cakes and stews), in perfumes, pharmaceutical products and in incense. It is also used in pulao or biryani. Cinnamon helps in digestion. Bark oleoresin is used for flavouring food and in soft-drink industry. Bark oil is used in flavouring processed foods, beverages, dental and pharmaceutical preparations, much less in perfume industry as it has some skin-sensitizing properties. Cinnamon leaf oil is used in flavouring and perfumery and as a source of eugenol, which is used in the synthesis of vanillin, and for conversion into iso-eugenol, used to flavour confectionery products. It is largely used as fragrance component in soaps, detergents, cosmetic, candle manufacturing and alcoholic perfumery (1). It is also excellent as a mouth freshener. A decoction of cinnamon is good for coughs and colds. For acne and headaches, cinnamon powder paste can be applied (9).

Parts Used:

Dried inner bark.

Harvesting Practices:
Starts after 2-3 years of planting, usually during May-June or November-December in the case with seedlings. Usually coppicing is done in alternate years. Shoots with 1-2 m length, emerging after coppicing when attain finger thick (2-2.5 cm) and uniform brown colour are ideal for harvesting. Such shoots can be tested for their bark peeling flexibility by 'test cutting' to see if the bark gets lifted easily. If bark gets peeled off easily, the cutting can be commenced immediately. Shoots are cut between 6-8 am, clean close to the ground (0-15 cm above ground).
Starts 1-1½ years (when shoots are 2-3 m tall and 1.2-5.0 cm in diameter) after planting out in the field, compared with 3 years for seedlings. Micropropagation methods are also successful in producing large number of plantlets. Harvested during the wet season because the cambium will be active and the cortex can be easily separated from the wood. Shoots in the centre of the clump are cut down, while those on the outer are cut higher up to ensure that new buds sprout mainly on the outside of the clump. Harvest peaks are in May-June and October-November. First harvest is inferior in quality (thick bark), but improves on later harvests. Best quality cinnamon is obtained from thin bark from middle part of the shoots in centre of the stool. Leaves and twigs are cut off and used for mulching, or leaves retained for distillation (1).

Post Harvest Handling
Shoots are bundled after removing leaves and terminal shoots. Water must be sprinkled on these bundles and kept in shade. Peeling of the shoots is done after scrapping of the rough outer bark using a specially made knife which has a small and round end with a projection on one side to facilitate ripping of the bark. After scrapping outer bark, scrapped surface is polished with a small brass rod. Two horizontal cuts are made 30 cm apart and two longitudinal slits on opposite sides of the shoot. Working the knife between the bark and wood, the bark is ripped quickly. Shoots cut in the morning are peeled in the same day. Alternatively, outer and inner barks are separated from the wood together. Strips are packed together, wrapped and left overnight for slight fermentation, facilitating the subsequent scarping off of the outer bark (epidermis, cork and green cortex). Curled pieces are assembled into compound quills of 1 m length by joining the best and longest quills on outside and smaller pieces inside longer ones. They are dried in shade and then in sun for 2-3 days, until they are yellowish brown, sometimes bleached by sulphur treatment.
Yield: 168-224 kg bark/ha plus, 63 kg chips and 2.5 tons leaves from a ten year old plantation (1).
Grading: Criteria: Length, thinness, texture and flavour. Best quality quill will be very thin, soft, have less fibre content and fine.
Grades: Fine (continental): 'C-00000' (best grade) 'C-0' (coarsest quality) Mexican: 'M-00000' (best grade) 'M-0' (coarse grade) Humburg ordinary: H1, H2, H3 (H1 is the best and H3 last grade.
Specification: The 'fine' grades range from 10 mm in diameter or less in C-00000 to 19 mm in C-0 grade. Hamburg grades ranges from 23 mm to 82 mm. Best quality cinnamon will have plywood colour.
Quillings: Broken lengths and fragments of quills of all grades bulked and packed in bales of 100 lb (45 kg).
Featherings: Inner barks of twigs and twisted shoots, and packed in bales of 100 lb.
Chips: Shaved off barks of different size, barks obtained from small twigs by beating, hammerings etc. scraps contain woody material and often contaminated with sand, stones, other debris etc. Chips are compressed into bales of 2 or 3 CWT (100 or 150 kg).
Cinnamon powder: Powdered barks graded as '0'.

Produce and Products:

Bark oil: pale yellow or brownish yellow coloured oil, obtained by steam or hydro-distillation with cohobation, or solvent extraction of the distillate (gives finest quality of oil). Bark contains 0.5-2% oil. Bark oil contain mainly cinnamicaldelyde.
Leaf oil: Yellow to brownish yellow in colour, obtained from leaves stripped from shoots, together with small leafy twigs and stems left in the field for 3-4 days. Leaves contain 0.5-0.7% oil and is rich in eugenol.
Root oil: Roots not that much economical, is obtained from cleaned, trimmed and peeled roots that are then distilled. On distillation yield on oil (1.2-2.8%), rich in camphor.
Cassia oil: Obtained from distillation of cassia cinnamon. Both leaf oil and bark oil of cassia (Chinese cassia) are rich in cinnamaldehyde.
Cinnamon oleoresin: A deep reddish or greenish brown viscous liquid, obtained by solvent extraction of barks/leaves of cinnamon. Oleoresin yield varies from 2.5 to 12% depending on the solvent used.
Various forms and qualities are known: unscraped bark, scraped bark, compound quills, simple quills, quillings (broken pieces of quills), featherings (bark of twigs and twisted shoots) and chips (trimmings, shavings) (1).

Medicinal uses
Bark is acrid, bitter, sweet, aromatic, astringent, aphrodisiac, deodorant, stimulant, alexeteric, expectorant, febrifuge, diuretic and carminative. Useful in bronchitis, asthma, cephalalgia, odontalgia, cardiac diseases, diarrhoea, uropathy, nausea and vomiting, flatulence, fever, halitosis and restoring normal skin colour on the face. Cinnamon oil is stomachic, carminative, emmenagogue and styptic, and is useful in anorexia, inflammations, stomachalgia, vitiated conditions of vata, odontalgia, vomiting and tubercular ulcers (4). Cinnamaldehyde is anesthetic, antipyretic, hypotensive, hypothermic and sedative. Eugenol is antiseptic. Leaf oil has antioxidant and antibacterial properties. Chinese took cinnamon as a remedy for excessive gases in the stomach, to normalize the temperature of liver. Dioscorides prescribed cinnamon bark for colds. Ground cinnamon is added to hot milk, sometimes strengthened with whisky or sweetened with honey or stirred into a mixture of lemon juice, honey & hot water, to lessen the miserable effects of common cold. In modern medicine, cinnamon is used combined with other ingredients in medicines to relieve flatulence and arrest vomiting; for diarrhoea & internal haemorrhage (7). It is sometimes prescribed in gastrodynia, flatulent colic and gastric debility. In Europe, bark oil is used in teas and other galenicals for its antibacterial, fungistatic properties, in loss of appetite and dyspeptic disturbances (1).

Chemistry:
Cinnamon oleoresin contains steam volatile oil (16-65%), fixed oil and other components dependent on the solvent. Bark yields 10-12% oleoresin with ethanol as solvent, compared with 2.5-4.3% with benzene. Same tree produces three different kinds of essential oils, characterised by eugenol in leaves, cinnamaldehyde in stem bark and camphor in root bark.

Essential oil content of bark is 0.5-2.5%. Cinnamon bark oil contains mainly cinnamaldehyde (50-75%), eugenol (5-18%), cinnamyl acetate, linalool, 1,8-cineole, ß-caryophellene and benzyl benzoate. Oil form bark chips is richer in eugenol (30-38%), and poor in cinnamaldehyde (44%). Cinnamon leaf contains 0.7-1.2% essential oil. Composition is more or like that of clove oil. Apart from main component eugenol (70-95%) (ISO: 75-85% phenols) other components include cinnamaldehyde, benzyl benzoate, linalool and ß-caryophellene. Root bark contains 1-2.8% of a colourless to pale yellowish brown oil, having no commercial importance. Major component is camphor, which crystallizes on standing.

Mositure - 9.9%, protein - 4.6%, fat - 2.2%, fibre - 20.2%, carbohydrate - 59.5%, calcium - 1.6%, phosphorous - 0.05%, iron - 0.004%, sodium - 0.01%, potassium - 0.04%, oil - 0.3-2.8%.
Dried inner bark contains, steam-volatile oil, fixed oil, tannin, resin, proteins, cellulose, pentosans, mucilage, starch, calcium oxalate and minerals. Organoleptic properties are determined by steam-volatile oil and the trace amounts of coumarin (non-steam volatile). In US, regulatory status 'generally regarded as safe' (GRAS) has been accorded to cinnamon (GRAS 2289), cinnamon bark oil (GRAS 2290/2291) and cinnamon leaf oil (GRAS 2292). Seeds are used for candle making. Timber is suitable only as a low grade board wood (1).

Cinnamon leaf oil (form India) contains: 84.5% eugenol, 3.7% linalool, 2.5% cinnamyl acetate, 2.3% ß-caryophyllene, 1.5% (E)-cinnamic aldehyde, 1.0% a-phellandrene, 0.5% a-pinene, 0.5% a-humulene, 0.4% 1,8-cineole, 0.3% para-cymene, 0.3% ß-elemene, 0.2% methyl chavicol, 0.2% safrole, 0.2% ß-pinene, 0.2% camphene, 0.1% a-terpineol, 0.1% eugenyl acetate, 0.1% myrcene, 0.1% (E,E)-farnesol, 0.1% borneol, 0.1% terpinolene, 0.1% caryophyllene oxide, 0.1% camphor, 0.1% ?-cadinene, 0.1% a-thujene, 0.1% d-3-carene, 0.1% (E)-ß-ocimene, 0.1% ?-terpinene, trace terpinen-4-ol, trace a-terpinene, trace sabinene, trace ß-phellandrene, trace (Z)-ß-ocimene, trace nerol, trace citronellal, trace ß-selinene, trace (Z,E)-a-farnesene, trace cuminaldehyde, trace trans-linalool oxide (5) (furanoid), trace cis-linalool oxide (5) (furanoid), trace 2-phenylethanol, trace 2-phenylethyl benzoate, trace 2-phenylethyl propionate, trace (E)-mathyl isoeugenol, trace (Z)-cinnamic aldehyde, trace (E)-cinnamic alcohol (Source: Maallavarapu et al., 1995) (11).

Economic potential to the country

Area under cultivation is 690 ha; 40 tons in Himachal Pradesh. There are two cinnamon estates of about 240 ha at Ancharakandi and Kannur District, Kerala. Most of the cinnamon cultivation is confined to Kerala, Tamil Nadu and Karnataka (2). It is also grown to a limited extend in Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, West Bengal and North East India.

Export potential:

Export of cassia is 3449 t (Source: Spices Statistics, Spices Board, 1996-97).
Export of tejpat is 282 t (Source: Spices Statistics, Spices Board, 1996-97).
