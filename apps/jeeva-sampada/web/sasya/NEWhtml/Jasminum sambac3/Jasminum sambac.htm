DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:
 A scandent or sub-erect shrub.  Branchlets pubescent; leaves opposite or sometimes ternate, variable in shape, usually ovate or elliptic, glabrous or nearly so, with prominent lateral nerves; flowers white, very fragrant, solitary, or in 3-flowered terminal cymes; calyx teeth 5-9 linear-subulate; corolla lobes narrowly oblong, acute or obtuse, orbicular in cultivation; carpets black when ripe.

Chemistry:  

Determination of 3-hexenyl acetate (1.09%), cis-3-hexenol (1.49%), dipentene oxide (1.09%), linalool (14.33%), methyl benzoate (2.96%), benzyl acetate (11.04%), caryophyllene (13.24%), benzyl alcohol (10.12%), cadinene (4.67%), cis-3-hexenyl benzoate (16.51%), methyl anthranilate (0.62%), octadecene (6.54%), methyl oleate (2.02%) and methyl octadec-9-ynoate (0.62%) in essential oil. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984)a.
Linalool, cis-3-hexen-1-yl acetate, benzyl acetate, methyl benzoate, methyl salicylate, myrcene, a-fenchene, limonene, g-3-carene, cis-linalool oxide and trans-3-hexenyl butyrate isolated from top nodes. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984)b.
Friedelin, lupeol, betulin, a-amyrin and betulinic, ursolic and oleanolic acids isolated from leaves. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989)a.
Isolation of 9'-deoxyjasminigenin, jasminin and 8,9-dihydrojasminin from flowers. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989)b.
Linalool, cis-caryophyllene, indole, benzyl acetate, cis-3-hexenyl benzoate and methyl anthranilate identified in flower oil by GC-MS. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989)c.
Phenylmethanol, linalool, a-terpineol, cis-3-hexen-1-ol, methylphenyl acetate and methyl benzoate also identified in volatile oil of flowers. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989)d.
A new seco-iridoid glucoside - sambacolignoside (fig. 1) isolated along with oleoside 11-methyl ester and its structure determined. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989)e.
Linalyl-b-D-glucoside and its 6'-O-malonate isolated from flower buds. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994).
Jasminum sambac were extracted with supercritical CO2 at different conditions of pressure, temperature and contact time to estimate the yield and quality. The yield was 0.240% at better technol. Parameter of pressure range 12-15 NPa, temp range 308-323K and contact time 1-1.5h. (He et al, 1998). C.A. 1998, 129, 92472p.
Cosmetic lotions containing dipotassium glycyrrhetinate and plant extracts of Jasminum sambac with other plants. (Harada, 1997). C.A. 1997, 126, 190736k.
A comparison study of constituents of the absolutes extracted from flower of Jasminum sambac by supercritical CO2 and petroleum ether was made using GC and GC-MS. More than 100 constituents were detected, and among which 35 main compounds were identified. The primary chemical constituents of Jasmine absolute extracted by CO--2 are benzyl alcohol, 5.11%, linalool 9.43%, phenylmethyl acetate 7.91%, 6-ethenyl-tetrahydro-2,2,6-trimethyl-2H-pyran-3-ol 0.41%, cis-3-hexenl-benzoate 28.77% and 1-tetracosanol 10.14%. (He et al, 1999). C.A. 2000, 132, 90703c.
New oligomeric iridoidal glycosides, molihuasides A-E were isolated from the flowers of Jasminum sambac. Their structures were determined by spectral and chemical evidence. Among them, molihuasides A (fig. 2a) and C-E (fig. 2c-2d) are new dimeric iridoidal glycosides and molihuaside B (fig. 2b) is a new trimeric iridoidal glycoside. (Ying et al, 1995).
The odorants in Chinese jasmine green tea scented with jasmine flowers (Jasminum sambac) were separated from the infusion by adsorption to Porapak Q resin. Among the 66 compounds identified by GC and GC/MS, linalool (floral), methyl anthranilate (grape-like), 4-hexanolide (sweet), 4-nonanolide (sweet), (E)-2-hexenyl hexanoate (green), and 4-hydroxy-2,5-dimethyl-3(2H)-furanone (sweet) were extracted as potent odorants by an aroma extract dilution analysis and sensory analysis. The enantiomeric ratios of linalool in jasmine tea and Jasminum sambac were determined by a chiral analysis for the first time in this study: 81.6% ee and 100% ee for the (R)-(-)-configuration, respectively. The jasmine tea flavor could be closely duplicated by a model mixture containing these six compounds on the basis of a sensory analysis. The omission of methyl anthranilate and the replacement of (R)-(-)-linalool by (S)-(+)-linalool led to great changes in the odor of the model. These two compounds were determined to be the key odorants of the jasmine tea flavor. (Ito et al, 2002).
Benzyl 6-O-beta-D-xylopyranosyl-beta-D-glucopyranoside (beta-primeveroside), 2-phenylethyl beta-primeveroside, and 2-phenylethyl 6-O-alpha-L-rhamnopyranosyl-beta-D-glucopyranoside (beta-rutinoside) were isolated as aroma precursors of benzyl alcohol and 2-phenylethanol from flower buds of Jasminum sambac Ait. The isolation was guided by an enzymatic hydrolysis and GC and GC-MS analyses. (Inagaki et al, 1995).

NBPGR: Leaves contain jasminin, quercitrin, iso-quercitrin, rutin, quercitrin-3- dirhamnoglycoside, kaempferol –3- rhamnoglycoside, mannitol, a-amyrin, b-sitosterol and aniridoid, glycoside named sambacin.  The absolute contains several pyridine and nicotinate derivatives including methyl-5-ethyl-4-methyl nicotinate as major one.

Pharmacology:  

The efficacy of jasmine flowers (Jasminum sambac) applied to the breasts to suppress puerperal lactation was compared with that of Bromocriptine. Effectiveness of both regimens was monitored by serum prolactin levels, clinical evaluation of the degree of breast engorgement and milk production and the analgesic intake. While both bromocriptine and jasmine flowers brought about a significant reduction in serum prolactin, the decrease was significantly greater with bromocriptine. However, clinical parameters such as breast engorgement, milk production and analgesic intake showed the 2 modes of therapy to be equally effective. The failure rates of the 2 regimens to suppress lactation were similar; however, rebound lactation occurred in a small proportion of women treated with bromocriptine. Jasmine flowers seem to be an effective and inexpensive method of suppressing puerperal lactation and can be used as an alternative in situations where cost and nonavailability restrict the use of bromocriptine. (Shrivastav et al, 1988)
