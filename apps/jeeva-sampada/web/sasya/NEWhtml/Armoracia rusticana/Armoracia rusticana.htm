Other Biological Details

HABIT HABITAT                                                                                                         

A subtropical perennial herbaceous plant native to Europe, cultivated extensively in Central Europe and America for its swollen roots. Grown in many parts in North India and also in the hill stations of South India. Cold weather improves the flavour quality. The word "horse" (as applied in "horseradish") is believed to denote large size and coarseness. "Radish" comes from the Latin radix meaning root (3).
Climate and soil
Suited for temperate climates and grows best in full sun to partial shade in a moist, rich, heavy soil with pH 6.8. Well-drained moisture-retentive loam is preferred. Sandy soils and shallow clays are not suited. Can be cultivated in the tropics with an altitude above 1000 m. survives severe winter (1).

Availability :

Believed to originate from south-eastern Europe and western Asia. Now found naturalized as an escape from cultivation in many temperate regions of the world. It is also cultivated in mountainous areas like in Indonesia, Malaysia and Philippines (1).

Conservation/ Threat Status : Wild and cultivated.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Erect, perennial, glabrous herb, up to 1.5 m tall, often grown as an annual. Primary root (tap root) thick, pungently aromatic when bruised, often-several headed, cylindrical to conical, up to 50 cm long, woody in wild, fleshy in cultivated plants, yellowish white, developing long secondary roots and subterranean runners. Stem single or multiple straight, branched in upper part, ribbed, hollow. Leaves smooth or crinkled; lower leaves initially rosette, long-petioled, ovate-oblong from an acute or cordate base, 30-100 cm long, irregularly crenate-lanceolate, coarsely serrate; cauline leaves spirally arranged, lower ones petioled, often pinnatilobed-pinnatipartite, higher ones gradually becoming subsessile, lanceolate and crenate-serrate or subentire (1).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Propagated by seeds and root cuttings. Inflorescence a raceme, 20-40 cm long, many flowered, racemes combined into a terminal panicle; pedicel erecto-patent, in flower 5-10 mm long, in fruit up to2 cm long; sepals 4, broadly ovate, 3 mm long; petals 4, broadly obovate, 5-7 mm long, white; stamens 6, tetradynamous; disk glands united into a ring; pistil with glabrous ovary, short style and slightly lobed stigma (1). Sterility is high, seeds rarely reach maturity. 

Fruit
Fruit a silique, globose to broadly obovoid, 4-6 mm long, abruptly contracted into the style, 2-valved.

Breeding system and varietal improvement:

Phenology:
Flowers in late spring – summer (1).

Pollinators: Mainly insects

Floral and seed biology: Seeds black, 1-6 per fruit.


CULTIVATION ASPECT:
Local cultivars 
Two types of horse-radish are under cultivation – the common type and the Bohemian. Former has broad, crinkled leaves and good quality. This is the most commonly cultivated type. Bohemian has narrow smooth leaves, and its quality is poor.

Chromosome number:
2n=32

Selection of planting material:
Though propagated by seeds, normally done using root cuttings. One year old secondary roots are selected from 2 year old primary roots for planting the following year.

Planting:
Cuttings (12 mm diameter and 35 cm long) are placed obliquely in shallow furrows, filled with compost and cattle manure and covered with up to10 cm of soil, and is firmed by rolling. Spacing is 45-60 cm within the row and 90-100 cm between the rows. Can be propagated in vitro by leaf pieces also (1). To obtain juiciest and most succulent roots, use root cuttings about 1.2 cm wide and 20 cm long and plant in February – March, making sure that each has a bud. Remove the side roots and plant in holes 30 cm deep, 45 cm apart. Deep moist soils are preferable and at any site. After a year or two later, when digging up the plants, remove piece of root, otherwise it will sprout again (2). 

Management after planting:
After the cutting has rooted, the crown portion is lifted slightly to break off new secondary roots on upper portion. High levels of fertilizer are not needed. 500 kg N, 630 kg P2O5, 90 kg K2O, 260 kg Ca and 100 kg Mg are needed for one season, which can be provided by 60 t/ha of farmyard manure, supplemented by 200 kg/ha N as a split dressing. Weed control can be mechanical or by weedicides such as Xyfluorfen, Oryzalin or Glyphoste at 4.5 kg/ha applied after 6-8 weeks after dehiscing (1).  


MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
Insects attacking horseradish are: onion thrips (Thrips tabaci), beet leafhopper (Circulifer tenellus), green peach aphid (Myzus persicae), mealy plum aphid (Hyaleptorus arundinis), horseradish flea beetle (Phylletreta armoracies), diamondback moth (Plutella maculipennis) and southern cabbage worm (Pieris protodice). Spraying insecticides damages the pests (seen in US) (1).

Diseases:
White rust, turnip mosaic virus and brittle root are the major ones in US. 
White rust – Caused by Albugo candida, attacks leaf and prevents root development.
Mottling and chlorotic ring – Caused by turnip mosaic virus.
Brittle rot – Causative not known, plants become chlorotic, aerial part collapses, roots become turgid (1).

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
The hot tasting fleshy roots have probably been used for at least 3000 years as a food flavouring (2). Has sharp mustard like taste and is used as a condiment with fish, beef, sausages, poached chicken, egg salad, potato salad and beets. Roots are dried, ground and mixed with vinegar, milk and seasonings to make horseradish sauce, used commonly as a condiment with beef. Young leaves and seedlings can be eaten as a salad. Root extract have been approved for food use by the Food and Drugs Administration (FDA) of US. It is a versatile spice and also has important medicinal uses. It has high content of vitamin C (1). Raw or dried root used for sauce with meat, especially beef; in vinegar, powdered form in meat, sausages, ham, vegetables and fish dishes, adds spice to egg dishes (2). It has high content of vitamin C, and is used by seasoning industry. 

Parts Used: Swollen taproot.


Harvesting Practices: Crop comes to harvest after one year. Swollen roots are dug out, aerial leafy portion is cut off, and stored in dark cool place. Secondary root should not be broken off. In some areas where they are grown as perennial, crop is harvested in every 2 years (1).

Post Harvest Handling
Roots develop green colour if exposed to sunlight, and this is to be avoided. Cuttings for next year are buried in outdoor pits or kept in cool cellars. Roots are cleaned of excess dirt and either graded and sold or stored under cool moist conditions without washing or chemical treatment (in polythene bags at 2°C) (1).  

Produce and Products: Horseradish and Horseradish oil - a pale yellow volatile oil having an intense, sharp odour, horseradish sauce.


Chemistry:
Horseradish possesses bitter taste and when cut gives out a pungent odour. This flavour is due to the presence of isothiocyanates and the peculiar taste is due to volatile sulphur containing compounds. Important ones are sinigrin and nasturition glycosides. They get hydrolysed quickly by the enzymes myrosenase or thio-glucosidase present in the root. Roots yield horseradish oil on steam distillation and this is used in canning and in salad preparation. Oil contains allyl-isothiocyanate and 2-phenyl isothiocyanate and the aroma is finally decided by the relative proportion of these compounds. Flavour components are methyl, ethyl, isopropyl, 2-butyl and 4-pentyl isothiocyanates. Dried horseradish root upon solvent extraction gives a very viscous resenoid having amber colour and pungent odour. This is used extensively in seasoning. Peroxidase obtained from roots is an important product used in analytical biochemistry, medicine and immunology for antibody and antigen labeling. 

Per 100 g edible portion of roots contains approx.: water – 65 g, protein – 3.9 g, fat – 0.2 g, carbohydrates – 27 g, fibre – 2.2 g, Ca – 117 mg, ascorbic acid – 100 mg, riboflavin – 0.08 mg, thiamine – 0.20 mg, Fe – 2.7 mg and niacin – 0.5 mg. At least 17 volatile compounds have been identified including several isothiocyanate and thiocyanate derivatives. High quality horseradish sauce is white and pungent but in storage, pungency gets lost and become discoloured and develops an earthy odour and taste. Shelf life can be increased by adding fresh whipped dairy cream, storing the sauce at -18°C and by adding vinegar (1).  

Horseradish root extract contains: 50.0% allyl isothiocyanate, 44.0% 2-phenylethyl isothiocyanate, 2.3% 4-pentenyl isothiocyanate, 2.1% allyl thiocyanate, 1.5% 2-butyl isothiocyanate, trace 3-butenyl isothiocyanate, trace 3-(methylthio)propyl isothiocyanate, (Source: Gilbert & Nursten, 1972) (4).

Other uses: Medicinal uses
Like mustard, acts as a stimulant to stomach and shows antibiotic activity against bacteria. It is used as a stimulant, diuretic, and as an antibiotic. It is effective against both gram positive and gram-negative bacteria. It also shows antifungal, cytotoxic and carcinostatic actions. Horseradish is a good remedy for urinary troubles. As a poultice it is effective against wounds, old sores, swelling and tumors. Fresh roots, use with care on boils and for bronchitis, coughs and as stimulant, but large quantities can be harmful both externally and internally (2). Early Greeks used it as a rub for low back pain and an aphrodisiac. Jews still use it during Passover seders as one of the bitter herbs. Some used horseradish syrup as an expectorant cough medicine; others were convinced it cured everything from rheumatism to tuberculosis (3).

Miscellaneous Information

 Root barks of horseradish tree (Moringa oleifera Lamk.), having the same pungent taste (adulterate) is used as condiment and garnish (1).
In the American South, horseradish was rubbed on the forehead to relieve headaches (Some folks still swear by it). 
Horseradish is added to some pickles to add firmness and "nip". 
Horseradish has only 2 calories a teaspoon, is low in sodium and provides dietary fiber.
Researchers at M.I.T. claim that the enzyme "horseradish peroxidase" removes a number of pollutants from waste water (3).
