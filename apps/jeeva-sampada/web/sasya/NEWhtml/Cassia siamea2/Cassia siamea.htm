Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:
A small or medium sized evergreen tree, 6-8m in height, some times reaching 20 m, and a girth of 2.8 m. Grows commonly in the forests of South Madras, East Pakistan and Burma and from Punjab to Arunachal Pradesh and Tripura, extending into Peninsular India. Largely planted all over India, up to an altitude of 1220m and in the Andaman Islands.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Bark gray, nearly smooth with slight features; leaves 12-30 cm long, leaflets 6-14 pairs, elliptic-oblong; flowers bright yellow, in up to 60 cm long curved at the posterior end, flattened, soft, ribbon like when young, purple or brown when ripe, 15-30 cm x 1-3 cm, many seeded, seeds shining brown.

Phenology :
The new leaves are produced during Feb-March. The flowers appear from June to January. The tree begins to bear pods from the age of 2 or 3 years and good seeds are produced every year. In South India, the seeds are available during March-April.

Cultivation:
The tree grows well on both flat terrain and half-slopes; it is found in the dry mixed deciduous forests of Tamil Nadu. It has been planted extensively in recent years both in forests and in avenues, and has become naturalized. As a shade tree it is grown in the tea estates and as a avenue tree in West Bengal and Assam. It thrives under a variety of climatic conditions within the tropics, but cannot withstand frost, though it is fairly resistant to drought. It is susceptible to fire. In its natural habitat, the absolute maximum shade-temperature range between 38 and 49. The plant needs a minimum of 1000mm of rainfall for good growth. It comes up almost on any kind of soils except laterite, provided it is moist, but well drained. Young plants need good drainage.

Chemistry
Analysis of the wood gave: cellulose-59.1; lignin-28.3; pentosans-16.1; ash-1.2%. Cassiamin, chrysophanol and 2,3,2',3'-tetra-hydroxy stilbene are reporetd to be present in the heart wood. The leaves yield sennosides (0.07%). They also contain siamine, siaminine A (C11H11O3 N.HCl, m p 215'), Siaminine B (C9H7O3 N.HCl, m p 230'), siaminine C (m p 240'), cassiamin A, physcion, chrysophanol, rhein, p-coumeric acid, apigenin, its 7-0-galactoside (thalicitiin), 2-methyl-5-acetonyl-7-hydroxychromone (Cassiachromone), kaempferol glycoside, beta-sitosteroland barakol. The purgative properties of the plant are attributed to various anthraquinones present in it. The leaves also contain tannin. Flowers contain 5-acetonyl-7-hydroxy-2- methylchromone, cassiadinine, (+)-6-hydroxymellein, cycloart-23-ene-3, 25-diol, fredelin, betulin, sigmasterol, 24-methyl-cholesterol. Bark contains siameanin, soameadin, cassiamin A, B and C, lupeol, lupenone, betulin, betulic acid, betulinic acid, oleonic acid, chrysophanol, rhein, physcion, beta-sitosterol and sucrose.

Other Uses

  1. Leaves can also be used for green manure and compost.

2.      Wood is used for bridges, helves, walking sticks, millets, pit props and mine props. It is good for inlay and the best class cabinetwork. The heartwood is used for tanning and dying. It is an excellent fuel and makes good charcoal. Mechanical pulp suitable for pulp boards can be produced from the wood. Sulphate pulp suitable for a fair quality of paper for writing and printing can also be produced from the wood. The leaves are eaten by cattle, sheep and goats and are classed as a medium to good fodder. The blackbuck and barking dears have been known to browse on it and leaves are purgative. The flowers sometimes eaten in curries. Bark contains tannins (22.84%) and can be utilized for tanning as a substitute wattle bark. The roots are employed in intestinal worms and for preventing convulsions in children.

3.      Used chiefly as a fuel, but in Burma exploited for making walking sticks, mallets, shelves, employed in Ceylon for locomotives and as a fuel. It can be tried for joinery work, inlay work and furniture.
