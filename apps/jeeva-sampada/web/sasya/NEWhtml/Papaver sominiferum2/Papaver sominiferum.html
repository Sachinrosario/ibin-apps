HABIT & HABITAT
An erect, rarely branched, usually glaucous annual 60-120 cm . Native of Asia Minor and indigenous to Mediterranean coastal region; cultivated in Rajasthan, Uttar pradesh and Madhya pradesh.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:
 An erect, rarely branched, usually glaucous annual 60-120 cm. high.  Leaves ovate-oblong or linear oblong, amplexicaule, lobed, dentate or serrate; flowers large, usually bluish white with a purple base or white purple or variegated; capsules large 2.5 cm diameter Globose, stalked, seeds white or black, reniform.

Economic Potential and Uses
UTILIZATION:
Uses:
 Latex from capsule – analgesic, aphrodisiac, astringent, narcotic, sedative, expectorant, antispasmodic, hypnotic, sudorific; seed – demulcent, emollient, purgative, in catarrh of the bladder; seed oil and latex from capsule – anti diarrhoeal; capsule – antiinflammatory, antiodontalgic, in bruises, sprains painful conjunctivitis; leaves and latex from capsule anodyne.


Chemistry:
The latex from the capsules is opium in raw form.  It contains more than 30 alkaloids, the chief being thebaine, condeine and morphine (biosynthesized in the plant in this order, strong bases and highly toxic) narcotine, narceine and papaverine (feeble bases, less toxic).  Codamine, condeinone, coptisine, the basic benzylisoquinoline unit – reticuline, the biogenetically important dienone intermediate – salutaridine, canadine, magnoflorine protopine, coreximine, corytuberine, cryptopine, N-methyl–14–o demethylepiporphyroxine, glaudine, gnoscopine, hydrocotarnine, 10-hydroxycodeine, papaveralidine, pseudomorphine, scoulerine, stepholidine and a host of others.  Recently, a new class of dimeric opium alkaloids named somniferine and O-methylsomniferine have been isolated.

·        A reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatographic procedure was developed for the qualitative and quantitative analysis of the main alkaloids (morphine, codeine and thebaine), together with oripavine, in poppy straw. The column consisted of radial compression cartridges packed with C18 as the stationary phase. The mobile phase-was a gradient of acetonitrile and an aqueous solution of octanesulfonic acid sodium salt. (Gomez-Serranillos et al, 1995). MAPA 9505-3393.

·        In addition to codeine and morphine, three more compounds; narcotine (noscapine), papaverine and thebaine were found in Indian and Netherlands poppy seeds (Papaver somniferum). The compounds were detected by GC/MS technique and the identities were confirmed by comparing retention times and ion ratios with the known references. The concentrations of codeine, morphine, thebaine, papaverine and narcotine in urine are of interest and were found to be 4, 4, 6, 0.4 and 4 ng/mL, respectively. The detection of urinary narcotine, papaverine or thebaine may be utilized to differentiate poppy seed consumption from illicit codeine, morphine or heroine use. (Paul et al, 1996). C.A. 1997, 126, 208285z.

·        In addition to codeine and morphine, three more compounds: narcotine (noscapine), papaverine, and thebaine were found in Indian and Netherlands poppy seeds (Papaver somniferum L). The compounds were detected by a GC/MS technique and the identities were confirmed by comparing retention times and ion ratios with the known references. The concentrations of codeine, morphine, thebaine, papaverine, and narcotine were 44, 167, 41, 67, and 230 micrograms/g in Indian poppy seeds, and were 1.8, 39, 1.0, 0.17, 0.84 micrograms/g in Netherlands poppy seeds, respectively. Because these compounds may be urinary products after poppy seed consumption, the lowest detectable concentrations of codeine, morphine, thebaine, papaverine, and narcotine in urine are of interest and were found to be 4, 4, 5, 0.4, and 4ng/ml, respectively. The detection of urinary narcotine, papaverine, or thebaine may be utilized to differentiate poppy seed consumption from illicit codeine, morphine, or heroin use. (Paul et al., 1996).

·        Endogenous scopolin and its aglycon scopoletin were tested in Papaver somniferum cell suspension by HPLC. The results indicate that the levels of these compounds varied greatly from one plant cell suspension to another (10-3‑5 mg/g dry mass). Both scopoletin and scopolin were detected in the P. somniferum L cell suspensions. The identity of this scopolin was confirmed by NMR and M.S. data. (Fliniaux et. al., 1997). C.A. 1997, 126, 290714v.

·        GC with V-17 column was effective for determination of morphine, codeine, papaverine in samples of opium poppy (Papaver somniferum). Chloroform was used in the extractions. Recoveries were 100-103%. (Ji and Wang, 1998). C.A.-130, 77189d.

·        After the isolation of an alkaloid from Papaver somniferum L. var. nigrum,its structure was determined by IR, UV and 1H and 13C-NMR spectroscopy and MS, together with selective O-methylation assay. The alkaloid was identified as 6,7,8,14-tetrahydro-4,5-epoxy-6-methoxy-17-Me morphinan-3-ol (oripavine). The analgesic activity of this compound was studied in the mouse using the hot-plate technique after i.p. drug administration. The effect lasted 60-90 min. the percentge analgesic effect (%A) and LDs (LD50) are presented. Oripavine appears to have a similar analgesic potency to morphine, but a lower therapeutic index because of severe toxicity. Toxic signs of oripavine were clonic-tonic convulsions followed by death. (Gomez, et. al., 1998). C.A.-129, 298287r.

·        Thin-layer chromatographic (TLC)-UV densitometric and gas-chromatographic-mass spectrometric detection (GC-MSD) methods were developed for simultaneous quantification of morphine and codeine in poppy capsules (Papaver somniferum). Morphine and codeine were isolated by extraction with chloroform: isopropanol (3:1, v/v) at pH = 8.5 and by solid-phase extraction on Snap-Cap cartridges at pH = 8.5. The TLC-UV densitometric quantification was performed by external standard method on silica gel plates using ethyl acetate: toluene: methanol: ammonia (68:17:10:5, v/v) as developing solvent and UV detection at 275 nm. For the GC-MSD analysis, the drugs were derivatized with acetic anhydride: pyridine (1:1, v/v) and separated on a 30 m HP5 capillary column. The quantification was performed using nalorphine as internal standard. (Popa et al., 1998).

·        Simple quick and accurate analytical procedures using HPLC with photodiode array detector are reported for Papaver somniferum for the important alkaloids. (Gupta et al, 2000). MAPA 2001-04-2308.

·        HPLC and HPCE methods have been established for determination of three bio-active alkaloids in the Chinese drug pericarpum papaveris (pericarp of Papaver somniferum) and to evaluate the quality of the drug. HPLC system includes ODS column, 0.5% ammonium acetate, 1% triethylamine methanol (49: 1: 50) and UV detection (230nm). HPLC system includes a 50 microm (ID) x 47 cm (effective length is 39.4 cm) capillary, 80 mmol/L. SDS-60 mmol/L borax (0.8 mol/L boric acid adjusted to pH 7.8), 10% methanol and UV detection (241 nm). It is suggested that the quality drug should contain morphine, codeine and papaverine not less than 0.04%, 0.02% and 0.01% respectively. (Hao et al, 2000). MAPA 2000-05-3227. CA 2000, 133, 22841z.

·        A simple and rapid method for the quantitation of 8 drugs, morphine, codeine, oripavine codeinone, reticuline, thebaine, papaverine and narcoline in P. somniferum samples by RPHPLC with photodiode array detection is described. The separation of these compounds was performed with MeCN pH 3.8 – phosphate buffer (20:80) on a Duresil C18 column with 10 mm particles (250 nm x 4.6 mm I-D). (Singh et al, 2000). C.A. 2000, 110079c.

·        HPLC and HPCE methods for determination of 3 bioactive alkaloids (morphine, codeine and papaverine) in the Chinese drug pericarpium papaveris (Papaver somniferum) were presented. A HPLC system included ODS column, 0.5% NH4OAc- 1%, Et3N-MeOH (49:1:50) and UV detection (230nm). The HPCE system included a 50 mM (ID) x 47 cm (effective length is 39.4 cm) capillary, 80 mmol L-1 SDS-60 mmol L-1 borax – 10% MeOH and UV detection (241nm). Based on the contents of the 3 bioactive alkaloids in Pericapium Papaveris should contain morphine, codeine and papaverine not < 0.04, 0.02 and 0.01% respectively. The 2 methods were accurate precise and sensitive and can be used for determination of 3 bioactive alkaloids in Pericapium Papaveris. (Hao et al, 2000). C.A. 2000, 133, 22481z.

·        Together with bismorphine A (1), a new compound bismorphine B (2) was identified in the wounded capsules of Papaver somniferum. On the basis of analyses of the various spectral data, bismorphine B (2) was determined as a novel morphinan alkaloid, in which two morphine units are coupled through a biphenyl ether bond. When the physiological function of bismorphine B (2) in opium poppy was investigated, it was evident that this alkaloid more effectively cross-links cell wall polysaccharide pectins than bismorphine A (1) and that such cross-linking reaction leads to resistance against hydrolysis by pectinase. (Morimoto et al., 2003).

Pharmacology:

·        Papaverine enhanced oxygen consumption and formation of creatine phosphate during respiration in myocardial tissue of rabbits. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1960-1969).

·        Rapid I.V. injection of 0.12-7.0 mg papaverine decreased arterial blood pressure for 10-20 seconds in dogs anaesthetized with pentobarbitol and and caused marked increase in cardiac output at a nearly constant heart rate, probably the result of marked peripheral vasodilation. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1960-1969).

·        Marked spasmolytic effect was produced by papaverine and spasmaverin on corpus and isthmus of human uteri. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1960-1969).

·        Morphine at dose of 11-20 mg/kg decreased respiratory rate, respiratory minute volume, pulse rate, blood pressure and serum pH and increased serum carbon dioxide concentration in decerebrate cat. Nalorphine (5 and 10 mg/kg) produced moderate increase in respiratory rate and minute volume and modest decrease in serum carbon dioxide and blood pressure. Nalorphine administration to decerebrate cats severely depressed by morphine, produced transient and marked increase in respiratory rate, minute volume, blood pressure and pulse rate. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1960-1969).

·     Morphine sulphate (2 mg/kg) significantly increased serum magnesium level in rats; the increase was antagonized by nalorphine hydrochloride administered simultaneously with the agonist. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·     Morphine significantly decreased content of cytochrome p-450 in male, but not in female rats; hydroxylation of progesterone and testosterone was markedly decreased in morphine-treated male rats. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·     Morphine at 100 mg/kg and 200 mg/kg doses increased brain acetylcholine in mice by 18 and 32% respectively. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·     Morphine hydrochloride (20 or 40mg/kg) significantly elevated glucose level in blood, but did not change concentration of free fatty acids in serum of rats. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·      Morphine caused both local and neurally mediated decrease in vascular resistance of isolated canine gracilis muscle. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·     Administration of morphine (0.5 and 5.0 mg/kg, i.p. or I.V.) to unanaesthetised cats caused dose-related efflux of acetylcholine into cerebral lateral ventricle from cerebral cortex. Pretreatment with naloxone (0.1 mg/kg) prevented morphine -induced increased release. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·      Morphine antagonised intestinal stimulatory effect of 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) in anaesthetised guinea pigs, dogs, cats and monkeys. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·     Administration of morphine to adult male rats decreased their body weight, thyroid weight and pituitary thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) content and increased pituitary and adrenal weights. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·      Intestinal motor responses (contraction) produced by 10 and 20 Ilg/kg morphine were markedly reduced in intestinal segments isolated from daily morphine- treated dogs; thereby concluding that tolerance to intestinal stimulatory effect of morphine occurs in dog. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·     Chronic administration of morphine decreased both wet and dry weights of prostate and seminal vesicles and reduced their secretory activity. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·     Effect of acute administration of morphine sulphate up to 256 mg./kg, s.c., on cerebral excitability in adult male rats was investigated with flurothyl and pentylene tetrazol; morphine showed anticonvulsant activity against both drugs. Morphine sulphate (0.5-16 mg/kg,I.V.) caused dose-related increase in intestinal intraluminal pressure in anaesthetised rats. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·     Subcutaneous administration of morphine hydrochloride (10 mg/kg) produced marked increase in locomotor activity in mice. Morphine- induced hyperactivity was potentiated by scopolamine and attenuated by physostigmine. Activity-increasing effects of morphine were mediated by release of catecholamines from adrenergic neurons in brain. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·     Both acute and chronic administration of morphine significantly lowered luteinizing hormone and testosterone levels in scrufi of rats. Morphine exerted its effect on hypothalamus rather than on pituitary, probably by inhibiting release of LH -releasing factor. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·     Morphine and azidomorphine given to cats and rats depressed spontaneous and bradykinin- induced neuronal electric activity of sensory motor cortex. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·      S.c. administration of morphine twice a day in adult male rats (initial dose 20 mg./kg, dose increased by 20 fig/kg every 3 days, after 16th day dose fixed at 100 fig/kg) for 45 days caused loss of weight, hypertrophy of adrenals, decrease in weight of accessory sex organs, sperm count and in copulation rate; serum luteinizing hormone and testosterone levels were decreased, but serum FSH level was increased; results suggest that morphine inhibits hypothalamic- pituitary-gonadal axis. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·     Morphine caused more side effects than azidomorphine in humans; most frequent were vomiting, nausea and dizziness. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·     Morphine sulphate (2.5-50 mg intracerebroventricularly or 1-10 mg/kg,I.V.) produced dose-related hypothermic response in conscious, unrestrained cats. It was 850 times as potent when injected into third ventricle as when given i. v. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·     Morphine sulphate (2.5-160 mg/kg) administered to mice at 25°, produced dose-dependent hypothermia; dose-dependence was obscured when ambient temperature was either 30° or 20°. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·     Morphine increased serum prolactin (PRL) level of male rats in adose-dependent manner morphine inhibited catecholamine-stimulated adenosine triphosphatase (A TP-ase) activities in mouse brain synaptosomes both in vivo and in vitro; upto 0.001M it had no effect on basal ATP-ase activities but at 0.000lM it significantly inhibited dopamine-sensitive ATP-ase activities in vitro. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·     Papaverine and eupaverine increased rate of uptake of calcium by sarcoplasmic vesicles isolated from rabbit white skeletal muscle. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·     Papaverine (5 mg/kg,I.V.) prolonged total duration of synchronized sleep and depressed the duration of total desynchronized sleep in deprived rats as compared to nondeprived rats. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·     Papaverine blocked 45Ca2 + uptake in depolarized smooth muscles of Taenia coli of uinea pig; the blockade was less in partially depolarized muscles, which suggests that papaverine possibly competes for the same receptor site with calcium from extracellular fluid in depolarized muscle. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·     Papaverine (0.026 mM) inhibited growth of and increased cyclic AMP and cyclic GMP in human neuroblastoma cultures. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979).

·     Analgesic, antitussive, cardiovascular and binding properties of (+) codeine and (-) codeine were compared. (-)Codeine was active as analgesic while (+)codeine had no analgesic activity. Both showed antitussive effect by decreasing amplitude of cough in dose- related manner; effect being 6-fold greater with (-)codeine than with (+)codeine.    (-)Codeine decreased heart rate, caused transient decrease in blood pressure and at 0.000016 M inhibited stereo specific binding of 3H-labelled dihydromorphine to mouse brain homogenate by 50%. (+)Codeine had more pronounced cardiovascular effects but failed to inhibit binding even at 0.0001 M concentration. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·     Coramine at small doses showed sedative effect in laboratory animals. It had no analgesic or tranquillizing effects. At higher doses it induced clonic convulsions and had stimulatory effect on isolated intestinal and myometrial muscles. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·     Intra-arterial injection of papaverine hydrochloride (1-30 mg) did not induce secretory action of pancreatic juice in isolated blood-perfused pancreas of dog, while dose-dependent increase was observed at larger doses (0.1-1.0 mg). Secretory activity of papaverine (1.0 mg) was approximately equal to that of 0.06 unit of secretin. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·     Papaverine decreased efflux of potassium- 42 in guinea pig atrial heart muscle. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·     Papaverine (0.5 and 1 mg/kg, I.V.) given to cats diminished regional cerebrovascular resistance to 86 and 73% respectively after one min of administration. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·     Vasodilatory effects of papaverine on coronary blood flow markedly reduced in atherosclerotic rat heart. Reduction of potency attributed to calcification and loss of elasticity in arterial wall. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·     Ability of papaverine to increase brain blood supply and decrease arterial pressure was enhanced in dogs and cats during indomethacin-induced inhibition of prostaglandin biosynthesis. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·     Papaverine maintained compartmentation of K+, energy metabolism and membrane fluidity of Ehrlich ascites tumor cell membranes by regulating intracellular mitochondrial metabolism of cells. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·     Papaverine enhanced cytotoxicity of nitrogen mustard in cell cultures of C1300 round cell neuroblastoma. It showed synergistic effect with chemotherapy of neuroblastoma in mice and may be useful clinically as adjunct in treatment of neuroblastoma. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·     Papaverine inhibited cholinergic stimulation of colonic smooth muscle from transverse colon of cats but had minimal effects on stimulation of this muscle by cholecystokinin octapeptide. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·     Papaverine depressed and antagonised noradrenaline, renaline-induced muscle contractions but did not depress acetylcholine induced contractions in chick (early life) smooth muscle. Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·     I.V. injection of papaverine in human patients increased pH and decreased carbondioxide in arterial blood and cerebrospinal fluid. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·     Studies on spasmolytic action of papaverine in isolated seminal vesicles from guinea pigs in relation to c-AMP showed that it inhibited responses to contractile agents (K+, Ba++ and Ca++) whereas c-AMP content was increased in dose-dependent manner. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·     In isolated rabbit aortic smooth muscle preparation, papaverine inhibited both noradrenaline and K+-induced contractions with IC50 of 2.5 and 3*0.00001 M/1 respectively. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·     Gastric cytoprotection by papaverine in rats was observed after intragastric administration of papaverine, which is known to stimulate endogenous prostaglandin synthesis. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·     Papaverine (0.7 mg/kg, I.V.) abolished nociceptive thalmic responses in anaesthetised rats whereas at 2.5 mg/kg it attenuated medullary dorsal horn response. The greater sensitivity of thalmic responses indicated that action of morphine at this site is not merely spinal but mainly supraspinal. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·     I.V. administration of morphine to normal and genetically-deficient antidiuretic hormone (ADH) rats caused significant decrease in mean arterial pressure and urine flow. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·     Morphine (0.33 mg/kg, s.c.) produced antidiuresis in normal dogs, which was suppressed by simultaneous administration of naloxone (5 mg/kg, I.V.). (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·     Acute administration of morphine to rats (4 fig/kg, I.V.) followed by infusion of 2 mg/kg/h caused increase in urine flow, which was variable in magnitude and duration. Initial diuresis was not maintained despite continued morphine administration and replacement of lost fluid. All rats excreted hypotonic urine. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

·     Morphine sulphate (0.01, 0.1 and 1 mg/kg, I.V.) increased net intestinal sodium and water absorption in fed but not fasted dogs, primarily due to increased absorptive fluxes. It reduced arterial pressure and increased absorptive site blood flow due to reduced resistance. Morphine also reduced secretory fluxes at a given capillary pressure in fed dogs. Electrical field stimulation evoked a biphasic contraction, consisting of cholinergically-mediated fast contraction followed by non-cholinergically-mediated slow contraction. Morphine caused concentration dependent inhibition of non-cholinergic contraction.
