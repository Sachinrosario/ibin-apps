Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Turmeric (Curcuma longa L.) is a tropical herbaceous erect perennial herb of the family Zingiberaceae grown as an annual crop. Plant has thick fleshy rhizomes and leaves borne in a tuft. The genus Curcuma originated in the Indo-Malayan region (Purseglove, 1968). It has 42 species out of which, Curcuma longa is cultivated in larger areas under commercial cultivation while, Curcuma aromatica for aromatic flavour and recently Curcuma amada (Mango ginger) cultivated in limited area for preparation of chutnies. The antiquity of turmeric dates back to the Assyrians of 600 BC. Ethnobotanical evidences indicate that the use of turmeric has been in India since very ancient days. Turmeric is traditionally used in India for medicinal, religious, culinary purposes and also as a cosmetic and dye (Shah, 1997). Rhizomes are rich in curcumin for which turmeric is cultivated. Though turmeric is now grown in India, Pakistan, Malaysia, Myanmar, Vietnam, Thailand, Philippines, Japan, China, Korea, Sri Lanka, Nepal, South Pacific islands, East and West Africa, Malagasy, Caribbean Islands and Central America also cultivate turmeric (5).  

Climate and soil
Turmeric can be cultivated in most areas of the tropic and subtropics provided that rainfall is adequate or facilities for irrigation are available. It requires a hot and moist climate. It grown in regions with an annual rainfall of 1000-2000 mm; below 1000 mm irrigation is required. It can be grown to an altitude of 1200 m in the Himalayan foothills. A temperature range of 30-35°C during germination, 25-30°C during tillering, 20-25°C during rhizome initiation and 18-20°C during bulking stage are optimum (Panigarhi et al., 1987). Turmeric thrives best in loamy or alluvial, loose friable, fertile soils with a pH range of 5.0 – 7.5 and cannot withstand waterlogging. Gravely, stony and heavy clay soils are unsuitable for the development of the rhizomes. Turmeric can be rotated with crops such as finger millet (Eleusine coracana), rice and sugarcane. It is rarely cultivated in a pure stand, but is usually grown mixed with such crops as castor, maize, finger millet, onions, brinjal and tomato (8).

Availability :

The plant is cultivated throughout the tropics, but is doubtfully wild anywhere in India. It is widely cultivated in all states. According to many authors, the native country of the species is not certainly known.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:
An erect pseudostem bearing leaves and inflorescence mainly represents morphology of the plant. There may be 2-3 pseudostems (tillers) per plant. The height of pseudostem varies from 90-100 cm depending on the variety. Leaf number varies from 7-12. In fact it is the green leaf sheath which forms the pseudostem. petiole is thin, rather abruptly broadened to the sheath. The ligule is a small lobe, about 1 mm long. Lamina lanceolate or elliptic in shape, thin with acuminate tip. The lamina is green above and pale green below with pellucid dots, having a length of about 30-40 cm and width 8-12 cm. The sheath near the ligule has ciliate margins. At the base of the pseudostem, below the ground, rhizomes are formed consisting of mother rhizome(s), primary, secondary and even tertiary fingers, the whole forming a compact clump. The primary tuber is ellipsoidal, about 5 cm by 2.5 cm. The rhizomes are 5-8 cm long, 1.5 cm thick, straight or a little curved. Rhizomes grow symbodically and are of orange brown, pale yellow or reddish yellow colour.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR: Inflorescence, which is terminal, is a fleshy, cylindrical, central spike of 10-15 cm length and 5-7 cm wide, arising through the pseudostem. Flowers are subtended by bracts in the spike. The bracts, 5-6 cm long and 2.5 cm wide, are adnate for less than half of their length and are elliptic, lanceolate and acute. Upper sterile bracts are white or white streaked with green, pink tipped in some cultivars, grading to light green bracts lower down. Bracteoles are thin, elliptic and up to 3.5 cm long. One to four flowers are borne in the axil of the bract, opening one at a time and about 5 cm long. About 30 flowers are produced in a spike (Nazeem and Rema Menon, 1994). Calyx is short, unequally toothed and split nearly halfway down on one side. Corolla is tubular at the base with upper half cup-shaped with three unequal lobes inserted on the edge of the cup lip, thin, translucent, whitish with a yellow tip and dorsal lobe hooded. There are two lateral staminodes, elliptic-oblong, which are creamy white in colour, and with inner edges folded under the hood of the dorsal petal. Lip or labellum is obovate, with a broad thickened yellow band down the centre and thinner creamy white side-lobes upcurved and overlapping the staminodes. Filament of the stamen is short and broad, united to a versatile anther about the middle of the parallel pollen sacs, and with a broad, curved large spur at the base. Cylindrical stylodes are about 4 mm long. Ovary is inferior and trilocular with a slender style passing between the anther lobes and held by them. Fruits are seldom, if ever produced. Normally the uppermost and the lowermost bracts will be sterile (6).  

The time required for the appearance of inflorescence is 102-116 days and the number of flowers per inflorescence ranged from 47 to 55. The opening of the flower starts at 7 am and continues up to 4 pm. Anther dehiscence occurs between 10.15 to 11.40 am. Even though pollen fertility is reasonably high (53-58%).

Breeding system and varietal improvement:

Phenology:
Flowering and fruiting: September - October. The time required for the appearance of inflorescence was 3 to 4 months.

Pollinators: Insect pollination especially by honey bees.

Floral and seed biology: Seed set is observed and seeds are viable. Seeds are produced in sunken capsules and there will be from one to numerous capsules in an inflorescence depending on the flowers fertilized. Most of the turmeric varieties flower and set seed under Kerala conditions. The seeds take 45 to 60 days for germination. The seedlings vary widely for morphological, quality and rhizome characters (7).
Cytology 
C. longa is considered to be a triploid with a chromosome number of 63 (2n = 63), C. domestica has 2n = 32 (Sato, 1948) and C. aromatica has 2n = 84 (Nambiar, 1979).

CUITIVATION ASPECT:

Planting Material: Turmeric is always propagated vegetatively by fingers or rhizomes with one or two buds. Seed requirement for turmeric is high i.e. 2½ tonnes of seed is required to plant one hectare. Different plant materials, mother rhizomes, cut mothers and primary fingers are used as seed. Aiyadurai (1966) states that mother rhizomes are better than daughter rhizomes for use as seed material. The sprouts measuring 8 to 12 inches, detached from sprouting rhizomes were found to be best material for planting and gave the highest yield (6).

Time of planting: The planting time varied with region and cultivar and usual time for planting of turmeric in India is May and June months. 

Method of planting and spacing: The land should be thoroughly prepared either by digging or several ploughings to provide a good tilth. The crop can be planted on flat beds or on ridges. Flat beds or broad ridge method of planting are used depending upon the area. The optimum spacing found to be 45 and 60 cm between the rows and 22.5 cm between the plants (Aiyadurai 1966). A closer spacing of 22-25 cm with high plant densities was suggested in Kerala by Philip (1985) and in Punjab (Radhawa and Misra, 1974). 

Mulching and Intercropping: Germination of the setts is completed in 2-4 weeks. Active rhizome development continues until the crop is ready for lifting at about 7-9 months, when the lower leaves turn yellow (6). Mulching reduces the weed growth and enhances germination. Turmeric is cultivated as sole crop or intercropped with other crops such as maize, castor and also between coconut plants. In Tamil Nadu double intercropping with fenugreek and onion is practiced.

Nutritional Requirements: Turmeric is a heavy feeder and more uptake of nutrients (Nagarajan and Pillai 1979 and Sivaraman 1992). Manuring plays very important role in the management of turmeric and good response to Nitrogen for boosting up vegetative growth (Umate et al. 1984), Phosphorous is necessary for root development and K thought to be involved in the transportation of carbohydrates to rhizomes (Muralidharan and Balakrishnan 1972). In addition, K may also improve curcumin content (Mohan babu and Muthuswamy, 1984; Ahmed Shah et al. 1988). Applications of 100 kg/ha ammonium sulphate doubled the yield over that of unmanured crop. Applications of 100 kg N, 100 kg P2O5 and 200 kg K2O/ha under Kerala conditions is recommended. Nutrient uptake of nutrients is greater up to 3rd month for K, 4th month for N and 5th month for P with subsequent decrease in the concentration of these nutrients. Large quantities of organic manures in the form of FYM oil cakes, green leaves (mulch), tank silt and sheep penning are applied in the different turmeric growing area. Organic manures were found to be helpful in the improvement of rhizome quality especially colour and curing percentage (2000).
Turmeric is usually harvested 8-9 months after planting (Aiyadurai, 1966), when the lower leaves turn yellow. The leafy tops are then cut off and the rhizomes are well washed with water. Fingers are separated from the mothers. A few rhizomes may be used as fresh and, apart from those that are required for replanting, the remainder are subjected to the various processing techniques.
MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:

Diseases:

The crop suffers from foliar as well as rhizome diseases. Among the foliar diseases, Leaf spot caused by Colletotrichum capsici and Leaf blotch caused by Taphrina maculans are important. In rhizome diseases, rot caused by Pythium graminicolum is the most serious malady of the crop. The diseases on turmeric were reviewed earlier by Joshi and Sharma (1980) and Sarma et al. (1994).

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Turmeric powder is used in mustard paste and curry powder as both colour and aroma are important in these products. It is a normal constituent of condiments, curry powers and prepared mustards. Turmeric oleoresin is used mainly in the brine pickles (Eiserle, 1966; Cripps, 1967) and to some extent in mayonnaise and relish formulations; in non-alcoholic beverages such as orangeades and lemonades; gelatins; in breading of frozen fish sticks; potato croquettes; butter and cheese in the form of powder or granules for garnishing and even in ice creams (Perotti, 1975). In all these cases, its function is predominantly to colour the product and it merely replaces the synthetic colours such as tartrazine (Govindarajan, 1980). In Asian countries whole, dry or fresh turmeric, ground or turmeric powder with other spices like chillies, coriander, pepper, cumin, etc are used for making vegetable, meat and soup-like dishes. Turmeric powder mixed with sesame, coconut or groundnut oil is used for pickling mango, lime, gooseberry, garlic etc. (Govindarajan, 1980). Turmeric powder, extracts and curcumin exhibit antioxidant property. In foods this prevented the formation of peroxide (Khanna, 1999). 

Medicinal uses
Turmeric is used medicinally in the traditional system of medicines against many ailments. The biological activity of turmeric is as anti-inflammatory, hypocholestremic, choleratic, antimicrobial, antirheumatic, antibacterial, antiviral, cytotoxic, spasmolytic, hypersensitive, antidiabetic and antihepato toxic (Govindarajan, 1980; Tonnessen, 1986; Velayudhan et al., 1999). Turmeric is also credited with anticancerous properties (Kuttan et al., 1985; Rao et al., 1995). Aqueous extract, fresh juice and essential oil of turmeric also have biopesticidal properties (Kapoor, 1998; Saju et al., 1998; Bora and Jaya Samuel, 1999). It also has actions against many pathogens. Turmeric oil is also effective as a mosquito repellent, housefly deterrent and in aphid vector control (Khanna, 1999; Saju et al., 1998) (5).  

Due to the strong antiseptic properties, it has been used as a reputed remedy for all kinds of poisonous affections, ulcers and wounds. It is aromatic, antiperiodic, germicidal, carminative, stimulant, tonic and vermifuge. A paste of turmeric alone or combined with a paste of Azadirachta indica leaves, is used to cure ringworm, obstinate itching, eczema and other parasitic skin diseases and also in chicken pox and small pox. The drug is also useful in cold, cough, bronchitis, conjunctivitis and liver infections (Nadkarni, 1954; Kurup et al., 1979; Kolammal, 1979). Ethanol extract of turmeric and an ointment of curcumin produce remarkable relief in patients with external cancerous lesions (Kuttan et al., 1987) (4).

Turmeric is also a great tonic in general, diuretic, expectorant, blood-purifier, skin tonic, pain reliever, germicidal, anti-flatulent, producer and enhancer of red blood corpuscles, anti-phlegm, attic, anti-bilious, protector of eyes and imparts coolness to the system. It corrects the disordered process of nutrition and restored the normal function of the system. It is also useful in curing period attacks of hysteria, measles, asthma, anemia, insect bite, skin disorders, dental problems, bacterial infection and convulsions (3).

Therapeutic Actions: Analgesic, anticoagulant, antifertility action, anti-inflammatory, antineoplastic, antioxidant. antiplatelet-aggregator, carminative, cholagogue, emmenagogue, hepatoprotective, hypotensive, increases glutathione content in liver, rate-limiting step of cholesterol conversion into bile acids, inhibits platelet aggregation, lymphocytic activity, lowers LDL cholesterol and raises HDL cholesterol, stabilizes lysosomal membranes, stimulates digestive enzymes (9).

Major types of turmeric
The various physical forms of turmeric are as follows:
Fingers - These are lateral branches or secondary, ‘daughter’ rhizomes which are detached from the central rhizome before curing. Fingers usually range in size from about 2.5 to 7.5 cm in length and over 1 cm in diameter. Broken and very small fingers are combined and marketed separately from whole fingers.
Bulbs - These are the central ‘mother’ rhizomes which are ovate in shape and are of a shorter length but a greater diameter than fingers. 
Splits - Splits are bulbs which have been cut into halves or quarters before curing to facilitate subsequent drying. 

Characteristics of three major types of turmeric entering the international market are summarised as follows:
Alleppey turmeric
Produced in Kerala and marketed in Alleppey, but grown in surrounding regions, particularly in the Moovatupuzha and Thodupuzha areas. Alleppey turmeric is deep yellow to orange-yellow in colour and has higher tinctorial powers (curcumin contents of up to 6.5%) than other types of turmeric. Almost the entire production of Alleppey turmeric is exported, mainly to US market and in the unpolished form, where it is used largely as a food colourant. 

Madras turmeric 
Produced in Tamil Nadu and marketed in Madras but grown from several regional cultivars. Rhizomes are mustard-yellow in colour and have a curcumin content of around 3.5%. It is the most common type used in UK, where it is regarded as superior in quality and flavour.

West Indian turmeric 
Exported from the Caribbean, Central and South American countries. Rhizomes are dull yellowish-brown in colour, mostly small and of poor appearance. West Indian turmeric is regarded as inferior in quality to Indian turmeric (8).

Produce and products
Turmeric powder: Turmeric is valued for its aroma and colour. The colouring matter is stable to heat, hence "Cryogenic grinding' is not necessary. The powder is mostly consumed domestically for culinary purposes. Turmeric powder is an important constituent to prepare curry powders, various spice mixtures, pickles and soups.


Produce and Products:
Oleoresins: Oleoresins are generally mixture of compounds, volatile oils and other active ingredients non-volatile fatty and resinous materials, extractable by solvents, used singly, in sequence or in combinations. Oleoresin is a highly viscous orange brown product containing 30-35% curcumin, 15-20% volatile oil and has a characteristic turmeric aroma. Turmeric oleoresin is extracted from good quality turmeric powder with solvents like acetone - alcohol or ethylene dichloride and subsequent desolventization. Since it is highly viscous, it is mixed with diluents like propylene glycol or vegetable oil to obtain a homogenous pourable product. Turmeric oleoresin finds application in meat and fish products pickles, dairy products and confectionary. The oleoresin content in turmeric varied from 4 to 7.5 (Murthy et al., 1972).

Volatile oils: Turmeric rhizomes containing 3 to 5% essential oil which is usually obtained by the steam distillation of ground spice. Turmeric oil is light yellow in colour with harsh odour. Turmeric oil is not having much commercial importance at present. The major constituents of the oil are Turmerones (58%) and Zigiberene (25%). The odour is mainly due to Turmerones. Zingiberenes did not contribute much for the aroma. Turmeric dried and cured generally yields 1.5 to 5 per cent volatile oil. Among the three groups of turmeric, C. aromatica types and medium duration Kesari types has higher oil content while it is lower in long duration types. Subbarayudu et al. (1976) observed higher oil content in medium duration types (5.3 to 6.8%), low (2.2 to 4.2%) in long duration types, and moderate in short duration types (3.3 to 6.0%). Volatile oil content is more in C. aromatica (Reddy et al., 1990). 

Curcumin: Colouring pigment present in turmeric. Alleppey turmeric contained 6.5 percent curcumin as against 3-4 percent in other varieties (Lewis, 1973). 0.2 to 3.8 percent of curcumin is present in dried rhizomes of turmeric (Shankaracharya 1974). Among 38 types of turmeric, curcumin content varied from 3.0 to 8.1 % with the lowest in Nandyal and the highest in Vontimitta (Mathai, 1974). Cultivars of C. longa comprising medium and long duration types contained higher proportion of curcumin than C. aromatica types which recorded lowest values (Rao et al., 1975).

Chemistry:

Turmeric is valued mainly for its principal colouring pigment, curcumin, which imparts the yellow colour to turmeric (Peter, 1999). Besides curcumin there are few other related pigments that impart the yellow colour, all together called curcuminoids (Verghese, 1999). Curcumin [1,7-bis (4-hydroxy-3-methoxy-phenyl)-1,6-heptadiene-3,5-dione]; demethoxy curcumin [4-hydroxy-cinnamoyl (4-hydroxy-3-methoxycinnamoyl)] methane and bis-demethoxy curcumin [bis-(4-hydroxy cinnamoyl methane)] together make the colouring pigment in turmeric rhizomes. ‘Alleppey’ type recorded maximum colour. In the pure form curcuminoids separate as an orange yellow crystalline powder, insoluble in water, slightly soluble in ether, soluble in alcohol and in glacial acetic acid. Turmeric oil has a major role in the aroma and flavour of turmeric though the oil as such is not used. Turmeric oil is comprised of oxygenated sesquiterpenes which are accompanied by smaller quantities of sesquiterpene hydrocarbons, monoterpene hydrocarbons and oxygenated monoterpenes. Among the various constituents of the oil, sesquiterpenes, ar-turmerone and turmerone comprise 50 percent of the oil (Purseglove et al., 1981) (5).  

Analysis of Indian turmeric gave the following values: moisture, 13.5; protein, 6.3; fat, 5.1; mineral matter, 3.5; fibre, 2.6; carbohydrates, 69.4%; carotene calculated as vitamin A, 50 I.U./100 g. (Hlth Bull., No. 23, 1941, 37). The essential oil (5.8%) obtained by steam distillation of dry rhizomes has the following constants d20˚20˚, 0.929; nD30˚, 1.5054; [α]D30˚, -17.3˚; ester val., 3.2; and acet. val., 26.3. It contains d–α-phellandrene, 1; d-sabinene, 0.6; cineol, 1; borneol, 0.5; zingiberene, 25; sesquiterpenes (turmerones), 58% (Kelkar & Rao, J. Indian Inst. Sci., 1933, 17A, 7). A ketone, C13H20O, and an alcohol, C9H11OH, identified as p-tolylmethyl carbinol, have been obtained from the volatile distillate (Chem. Abstr.,1933, 27, 4876). The crystalline colouring matter, curcumin (yield, c., 0.6%; m.p., 180-183˚), is a diferuloyl methane of formula C21H20O6. It dissolves in concentrated sulphuric acid giving a yellow-red colouration. The antioxidant properties of curcuma powder are probably due to the phenolic character of curcumin (Mayer & Cook, 93; Chem. Abstr.,1948, 42, 8496). The choleretic action of the essential oil is attributed to p-tolylmethyl carbinol. The dyestuff acts as a cholagogue causing the contraction of the gall bladder (Chem. Abstr.,1934, 28, 3833, 1935, 29, 1492; 1936, 30, 6822).

Alantone, tumerone, zingerberone, resin, sugars: arabinose, glucose, fructose, ukonan-A, a phagocytosis activating factor, ukonan-D, a reticuloendothelial system-potentiating activity are also present.

Processing Techniques:

Consists of boiling, drying, polishing and colouring of raw turmeric rhizomes. Boiling is done in turmeric to kill the biological activity of the cells as well as to get uniform colour throughout the rhizome.

Boiling: After digging, the rhizomes are cleaned and finger and mother rhizomes are separated. Normally mother rhizomes will take more time for cooking than the fingers (Rao et al., 1975) and separate boiling in clean water is required to get uniform cooking. Raw rhizomes are cooked in water just sufficient to cover rhizomes in a boiling pan. Turmeric leaves are used as a fuel. While boiling, over cooking spoils the colour and under cooking renders the dried product brittle, resulting in a higher percentage of pieces during subsequent polishing (Govindarajan, 1980). Curing should be completed within 2 to 3 days of harvesting to get maximum colour. CFTRI has developed a simpler, hygienic and efficient technique of curing and colouring of turmeric where, rhizomes are boiled in lime water or sodium carbonate. A water solution containing 20 g sodium bisulphite and 20 g of hydrochloric acid per 45.3 kg of rhizomes gives them desired yellow colour. To export turmeric, any chemicals or mammalian excreta should not be added while boiling. 

Drying: Cooking of the rhizomes facilitates quicker drying and boiled turmeric is sun-dried for 10-15 days to dry about 5 per cent moisture level, till it becomes quite hard and brittle. When mechanical driers are used for drying, the temperature is maintained within 50-60˚C as there is loss of volatile substances beyond this range (Natarajan and Shankaracharya, 1974). Artificial drying is found to be advantageous in case of sliced turmeric as it suffers surface bleaching in sun-drying. Turmeric should be properly dried or else aflatoxin problems may arise due to more moisture content and become unsuitable for export.

Polishing: Dried turmeric will have rough and dull appearances with scales and root bits. It is subjected polishing the outer surface by manual or mechanical drying (Shankaracharya and Natarajan, 1973; Narayanan et al., 1978) in order to improve its colour and appearance. The loss in weight due to full polishing is about 5-8% (Rao, 1965; Shankaracharya and Natarajan, 1973) whereas it is 2-3% in half drying. There are power operated polishing machines for polishing.

Colouring: Rhizomes are made more attractive by resorting to artificial colouring. There are two methods of colouring viz., dry method and wet method. Central Food Technological Research Institute (Pruthey, 1976; Anonymous, 1979) has developed an improved and harmless method for colouring turmeric where the fingers are taken in a wicker basket, shaken continuously and simultaneously a prepared emulsion is poured in to it, when the fingers are uniformly coated with the emulsion, they are dried in sun. The wet process requires further drying before storage (Govindarajan, 1980).

Quality requirement: Quality of cured turmeric is assessed on the basis of several factors such as curcumin content, organoleptic character, general appearance, size and physical form of the rhizome. Chief factors of good quality in finger turmeric are a high content of pigment, giving a deep, yellow colour, and a low bitter-principle content. Rhizomes should also be rough, hard and brittle, with numerous encircling, ridge-like annulations. Length and thickness of the rhizomes and internal colour are also important characteristics in differentiation of cultivars. When manually fractured, the break should be clean, not splintery or fibrous, and the broken surfaces waxy or horny and resinous in appearance. Endodermis, separating the cortex from the central cylinder (stele), should be clearly visible. When used as a spice or condiment, the aroma and flavour imparted by the volatile oil are important. The aroma should have a musky, pepper-like character and the flavour should be slightly aromatic and somewhat bitter.

Other uses: In middle ages it was called as “Indian saffron” because of its orange-yellow colour. In parts of Asia, turmeric water is applied as a cosmetic to lend a golden glow to the complexion. Its antiseptic properties help in preservation of food. It is used also for dyeing wool, silk and unmordanted cotton to which it imparts a yellow shade in an acid bath. It is used as a colouring matter in pharmacy, confectionery and food-industry. Turmeric paper is an official reagent in the British Pharmacopoeia for testing alkalinity. A diluted tincture of turmeric is suitable for use as a fluorescence indicator even in brown and yellow solutions (Chem. Abstr., 1938, 32, 6579).

Export potential: In India turmeric is cultivated in an area of 168,510 ha with a production of 697,970 t. The major turmeric growing states are Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Orissa followed by other states West Bengal, Assam, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Kerala, Bihar and Meghalaya. Exports
For exporting the turmeric we have to meet the standards of American Spices Traders Association Standards (ASTA). A large part of the turmeric produced in India is consumed within the country and only the surplus is exported. The fingers are considered superior in quality. India is the single largest exporter of turmeric in the world. The major importers of Indian turmeric are the Middle East countries, Japan and USA. The ITC, Geneva has estimated an annual growth rate of 10% is the world demand for turmeric. 3450 t of turmeric is exported (CMIE Report, 2000-01).

Miscellaneous Information

 Uptake of nutrients
It has been observed that the uptake of nutrients was higher up to third month for K, up to fourth month for N and up to fifth month for P with subsequent decrease. The crop attains maximum vegetative growth during the fourth and fifth months suggesting the need for earlier application of N, P, K. of all the nutrients the uptake of potassium was the highest, followed by nitrogen, magnesium and calcium (8).  

Genetic Resources
Genus Curcuma has 40 to 50 species, found in tropical Asia. Among them only two species viz., Curcuma longa L. and C. aromatica Salisb. are commercially cultivated for the production of turmeric. Details on Curcuma genetic resources, cataloguing, taxonomy, etc can be had from the book – Curcuma Genetic Resources. (Eds.) K.C. Velayudhan, V.K. Muralidharan, V.A. Amalraj, P.L. Gautam, S. Curcuma longa (Popularly known as Longa types)
Most of the high yielding cultivar fall in this group and mature in between 8-9 months. Pseudostem is tall, robust with oblong elliptic leaves narrowed at the base. Plant height reach a height up to 1m with 8 to 10 leaves. Spike length ranges between 10-18 cm. with pale yellow flowers (Bailey, 1961). Starchy root tubers are not produced at the end of fibrous roots. Rhizomes are bigger in size with more curcumin content but moderate in volatile oil.

Curmcuma aromatica (Popularly known as Kasthuri types)
In this group cultivars mature early, within 6-7 months. Pseudostem is short with elliptic/oblong leaves. Flowering is common in this species, tetraploid, fertile and set viable seed (Nair et al., 1980). Plants produce peculiar root tubers at the end to fibrous roots. Rhizomes possess pleasant unique aroma due to rich in volatile oil but moderate in curcumin content. Rhizome production is less as compared to C. longa types. It is used in the preparation of dyes, cosmetics and drugs but not used as a spice or condiment.

Curcuma amada (Mango ginger)
Popularly known as mango ginger cultivated in India for its rhizomes, which have the odour of raw mangoes and it will be used for the preparation of pickles.
