Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:

Small or moderate-sized tree with spreading branches. Obliquely cordate leaves. This is particularly characteristic of secondarygrowth forests but is also common in dry and moist thicker areas, open clearings, stream banks, pastures and lower mountain slopes. Occurs in tropical areas. Grows from sea level to about 1,200 m elevation. The tree grows best where annual rainfall is 700-1,500 mm; this corresponds usually to a 4 to 7 month dry season in tropical regions. The tree adapts to drier situations well. It adapts to a wide range of soils and does not usually require fertilization.

 

Conservation/ Threat Status : 

Wild/ naturalised/ cultivated

Lower risk - least concern

Availability: Largest supply from the south zone – chiefly Coorg and Kanara forests. Moderate supplies from North zone. Small supplies from central zone.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

A small tree with obliquely cordate leaves. It occurs either as a many-branched shrub or as a single-trunked tree, ranging from 2 to 30 m in height. Mature trunks may be 30-40 cm I diameter. The trees are usually evergreen; leaves are shed only after prolonged drought. Showy yellow flowers in large terminal and axillary panicles. Warty fruits contain a little sweet pulp and many hard seeds.

Floral and seed biology:

Tree is propagated from seed. Seeds are plentiful and they germinate easily. Seeds are believed to be dispersed by birds and mammals, including cattle. Germination is favoured by having the seed run through the digestive tract of cattle. It may also be grown from cuttings of ripe shoots, which root freely.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts Used: Bark, young foliage, seeds and Fruits.

Uses:

Wood makes an exceptional fuel and for making charcoal. Used for furniture, panel of coaches, packing cases and slack cooperage. Yields light timber used for boats, barrels.

Other uses:

Planted as an ornamental tree.

Tree yields fairly good fiber useful for rope-making.

Young branches yield fibre, occasionally made into ropes.

Decoction of inner bark reported to be used for clarifying sugar juice in West Indies.

Food – The mucilagenous green and black fruits are edible when fresh, dried or cooked. Fruits roasted seeds, extract of leaves, bark of medicinal value.

The fruit is reported to be used in Mauritius as a pectoral in cases of bronchitis. Roasted seeds are considered astringent, in Java, they are used in stomach troubles. Extract of leaves is said to reduce corpulence. Bark is tonic and demulcent. In West Indies, the inner bark is valued as a remedy for elephantiasis; an infusion of old bark is considered sudorific and useful in cutaneous and chest diseases.

Forage – Young foliage and fruits are browsed by horses, cattle and deer in the dry periods. The fruits are sought by hogs and cattle. Fruit contains edible mucilage. If consumed in excess causes diarrhoea.

Shade – The tree is often grown as a shade tree for pastures and streets.

Miscellaneous Information


 Wood: Wood yellowish or light brown, strong, light to medium heavy and even grained.

The tree could become a weed tree when introduced to new areas. Dispersed through cattle.
