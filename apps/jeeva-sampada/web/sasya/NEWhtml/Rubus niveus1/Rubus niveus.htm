HABIT & HABITAT
A large spreading prickly shrub with long purple prunose stems often overhanging and rooting at the extremities It is found in the temperate Himalayan regions from Punjab to Assam and in the Deccan peninsula and western ghats at altitudes of 1,200 m. and above. 

The fruits are well flavoured and plentiful. The plant is said to be common in moist evergreen forests of Mahabaleshwar. This species is said to have been successfully introduced into kitchen gardens as well as commercial cultivation in S. Florida, USA, under the name Mysore Raspberry and from there it has recently been taken to Puerto Rico .It is said to grow even on alkaline soils in S. Florida but grows best in the drained fertile soils.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES : 
Leaves 10-20 cm. long, with 5-9 leaflets; flowers pink, in lax terminal and axillary corymbs; fruit 0.6-1.8 cm. in diam., black when ripe.

Seed Biology :

Seedling Germination : 
The plants can be propagated by seed, cuttings or tip layers; propagation by seed is rarely done. Provision of stakes or trellises recommended. 

Cultivation:
Blackberries and raspberries thrive well under temperate or sub-tropical conditions that exist at elevations of 1,500-2,100 m. in this country. They can be successfully cultivated on a wide range of soil types though, in gene- ral, soils of moderate fertility well supplied with humus are desirable. In sites where the water table remains low throughout the year or where it approaches the surface for only a few days in the spring, the roots penetrate deeply and branch profusely, shoot growth is vigorous, and yields are moderate to heavy. Soils with a range of pH 5.8-6-7 are said to be very favourable. Some species like R niveus (Mysore Raspberry), have been found to grow even on alkaline soils in USA. The soil is prepared by deep ploughing and through pulverizing, and where possible a leguminous crop like cow pea is ploughed under. A liberal application of farmyard manure at 20-25 tonnes per hectare is said to be very helpful. Goat manure is applied in Bangalore. Manuring is done at the time of planting and again twice a year afterwards. Arti- ficial fertilizers are not generally preferred, though complete fertilizers are said to have proved as effective as farmyard manure. High nitrogen favours vegetative vigour, but for fruit production additional potash is re- quired.

Propagation is done by means of tip layers in the case of many species, while root-suckers are used in others. Root-cuttings are also employed in some types of black-berry. The suckers which are produced in abundance from the underground parts of the parent plants in summer are dug out the following year for planting. The best period for tip layering at Coonoor is said to be September-October. Small holes 7-10 cm. deep are made in the ground near the parent plant and a small quantity of manure is placed in each hole. The canes are bent and their tips buried in these holes.

The tip layers grow a good root system in two or three months, but may be left to grow where they are, till transplanting. While separating the tip layers from the parent plant it is better to leave a length of 15-20 cm. of the original branch so as to providing a sort of handle to facilitate later hand- ling of the layered plants (Shoemaker, 250-58; Rao & Rangacharlu, loc. cit.). Planting is best done in moderately rainy weather.

The plants are set in rows with a distance of 0-6 m. between plants and 1-8 m. between the rows. In Bangalore, where propagation is carried on by tip layering, planting is done throughout the year, wherever irrigation is avail- able. The plants are set at a distance of 2-3 m. in pits 0-4 m.x0-4 m.x0-4 m., with about 1,000 plants to a hectare. In USA where the raspberry is grown on a large scale, the plants may be planted and trained in the 'hed- gerow system' or in the 'hill system'. 
Shallow intercultivation may be done so as not to injure the roots of the growing plants. Mulching may also be done to keep down weeds and conserve moisture.
As the plant grows, a number of branches or 'canes' are produced which are cut back early in spring. This induces the formation of side branches on which the fruit is borne later on in the year. Old canes which have borne fruit are cut back after the harvest is over and others allowed to emerge for subsequent fruiting and this pro-cess is repeated every year. Erect types of raspberries and blackberries are pruned, thinned and trained without the support of trellis or stake but trailing types give im-proved yields if trained on a wire trellis. The plants may, in the case of some types, produce during their growth a number of root-suckers and these are periodically thinned as they are likely to weaken the main plant.

Blackberries are said to come to bearing from the second year after planting, while raspberries begin to yield an year later. In Bangalore the plants are said to come to flower about five months after planting. In this area the plants flower and fruit throughout the year, though the period February-March gives the highest yields (6,000 kg./ha.), while the period June-August gives the lowest yields (1,500 kg./ha.). The economic cropping life of a raspberry plantation is 5-6 years in this area. under the conditions obtaining in the Nilgiri hills the blackberry crop comes to harvest from May and lasts for a month, while the raspberry crop comes to harvest a month later. Great care is required while picking and in subsequent handling as the fruit is liable to be very .easily injured or bruised and this may lead to loss by the attack of mould. Raspberries are ready for picking when they separate readily from the receptacle or 'core'.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES :
Pests: The incidence of fruit fly is said to have limited its cultivation.

Diseases:  A number of fungi have been recorded on the various species of Rubus found in this country but there have been so far no reports of attacks of an epidemic nature. Since many kinds of red raspberries belonging to R idaeus are said to carry a masked form of green mosaic that is not greatly harmful to them but may be transferred by aphids to other raspberries which may be seriously affected, it is better not to grow these together.

Fruits

Uses
It is also cultivated for its fruits which are sold dessert and for making into jam.
( In general : Fresh raspberries and blackberries are not adapted to storage and are usually not stored commercially. For short periods of 7-10 days most of them can be kept in fair condition at 31-32°F. and a relative humidity of 80- 85 per cent (Shoemaker, 315). Raspberries are consumed fresh in large quantities. They are made into jam, jelly and syrup and are also canned. They are popular as flavouring in ice-cream and sherbet, as a topping of ice-cream and for pudding.)
Harvesting Practices
(General information : They are picked directly into the box or cup in which they are sold and should not be handled again for grad- ing or' sorting. The ripe fruits have a fine flavour and aroma. when they first ripen, but this is not generally retained for more than a few days. If allowed to hang after ripening the fruit soon shrivels and becomes use- less (Information from Messrs Kissan Products Ltd., Bangalore; Rao & Rangacharlu, loc. cit.; Shoemaker, 312; Talbert, 143; Chittenden, IV, 1743). Produce and Products
Raspberries are consumed fresh in large quantities. They are made into jam, jelly and syrup and are also canned. They are popular as flavouring in ice-cream and sherbet, as a topping of ice-cream and for pudding.
The juice has a pleasant flavour and blends well with other fruit juices. The syrup is given to the sick for restoring taste and owing to its flavour and mild acidity it is especially effective for masking salty drugs such as bromides and iodides. A delicate wine is prepared from raspberries in the same way as from strawberries

Analysis of European raspberries (R. idaeus) gave the following values : water, 83.2: protein, 0-9; sugars, 5.6; and fibre, 7.4%; sodium, 2.5; potassium, 224; calcium,.40.7; magnesium, 21.6; iron, 1.21; copper, 0.21; phospho-rus, 28.7; sulphur, 17.3; chlorine, 22.3: carotene, 0.08; thiamine, 0.02; riboflavin, 0.03; nicotinic acid, 0.4; panto- thenic acid, 0.20; vitamin B,, 0.09; biodn, 0.002; folk acid, 0-005; and ascorbic acid, 25 ing./'l00 g.
Fruit is a good source of y- and 'i-tocopherols. Ascorbic acid con- tent varies from 17-7 to 37-7 rng. per 100 g. of fresh berries most of which is, however, lost in preserved products. Sugars consist of glucose and fructose, with little or no sucrose. The acidity of raspberry is due predomi- nantly to citric acid (97%). malic acid being present in minor amounts (3%). Pectin (av. value, 0-4% as calcium pectate) in freshly picked raspberry is of good jellying quality, but the pectic substances change rapidly after picking with consequent loss of gelling power; this change, caused by high enzymic activity of the fruit, is arrested by application of heat. Trace elements reported in the fruit are manganese, zinc, molybdenum and iodine (11 /ig./l00 g., on dry basis). Fruit also contains tannin, flavone, red colouring matter (anthocyanin), per- oxidase, an antimetabolite of thiamine, lecithin (0-13%) and some essential oil (03).

Average composition of raspberry juice is : solids, 10-4: protein, 0.3: acid (as citric), 1.7; sugars (as invert), 6-0; tannin, 0-1; and ash, 0.45 g. 100 cc. Some discolouration is observed in stored juice in the presence of air. The change is said to be caused by the reducing action of ascorbic acid on the anthocvanin pigment.

Seeds arc obtained in large quantities as waste mate- rial from the jam and syrup industry. Dried seeds yield 10-15 per cent of a fatty oil (iod. val.," 154-75) which dries as quickly as linseed oil. The oil contains 5 per cent saturated (chiefly palmitic) and 90 per cent unsaturated (oleic, 17; linolcic, 80; and linolenic, 3%) fatty acids. Lecithin content of the seeds is 0-6 per cent.

The flavour of raspberry is less injured by cooking than that of most fruits which explains the popularity of raspberry jam and soda-water syrup. The most characteristic flavouring constituents of the fruit are the carbonyls and alcohols which include diacetyl, acetoin, acetaldehyde, acrolein, acetone, propanal, fS, fi-dime- thylacrolein, 2-pentenal, 2-pentanone, CK-3-hexenal, 2- hexenal, hexanal, (+)-a-ionone and /3- ionone, p-hydro- xyphenylbutanone-3, methanol, ethanol, 1-butanol, trans- 2-buten-l-ol, 3-methyl-2-buten-l-ol, 3-methyl-3-buten-l-ol, 1-pentanol, l-penten-3-ol, 1-hexanol, CM-3-hexen-l-ol and geraniol. Besides these, the flavouring extract from raspberry contains ethyl acetate, several acids, phenols, and a number of unidentified products.

Economic Potential To The Country
Small quantities of raspberries are grown in Mahabaleshwar (Maharashtra) and Bangalore (Mysore) mainly for utilization in the fruit processing units located in Bombay and Bangalore. The species grown in Maha-baleshwar is presumably R. niveus while the specific identity of the one grown in Bangalore is not known, though it might also probably be a form of R. niveus.

An area of about 8 ha. is reported to be devoted to the crop in the Bangalore area producing annually c. 75,000 kg. of fruit which is consumed by the Kissan Products Ltd. for manufacture of jam. Though the cultivation of 'Bangalore raspberry' was found to be a good business proposition in the Nilgiris, the incidence of fruit fly is said to have limited its cultivation. Some types of rasp- berries imported from Australia were also tried, but proved to be unsuitable for this tract.
