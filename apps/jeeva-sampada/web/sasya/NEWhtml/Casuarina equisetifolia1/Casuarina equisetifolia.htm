Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:

Tall, evergreen tree, indigenous to north and northeast Australia, some Pacific islands, and from Indonesia and Malaysia ti India and Sri Lanka. Bole straight cylindrical; branches slender jointed. Xeric, found in the tropical high rainfall areas of Western Ghats, West Bengal and Assam (wet tropical), tropical medium rainfall areas of Indo-Gangetic Plains and the Peninsula (moist tropical). Chiefly in the coastal region, on loose sandy soils especially near sea. In its natural state it is gregarious with little or no undergrowth. Suitable for coastal sandy areas.

This is a lowland tree that can be planted from sea level up to 1,500m. This is a species for warm to hot subtropical and tropical climates. The monthly mean maximum temperature in its native areas is 100 – 330 C, but it is adapted to a wide range of temperatures. It is not frost hardy. Tree is fire sensitive and can be browsed only lightly without being damaged.

Characteristically sea-shore plant.


Availability :

Occurs wild on the shores at the head of the Bay of Bengal, Burma and Andamans. Chiefly found in Australia, Malaysia and the Pacific Islands.

In the coastal regions of the Indian Peninsula and occasionally also inland, as an avenue or ornamental tree.


Conservation/ Threat Status :

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Large, Evergreen, fast growing woody, with straight, cylindrical bole. Tree often buttressed at the base, having grooved stems and jointed verticillate i.e. whorled grooved branches. Foliage feathery, consisting of long, slender, drooping leafless branchelets, which are green and function like leaves. Leaves in whorls, scale like, linear or lanceolate, united at the base forming toothed sheath around the axis. Under favourable conditions tree attains a height of 100 ft. and more, and a girth of 5 ft.

Branches ridged and furrowed, verticillately arranged, green and photosynthetic. Photosynthetic tissue containing palisade cells located in the ridges of the branches below sclerenchymatous hypodermis. Sunken stomata situated in furrows protected by profuse hairy outgrowths.

Nitrogen fixing bacteria form nodules on roots.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Flowers unisexual. Simple, almost / completely perianthless in catkin inflorescence. Each flower at the axil of the bract surrounded by 2 lateral bracteoles.

Male flower: single stamen with rudimentary / vestigial perianth of 2 antero-posterior scales with lateral bracteoles, or sometimes perianth completely absent. 2 lateral bracteoles subtending the male flower. The unions of several bracts in verticillate rows on the terminal catkin-like spikes being invested by sheath like structure forms the male flowers. Anthers basifixed, dehisce by vertical slit.

Female flower: Flower naked, present at the axil of the bract, subtended by two lateral bracteoles. Flowers arranged in short catkin-like capitate spikes. Carpels 2, style 1, stigmas 2 and long, filamentous. Posterior carpel abortive and the anterior fertile. Ovary unilocular. Ovules usually 2, but only one matures to form seed, ascending and orthotropous.

Fruit one seeded, winged nut enclosed by hardened bracteolar covering. Endosperm absent. Embryo with large cotyledons.

Fertilization chalazogamous. Wind Pollination.

Floral and seed biology:

Natural regeneration of the plant is rare in plantations except in North Kanara. Entire transplanting.

For cultivation, seedlings are first raised in a nursery and transplanted in the field. The seeds after sowing are liable to be carried away by ants. For preventing this, a dilute copper sulphate solution or a decoction of Derris elliptica root is used for "watering" or treating the seeds; or wood ash is spread on seed beds. The times for sowing, pricking out and transplanting differ in different localities.

Transplanting is carried out 6-18 months after sowing, at the time of the SW monsoon on the west coast and at the time of commencement of NE monsoon on the east coast, in prepared 1 ft. cube pits. A spacing of 9 ft. each way is recommended in south India; the usual spacement is 6 or 7 ft. each way. In some plantations in Bombay, triangular planting with 12 ft. spacing is adopted. Where fuel production is the main object of cultivation, a moderate spacement, a reasonably short rotation, and no thinning, are recommended. But where, in addition to fuel, timber production is also aimed at, moderately close planting, a long rotation and intermediate thinning are practised. Adequate watering is needed immediately after transplantation, and also for 1-3 years after the plants are established.

 

MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:

Nursery seedlings are liable to attack by crickets, chiefly Brachytrupes achatinus. Fencing the nursery and clearing of vegetation 20 yards all round, daily digging out and destroying the crickets, are the usual remedies.

Other insect pests are, e.g., Arbela tetraonis, the bark-eating caterpillar, Coelosterma scabrata, a longicorn, and grubs of the rhinoceros beetle, Oryctes rhinoceros, also cause considerable damage to plantations.

Infection by the root fungus Trichosporium visiculosum is among the more serious diseases affecting Casuarina, and is favoured by excessive watering and congestion. Thinning , commenced early, checks it to some extent. As a preventive measure, trees infected by insects and fungi should be removed and the stumps grubbed up.

Keeping an interval of two years between felling and replanting, and planting other trees such as Azadirachta indica, Syzygium cumini, Dalbergia sissoo, Anacardium occidentale, Pongamia glabra, Pithecellobium dulce and Sapindus laurifolius along with Casuarina are recommended. The later practice assists in segregating the plants, thereby minimising the spread of infection. It also helps to attract insectivorous birds which are otherwise remarkably scarce in Casuarina plantations (Troup, III, 903-8; Raghavan, Indian For., 1947, 73, 241)

A symbiotic fungus, Phomopsis casuarinae F. Tassi has been recorded in all organs of Casuarina (Bose, Nature, 1947, 159, 512).

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts Used:

Fuel:Stem trunk and branches extensively used as fuelwood. Excellent fuel. Calorific value of wood 4950 cal, 8910 Btu. Burns readily, even when green and ashes retain heat for a long time. Claimed to be the best firewood in the world. Wood burns readily even when green, ashes retain heat for a long time.

It is used for both domestic and industrial fuel. In India, it fuels some railroad locomotives and in the State of Karnataka it is the major species planted for firewood.

Roots used for making charcoal. Wood burns with great heat. It is the best firewood in the world.

Wood:Sapwood pale brown, heartwood light to dark reddish brown. Often with irregular belts of darker colour towards centre. Turns dull brown with age. Strong and heavy. Average unit weight at 12 per cent moisture content 765 kg/m3. Specific gravity 0.88 – 0.95, wt. 56-61 lb/c ft. Not durable in exposed places and on ground. Moderately durable under cover or in contact with water.

Wood is also used for house posts, rafters, electric poles, mine props, masts of country craft. Also employed for lagging galleries, sledge, hammer shafts, oars, yokes and felloes of wheels. Wood very hard and heavy, sometimes used for club making, frames of cots, and poles for sustaining heavy weights. Timber is used for shingles, fencing.



Harvesting Practices:

Fuelwood: Casuarina Plantations worked under clear-felling system, with a rotation of 7-35 years. Some estimates showed that long rotation of 33 years gave the greatest volume production. Considered that shorter rotation of about 15 years is more profitable.

In Madras, the plantations are worked with a short rotation of 7-15 years (usually 10 years), while in North Kanara a 30-year rotation is followed. From a purely silvicultural consideration, the proper rotation appears to be 7 years.

In Madras, a yield of 40-80 tons per acre, for a rotation of 7-9 years has been obtained, the poorest areas yielding 40 tons per acre and a revenue of 500-750 rupees during the pre-war period. The proceeds from the pruned branches, and from the dead, dying and suppressed trees, generally cover the cost of raising seedlings, planting, watering, etc.

Other uses:

Bark of medicinal use.

Used for pulp for wrapping paper and core of duplex paper.

Bark contains 6-18% tannins so used for tanning purpose.

Needles used for preparing activated carbon by zinc chloride method.

Avenue or ornamental trees, wind breaks, checking erosion, afforestation of sandy beaches, as it is salt tolerant and can reproduce in sand.

Land Protection: It is useful for checking erosion, preventing a landward drift of sea sand and afforesting sandy beaches. Numerous stretches of land along the Indian coast have been reclaimed this way, where it is eventually possible to introduce other species. The growth of the plant is rapid. Under unfavourable conditions, however, stem becomes hollow and mis-shapen and it scarcely survives 25 years. It thrives best on loose sandy soils in proximity to the sea, in abundant sunlight. Heavy and close soils and also soils with poor drainage are detrimental to the plant. This is probably due to the fact that the activity of nitrogen fixing bacteria, which forms nodules on its roots, is inhibited in such soils.

Dye, tanning – The bark contains 6-18 per cent tannin and has been used extensively in Madagascar for tanning purposes. It penetrates the hide quickly and furnishes a fairly plump, plaint, and soft leather of pale reddish-brown colour.

Pulp – The wood has been found to make a useful pulp by use of neutral sulfite semichemical process.

Bark - Bark is a source of tannin.

Miscellaneous Information

 Natural regeneration rare except in North Kanara.

Pulp of the bark medicinally used in dressing up wounds, the decoction of bark used as medicine for diarrhea.

Tree can exhaust the moisture in the soil, lower the water table of the site, and restrict growth of a healthy understory, leaving the soil exposed.

Tree is a nitrogen fixer. Nitrogen fixing actiniomycete symbiont Frankia is found to be associated with roots.
Plant is good for hedging and topiary.
Plant has an ectomycorrhizal association. Mycorrhizal roots function as nutrient absorbing organ in plant.
