HABIT & HABITAT

Kittul is a lofty and some palm. The palm is considered to be indigenous to India, Srilanka and is met within large numbers in the moist foreign regions of the Western and Eastern coasts and in the coel and shady valleys of Chota Nagpur, Orissa, North Bengal and Assam upto 5000 ft. above sea level. It is commonly cultivated in gardens

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Kittul has a smooth, cylindrical, annulate trunk. It is 40-60 ft. high and 1 ½ ft. in diameter bearing large leaves, 18-20 ft. long and 10-12 ft. broad. The leaves are not grouped in a terminal crown as in the case of the great majority of palms, but spring successively from the trunk for a good distance below the summit. They are bipinnate with leaflets shaped like the tail of a fish .

Back to Top

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

The palm grows rapidly attaining its full height in 10-15 years, after which it begins to flower. It is monoecious. Flowers are borne on pendulous inflorescences, 10-20 ft. long appearing first at the axils of the leaves and later, with advancing age, continuing downwards till the palm is 20 or 25 years old, when it dies.

The fruits or nuts which vary in size from 0.5-1.0 inch in diameter are red to black in colour when ripe.
Ovule is anatrospous

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
fiber
Kittul fiber obtained from the fibro-vascular bundles found naked at the base of leaf sheaths, petioles, and flowering stalks is used for making ropes, bow strings, fishing lines and nets owing to its strength and durability.

Chief use of the fiber is in the manufacture of brushes, and brooms. Hair, cloth and horse brushes for polishing linen and cloth yarns and for cleaning scotched flax and velvets and made from it.

It is used as stiffner in admixture with bristle in brooms and gun brushes. Serves as a substitute for horchair and Oakum.

BEVERAGE

Unfermented toddy is sweet and refreshing and forms a healthful drink.

WOOD

The outer wood of the trunk is strong and durable and finds use for agricultural purposes, water conduits, tank pipes, buckets and rice powders. It is also employed for plough shafts and for rafters in house building. For purposes of timber, the trees should be utilized after the last flowering. It is said to be resistant to termite attacks. Trunk used for making drums.

Harvesting Practices:
fiber - The best fiber is said to be obtained when the palm begins to flower and the amount which can be obtained from on tree depends on its age and size. Each leaf sheath yields between a quarter and one pound of fiber and 2-4 leaves can be cut annually without damaging the palm. The fiber varies considerably in texture from very fine hair-like fibers to coarse strands about one eighth inch in diameter .

Toddy - The palm yields a plentiful supply of sweet toddy, from the time it begins to flower till it dies i.e., for a period of 4-7 years. The juice is tapped from the spadices before they are fully developed. The method of tapping is similar to that employed for tapping palmyra or coconut palm and consists in opening the spathe when the spadix has attained the proper stage of maturity. The spathes are trained to incline downwards so that the juice flows into pots suspended close to them. As many as 3-4 spadices may be taped from a single palm. The yield of juice varies according to the vigour and age of the palm. The yield of juice is continues and lasts for 3-6 months, the yield per day ranging from 4-12 quarters to as much as 18-20 quarts. A single palm may yield as much as 10 gallons per year.

Chemistry:
Todd - the juice is sweet and transparent when freshly tapped, but ferments into pole or cloudy, sour and intoxicating liquor when allowed to stand for 24 hours. The fresh juice contains 13.6% sucrose and a trace of reducing sugar while fermented juice or toddy contains 1% reducing sugar, 3-4.5% alcohol and 0.3% acetic acid

Processing Techniques:
fiber - After removal from the tree, the leaves are either soaked in water to remove the gummy mater or left dry for a day, and the fiber then picked out by hand from the leaf stalk. Before shipment it is hackled wet and then graded into three grades according to length by the shippers. After drying, it is bundled into hales so that the thickest parts of the strand are at one end the bundle is, therefore, sorted at that end and tapered at the other.

Before being used by brush makers the fiber is dressed, normally by fiber dressers. Dressing consists of cleaning, combining and arranging in long straight fibers, and in dyeing the fiber black with aniline dye or with linseed or rape oil. If dyed with aniline dye it is known as "water-dyed" and is this form is used for pitch-set brushes. If dyed with oil it has a better appearance and much more resembles bristle, and is more able to withstand water. Oil dyed latool is used in drawn brushes and brooms.

Toddy - To obtain sweet toddy without fermentation, the collecting pots are either coated with lime or smoothed before use. Sometimes powdered bark of Vateria acuminata or leaves of Acromychia laurifolia syn. Cyminosma pedunculata are added to the juice to check fermentation.

Sweet toddy is largely used for making jaggery or gur. For this purpose it is strained into open clay or copper pots and obtained over a slow wood fire. The juice is continuously stirred and the froth periodically removed. The boiling is continued with stirring till graining occurs, when it is allowed to cool for a few minutes and poured into moulds to set. A gallon of fresh juice yields 1.26 lb jaggery.

Sweet toddy which has undergone fermentative change is unsuitable for jaggery making. Such material can be boiled down to a thick syrup and preserved with sodium benzoate. When allowed to crystallize a candy known in Sri Lanka as Veli Hakuru is obtained which is a substitute for jam. Other products obtained from sweet toddy are arrack, vinegar and food yeast.

Sago - From the soft and starchy pit of the kittul palm is obtained Kittul flour or sago which is said to be equal in quality to the best sago of commerce obtained form Metroxylon sagu Rotf. For preparing the flour, the trees are cut down during the rainy season, when the palm is dormant, and the pith separated, cut into pieces and pounded. It is ten mixed with water and strained though cloth. The starchy matter which settles down is separated and dried.


Other uses:

Kittul flour is made into bread or boiled into gruel and is highly valued for its coding properties. Jaggery is extensively used in Sri Lanka as a sugar food.

Roots carbonized to give a charcoal preferred by silversmiths and goldsmiths. The cabbage or terminal bud of the palm is edible and is eaten raw with jaggery, or cooked as a vegetable or pickled.

Fruits or nuts are used for making buttons, by carving, painting and polishing. The pulp is acrid and causes irritation when applied to the skin. It is said to allay thirst and fatigue. The nut is used as an application to the head in cases of hemicrania.
