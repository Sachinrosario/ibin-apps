Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
A large deciduous tree having a wide natural range occuring wild in Greece, the balkans, through Asia Minor, the Caucas, Persia, Afghanisthan, the Himalaya, the Hills of upper Burma. In Himalaya it is indigenous usually at 4,500 to 11, 000 ft. It is more abundant in Kashmir and Hazara, also found in the hilly areas of Himachal pradesh, Punjab and Uttar pradesh, but not so widely distributed. Walnut requires a climate free from frost in spring and extreme heat in summer. A temperature of 2-30F below freezing point kills young flowers, if the summers are hot, affects the yield of nut. It thrives in the areas with an annual rainfall of 30 inch or more. Walnut occurs chiefly on deep well drained fertile often containing boulders, in sheltered situations such as moist ravine depressions on the hill sides. It avoids stiff badly drained soil (1).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A large, deciduous tree with tomentose shoots (12-20 m tall and rarely upto 60 m tall). Bark brown or grey, smooth, longitudinally fissured. Leaf-scars without prominent pubescent band on upper edge, leaves alternate, imparipinnate, 6-15 inch long, foetid, pinnate, without stipules, leaflets to ovate-lanceolate, acuminate, margin irregularly serrate, glabrescent above, pubescent and glandular beneath, flowers developing from dormant bud of previous season's growth. Walnut wood is greyish brown in colour, often figured or mottled with darker streaks (1, 2, 3).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
The flowers scented and are manocecious. Inflorescence is catkin, male catkin or staminate flowers in axillary, pendulous aments 5–15 cm long, singly or in pairs above the leaf scars on the previous years shoots, developing 1–4 million pollen grains each, flowers in axils of scales, with 2 bracteoles, perianth-segments 1–4, stamens 3–40. Female or pistillate flowers in clusters of 3–9 at the apex of young shoots, Pistillate flowers lack visible sepals and petals, and are small and green with bifid style. Develops as many as nuts, in some varieties not only terminal bud produces fruit, but all lateral buds on previous years growth also produces fruit, perianth 4-lobed (2, 3, 5).

Breeding System :
All Juglans spp are monoecious, bearing male and female reproductive organs on separate flowers on the same tree. Although J. regia is self-fertile, it is heterodichogamous, either protandrous or protogynous depending on cultivar. Hence it is a cross-pollinated plant. Thus, pollinizers often are planted which overlap better with the main cultivar, with combinations of a protandrous and a protogynous cultivar best (2).

Phenology :
In Himalayas the leaves fall during September, some trees become leafless by the end of this month, others retain until October, but by later half of the November all become leafless. The new leaves appear in March- April. The flowers appear with the new leaves (5).

Pollinators:
Walnuts are wind pollinated. Pollinizers should therefore be placed on the upwind side of the main cultivars in orchards. Pollen will carry about 250-300 ft in mature orchards, so the minimum pollinizer arrangement would be about every 10th row (cross-wind). However, higher ratios are normally used since pollinizing cultivars also bear high quality nuts (2).

Seed Biology :
The fruit is green drupe, ovoid or nearly globose or slightly ridged, 3.5–5 cm in diameter about 2 inch long with a very aromatic leathery pericarp, which splits irregularly when ripe. The enclosed nut is 2 valved, wrinkled and furrowed on the surface and variable in size, shape and thickness of the shell, usually 1-1.5 inch long, it is divided inside by two thin dissepiments into four incomplete cells, one dissepiment separating the two cotyledons, the other dividing them into two lobes (1).

Seedling Germination :
Germination is hypogeous. The nut splits slightly at the suture and the radicle emerges from its basal end. The cotyledonary petioles elongate and the stout taproot attains a considerable length before the young shoot develops. The latter elongates first by arching, soon becoming errect (1).

Cultivation:

Nuts for raising seedling plants should be collected from vigorous and high-yielding trees. Desirable characters to look for in the selection of nuts are the following: big size, bright brown shell of good cracking quality, and bright yellow kernel with the good taste and flavour. Nuts should be stored in a cool dry place or stratified after harvest till the following December. If the soil is ready for sowing, beds may be prepared and nuts sown with 2 inches deep immediately after harvest in row 1 ft. apart, the distance between sowing in the row being 1 ft. qua Germination starts in the beginning of March and seedlings are ready for transplanting in the second year.

Walnut is propagated in other countries by vegetative methods, the methods in use are: whip grafting, cleft grafting, inlay bark grafting and patch budding in early spring. In Kashmir patch budding and crown grafting have been tried and found successful, but these methods have not been adopted on any appreciable scale.

Cultural practices: Walnut is usually grown under rain fed conditions. Only during the first dry season after planting water is supplied to plants by growers. It has been found advantageous to irrigate the trees during dry weather. Irrigation helps growth and trees come into bearing early. Irrigation should he continued till the maturity of fruits as it reduces nut fall and favours filling of nuts. If the trees are irrigated one or two weeks before harvest, the husks are seen to open and remain on the trees, while clean nuts drop to the ground. In India walnut trees are not manured. On account of its deep root system, the trees forage over an extensive area and bear fairly good crop. The yields however, are less in comparison with those of manured trees and also, unmanured trees tend to become alternate bearing. It is a good practice to manure the trees every year and also apply nitrogenous and phosphatic fertilisers, the doses depending upon the age, size and bearing quality of trees and on the fertility of soil. No pruning is given to walnut trees. The trees should be trained on a single stem upto a height of 3-4 ft. and scaffold branches should be retained at proper places. Any crossing or surplus branches and diseased and dry twigs should be removed every year.

In traditional orchards, seedlings are usually planted at densities of 50-70 plants/acre initially, and thinned to 30-50 trees/acre (30x30 or 40x40). Pollinizer cultivars are planted in solid rows (cross-wind) at selected intervals. Hedgerow orchards of lateral bearing cultivars can be planted at higher densities (100 trees/acre) and do not require thinning of trees since regular hedging and topping maintains trees size (1, 2, 3, 4, 5).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

1. Aphids:

(a). Woolly apple aphid: Bark feeding, covered with white wool-like, waxy material. Found in colonies in above ground portions of tree and on roots in winter. Nymphs migrate up/down trunk.

Damage: Stunting caused by root galls. Honeydew and sooty mold problems.

(b).Green apple aphid: Infest terminal growth, sometimes fruit. Shiny, black and football shaped. Mature aphids on apple foliage have bright green abdomen with darker green lateral spots.

Damage: Retards tree and shoot growth. Honeydew and sooty mold problems
(c).Rosy apple aphid: New hatched are dark green on new growth. Mature cluster in curled leaves or fruit are purplish covered with a waxy powdery bloom.
Damage: Most damaging aphid. Fruit size reduced or misshapen. Severe leaf curling. Soap may kill predators.

(d).Mealy plum aphid: Pale green wingless adults with three dark longitudinal stripes on their backs. Bodies covered with a white mealy wax.
Damage: Stunted growth and honeydew with black sooty mold.

(e).Black cherry aphid: Aphid appears at petal fall. Large and shiny metallic black.
Damage: Curling and distortion of leaves, mainly on young trees.

(f).Green peach aphid: Light green, on adults a large dark blotch in middle of abdomen. Attack shoots all over tree (pear).
Damage: Stunted shoots and leaf curl. Main problem is direct feeding on fruit, producing honeydew and black fungus that russet fruit.

(g).Leaf curl plum aphid: Color varies green brownish to green or brownish yellow. Shiny with wax bloom.

Damage: Leaves curl tightly. If early in the season honeydew can cause fruit to crack.

(h).Walnut aphid or Duskyveined aphid: Both damage walnut trees. The Duskyveined feed in rows along the mid vein on the upper leaf surface. The walnut aphid is smaller and is scattered on the lower side of the leaves.
Damage: Reduced tree vigor. Sooty mold on honeydew turns husk black which increases chance of sunburn on exposed nuts. An introduced wasp has reduced walnut aphid to a minor pest.

Aphid control: Clean garden debris. Do not over fertilize. Strong jet of water. Restrict ant movement.


2. Codling Moth:

Damage: This insect is a serious pest of apple which also attacks early blooming walnuts but it is confined only to Ladakh region in India. Three generations of moth damage the fruits. First generation larvae enter the growing fruit through the blossom end and the damaged immature fruits drop down from the tree becoming breeding sites for naval orangeworm. Second and third generation larvae enter the nut through the side of the husk. The damaged nuts are unmarketable on harvest.

Control measures: Synthetic pheromone traps can be installed in the month of May. Diazinon or carbaryl (0.2%) should be sprayed to control the pest.


3. Naval Orangeworm (Paramyelois transitella)

Damage: In contrast to the presence of only one larva of codling moth inside an infected nut, several larvae are found in case of naval orangeworm infestation. First and second generation larvae attack damaged and mumified nuts during spring and summer but the third generation larvae cause serious damage to nuts on splitting of husks.

Control measures: The control measures include removal of mummy nuts, control of codling moth and walnut blight, and rapid harvest of the nuts after husk split. Nuts can also be fumigated.


4. Mites

(a). Brown mite: At first molt they are brown, eight legs. Adults are flattened with long front legs. 3 generations between February and June.
Damage: Seldom a pest, feeding causes chlorosis. Infestations localized.
Control: Predatory mite, horticultural oil. Insecticidal soap (test foliage first). Forceful water spray. Green lacewing.

(b).Web spinning Spider mite: Over wintering females are red or orange, found under rough bark, litter or weeds. During season color changes from yellow to green to black with dark spots. 8 to 10 generations per season.
Damage: Leaf stippling by sucking juice from leaf.
Control: Parasitic wasp, horticultural oil. Predatory mite, Insecticidal soap (test foliage first). Forceful water spray. Green lacewing.

(c).Citrus bud mite: Primary pest of coastal lemons. Very small, elongated, tapered at posterior end. Four legs at front end near mouth. Eggs laid in bud scales of recent growth.
Damage: Kills buds or causes rosette like growth of the subsequent foliage and distortion of flowers and fruit ("Martian lemon").
Control: Horticultural oil. Predatory mite, Insecticidal soap (test foliage first). Forceful water spray. Green lacewing.


5. Walnut husk fly (Rhagoletis cingulata)

Damage: The walnut husk fly causes damage to only mid and late season cultivars feeding on the fleshy tissues of the husk. The nutshell gets stained rendering the nut in shell unfit for sale. The stains cannot be removed through normal bleaching. The infested nuts do not split making harvest a problem. The continuance of fruits on the tree causes the build up and serious attack of naval orangeworm subsequently. An early infestation of husk fly damages the kernels which shrivel and turn brown.

Control measures: The fly can be controlled by timely spray of insecticides based upon catches on green sticky trap spheres.


6. Walnut weevil (Alcidodes porrectirostris)

Damage: The weevil has been recorded in all the walnut growing regions. Female weevils lay eggs on the fruits which on hatching bore deeper and feed on kernels causing premature fruit drop. After pupation. adult weevils emerge from furits and feed on petioles and tender shoots. The grub is more destructive because it kills new shoots up to the old wood. Adults are 9 to 12 mm long. black to brown in colour, whereas grubs are 12 to 16 mm long and creamy white in colour.

Control measures: The pest can be controlled by spraying carbaryl (0.2%) twice at an interval of 7 days commencing from the time of first attack.


7. Indian gypsy moth (Lymantria obfuscata)

Damage: The moth passes through only one generation in a year and overwinters in egg stage. On hatching in the month of March, the caterpillars feed on buds and leaves. Due to nocturnal habit, it hides during day under rocks, stones or clods of soil and attacks the foliage after sunset often resulting in complete defoliation.

Control measures: Many scientists have tried a number of control measures and the pest population was most effectively reduced by applying endosulfon (0.05%) and fenvalarate (0.03%).


8. Long horned beetle (Batocera horsfieldi)

Damage: The bark boring beetle is an important pest in India. The grub eats the inner side of the bark making zigzag tunnels and later reaches to the centre of heartwood. Infested trees do not die but becomes unfit for timber purpose. The bark is also eaten by adult but it is not of much consequence.

Control measures: The grubs and adults can be hand picked and mechanically destroyed by treating the holes with carbon bisulphide or chloroform- creosote.


9. Leopard moth (Zeuzera spp.)

Damage: The young caterpillars feed on inner bark, bore into shoots and branches and pupate inside them. The affected leaves and shoots wilt and die adversely affecting the growth of the tree. The moth completes a single life cycle in about two years. Fully grown caterpillars are 5-7 cm in length and pinkish red in colour whereas moths are white with beautifully designed wings.

Control measures: The pest should be controlled by inserting cotton-wool soaked in carbon bisulphide, paradichlorobenzene or ethyl acetate in the hole followed by sealing with mud.


10. Storage pests: Tribolium casteneum and Oryzaephuus sunnamensis were the commonest pests which could be controlled by fumigation carried out with methyl bromide (4, 6).

Diseases:

1. Powdery mildew: Superficial white powdery growth on leaves and shoots.
Damage: Stunting/distortion of young growth. Infected fruit stunted and russeted. Reduced fruit set.
Control: Sulphur, lime Sulphur. Baking soda solution, water spray, copper fungicide, soap, reduce nitrogen fertilizer.

2. Phytophthora root/crown rot:
Damages: Unthrifty trees for several years or trees collapse and die after first warm weather in spring. Kills young trees.
Control: Improve drainage, clear garden debris, no wet crowns, aeration of root area.

3. Armillaria: Oak Root Rot.
Damages: Infects and kills cambium tissues in major roots and the trunk near the ground. Symptoms above ground are undersize, discolored leaves that drop prematurely. Eventually the entire plant may die.
Control: Use resistant species, prepare soil - remove old roots, air dry the soil. Maintain proper irrigation.

4. Fusarium wilt:
Damage: Yellowing and death of fronds, often older fronds on one side first. Cutting into infected wood may reveal tissue has turned brown.
Control: Avoid injuring living tissue, Practice proper cultural care (avoid stress to plant), Avoid undecayed amendments and excessive fertilizer.

5. Brown Rot:

Damage: Blossom and twig blight phase begins with the death of young blossoms and their associated spurs and leaves. Twig forms small cankers. Gum exudes at base of infected flowers. Fungus mummifies fruit. Moderate temperatures during bloom favor the disease. Fruit covered with brownish white powder.
Control: Remove "mummies". Thin tree. Prune out damaged wood.

6. Bacterial canker:
Damage: In spring limb dieback with rough cankers and amber colored gum. The "sour sap" phase causes inner bark to brown, fermented and sour smelling. Frequently trees sucker from near ground level.
Control: Avoid damaging trunk. Prune out dead wood. Disinfect cuts. Avoid overhead watering. Apply copper sulphate

7. Phytophthora gummosis: Mainly in citrus. Sap oozing from small cracks.
Damage: Lesions spread around circumference of trunk, girdling tree. Decline may occur rapidly or occur over several years.
Control: Excise diseased bark, paint with copper.

8. Bacterial blast:
Damage: Mainly citrus. The same pathogen as bacterial canker (Pseudomonas syringae). Black lesions on leaf petiole and progress into leaf axil. Leaves wither, curl and drop. Most severe damage on south side of tree. Diseased areas covered with reddish brown scab, small black spots on fruit.
Control: Bordeaux ,before first rains.

9. Downy mildew:
Damage: Attacks green parts of vine particularly leaves.Lesions are yellowish, oily, or angular yellow reddish brown and limited by veins. Cottony growth at lesions. Berries appear grayish when infected and remain firm.
Control: Copper, Sulphur, bordeaux, destroy infected plant parts.

10. Phomopsis cane and leaf spot: Infection occurs when shoots begin to grow.
Damage: Tiny dark to brown spots with yellowish margins on leaf blades and veins. Show 3 to 4 weeks following rain. Light brown spots on clean berries becoming dark brown. Berries may shrivel and become mummified.
Control: Sulphur.

11. Measles:
Damage: Small chloritic interveinal areas which enlarge and dry out. Red grapes = red margins around dead areas. Symptoms worst during July - August. On berries small, round, dark spots bordered by a brown purple ring may occur.

12. Pierce's disease: Symptoms: Leaves become slightly yellow or red, leaf margins dry and die in concentric zones. Petioles (leaf stems) remain attached to cane. Wood on new canes mature irregularly producing patches of green surrounded by mature brown bark.
Damage: By mid - season fruit clusters on infected cane may wilt and dry. Cane and root dieback. Plants become progressively weaker. Hot weather accelerates symptoms. Young vines are more susceptible. Disease is spread by leaf hoppers.
Control: Remove vines infected more than one year.

13. Peach leaf curl:
Damage: Leaves produced in spring are thickened curled and colored red or yellow. Severely affected shoots die. Irregular reddish lesions are sometimes seen on fruit. Badly diseased leaves fall by early summer. Repeated infection weakens trees and kills branches. Disease favored by wet weather.
Control: Bordeaux mix, fixed copper, lime Sulphur.

14. Blossom blast
Damage: Buds fail to open, dry out and die. Depressed black spots on fruit and leaves. Infection seldom progresses past the base of spurs in the lower portion of the tree. Disease can severely reduce crop yield, spurs. Bacteria serve as nuclei for ice crystal formation that induce freeze damage.
Control: Bordeaux mix, fixed copper, streptomycin, terramycin.

15. Crown gall: Bacteria survive in gall tissue and in soil. They enter through wounds.
Damage: Most damage to young trees, which become stunted. Gall is soft and spongy. Older trees develop secondary wood rot.
Control: Remove infected plants, microbial biological control (agrobacterum radiobactor)

16. Needle cast: A fungal disease that affects developing needles.
Damage: Mottled yellow spots appear in spring. Spots change to reddish brown. Orange fruiting bodies of the fungus develop in May or June. Needles fall in July. Mature infected trees have a thin appearance.
Control: Remove fallen needles. Remove shade by pruning. Apply fungicide.

17. Sooty Mold:
Damage: Unsightly but harmless until it becomes so abundant as to prevent enough light from reaching leaves, causing light stress.
Control: Wash off with a stream of water, and control honeydew producing insects. (insecticidal soaps will control most, and help wash off the mold.).

18. Walnut blight: Bacterial disease that survives in buds, twigs lesions and old nuts. Spread by rain splash.
Damage: Nuts have hard, rough, sunken black areas. Nuts may drop prematurely or turn black and shrivel.
Control: No overhead water, no water during bloom.Prune for good air circulation. Spray with fixed copper (4, 6).

Economic Potential and Uses

The kernels yield 60-70% of drying oil. The oil is pale-greenish yellow or almost colourless with a pleasant odour and a nutty flavour. Walnut oil is used for artists oil colours, printing inks, varnishes and for making soap. The nuts can be used as a wood polish. Simply crack open the shell and rub the kernel into the wood to release the oils. Wipe off with a clean cloth. The dried fruit rind is used to paint doors, window frames etc (it probably protects the wood due to its tannin content).

For medical treatment purposes: The product may be used for improvement of metabolism processes. The walnut oil is a magnificent nutritious agent capable of recuperating the patients following the period of illness or undergone surgery. It may be applied for treatment of the patients, who suffer from atherosclerosis, ischemic disease, hypertension, and diabetes. It may be successfully used for treatment for chronic hepatites, gastric juice hyperacidity, enlargement of the thyroid, and also as laxative and vermifuge remedies. For many years, the walnut oil has been used for tuberculosis treatment. The oil from the seed is anthelmintic. It is also used in the treatment of menstrual problems (7, 8, 9).

For food purposes: An edible oil is obtained from the seed, it should not be stored for any length of time since it tends to go rancid quickly. The oil has a pleasant flavour and is used in salads or for cooking, as an ingredient of cold sauces, also, as a nourishing component in cooking of other meals.
For cosmetology purpose: Walnut oil can be used in:

  • Anti-wrinkle products for around the eyes

  • The walnut oil may be used for soothing of dry skin and for prevention of painful sunburn, as well as a wound-healing remedy.

  • Body oils & hygiene products

  • Massage oil

  • Lip balms

Uses

Trees are cultivated commercially for the production of Nuts. Nuts consumed fresh, roasted, or salted, used in confectioneries, cakes, ice cream pastries, and for flavouring. The shells may be used as antiskid agents for tires, blasting grit, and in the preparation of activated carbon. (Activated charcoal and fructose have recently been suggested to foil the alcohol "breathalizer.") Crushed leaves, or a decoction (contains juglone) used as insect repellant and as a tea. Outer fleshy part of fruit very rich in Vitamin C and produces a yellow dye. The dye is often used as a colouring and tonic for dark hair. Young fruits made into pickles, also used as fish poison. Twigs and leaves lopped for fodder in India. Decoction of leaves, bark, and husks used with alum for staining wool brown. The sap is tapped in spring and used to make a sugar. Bark of the tree and the fruit rind are dried and used as tooth cleaner. Wood hard, durable, close-grained, heavy, used for furniture making, gun-stocks, veener etc., Tree often grown as ornamental. The jam made of green walnuts at a stage of their milky ripeness. At this stage, the nuts have the highest content of vitamins amounting up to 3000 mg %. Due to higher content of vitamins the jam may be used as an additional source of vitamins, as well as for treatment for thyroid gland, especially in cases of endemic craw (8, 9).

Harvesting Practices
The tree starts to bear crop at the age of 8-10 years. Walnut fruits ripen in September-October. The husks split at the time of maturity and the nuts which fall to the ground are collected. Nut fall is hastened by shaking the branches with hand or with bamboo poles to the ends of which hooks are attached, shaking is repeated 2-3 times at intervals. The yield of nuts varies according to the age, size and variety of tree. Outer branches produce best fruit. A grown up tree of big size may yield 185 kg of nuts, but the average yield of tree can be taken as 37 kg/tree (3, 5).

Produce and Products

Fruits or Nuts are consumed fresh or roasted. Oil of industrial significance (Paint and soap making) is extracted from the seeds. Fruits, leaves and seeds are known to have wide range medical importance. Dye is obtained from the plants is used as a colouring material. Wood is used as timber.

Other Uses
The seeds are antilithic, diuretic and stimulant. They are used internally in the treatment of low back pain, frequent urination, weakness of both legs, chronic cough, asthma, constipation due to dryness or anaemia and stones in the urinary tract. Externally, they are made into a paste and applied as a poultice to areas of dermatitis and eczema. The leaves are alterative, anthelmintic, antiinflamatory, astringent and depurative. They are used internally the treatment of constipation, chronic coughs, asthma, diarrhoea, dyspepsia etc. The leaves are also used to treat skin ailments and purify the blood. They are considered to be specific in the treatment of strumous sores. The cotyledons are used in the treatment of cancer. Walnut has a long history of folk use in the treatment of cancer, some extracts from the plant have shown anticancer activity. Male inflorescences are made into a broth and used in the treatment of coughs and vertigo. The rind is anodyne and astringent. It is used in the treatment of diarrhoea and anaemia.The bark and rootbark are anthelmintic, astringent and detergent (9).

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

All most all parts of walnut are utilized in one way or the other but the fruit and timber have been put to maximum use. The fruit has excellent flavour and is mainly consumed as a dry fruit being eaten for table purposes. Its usefulness in cookery is highly acclaimed because it adds desirable aroma, flavour, crispness, tenderness or garnishment to dishes depending upon the preparation. Walnuts have traditionally been regarded as tidbits, a special treat to be added to candy, cakes, cookies, and salted specialities or to be served at special parties or dinners held at auspicious occasions. They also have unique place in confectioneries and a large number of recipes are available for using nutmeats in candy plants, restaurants and homes. Walnut shell flour is extensively used as an ingredient in plastic fillers, battery cases, moulding resin forms, industrial tile and as insecticide spreader. About half of the total production of the flour is consumed by plastic industry.

Export Potential

In India, Jammu and Kashmir is principal walnut growing state having monopoly in the production of export quality nuts. In 1984-85, area under walnut cultivation in the state was 32 149 hectares yielding 20 190 tonnes. The export of 10000 tonnes of nuts to different countries fetched nearly twenty crore rupees in foreign exchange. The walnut has a tremendous export potential provided international standards are maintained in production and marketing technology (4).
