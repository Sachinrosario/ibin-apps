Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

A shrub or small tree, originates in the central Sahara. Aggressive, hardy shrubs or trees of desert, steppe and sea-shore. Available in all districts of Punjab. Haryana, Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh and Gujrat.

Grow well where temperatures are high. Some species will tolerate frost in the winter. Survives where annual rainfall is a mere 100 mm. But the optimum growth is probably at 350-500 mm. It comes among the most salt-tolerant trees. They will regenerate in very adverse sites, even those with salt appearing on the surface. Grow well on sand or sandy soils.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

A shrub or small tree. Bark grey, rough. The slender ultimate branches hoary with a saline efflorescence from the impressed, punctate glands. Leaves reduced to a short sheath, the free part to a small triangular tooth.

Back to Top

 

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Flowers hermaphrodite, in slender, usually interrupted spikes. Bracts sheathing, acute, shorter than the flowers. Sepals 5, broadly elliptic, much shorter than the petals, entire or minutely toothed. Petals oblong, conniving at the top. Stamens 5, filaments filiform, inserted in alternate notches of the 10-lobed disk. Capsules trigonous, rounded at the tip. Styles 3, about half the length of the ovary. Stigmas ovoid.

Floral and seed biology:

Seedling Germination: Tamarisk seeds lose viability within a few days,so for all practical purposes trees are established exclusively by cuttings taken in spring from the previous year's growth. The process is easy and inexpensive. Long, well-rooted cuttings can be planted directly into the sand, even on moving dunes.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Wood used in Punjab as a cheap fuel. It is slow to catch fire, but burns reasonably well. It, however, gives an offensive smell when burnt green. Calorific value of sapwood 4,835 cal., 8,704 Btu. Also used for making charcoal.

Wood: Wood pale yellowish or greyish brown. Moderately hard to hard. Specific gravity 0.60 – 0.75 air dry. Straight grained, coarse textured. Not very durable.

When the wood is cut on the quarter, it presents a most attractive silver-grain which can be made use of in cabinet-work as small inlay panels. Wood used to make ploughs, Persian-wheels, framework of beds, and small ornaments. In Rajasthan, sometimes employed to make screws of mills and presses and for carts and house-building, common brush-backs and similar purposes. Mature wood suitable for carpentry and turnery. Also suitable for manufacture of particleboards.

Processing Techniques:

Requires careful seasoning. Logs should be converted when green, stacked and protected from direct rays of the sun and from hot wind.

Other uses:

Twigs used for making baskets.

The bark and the galls have been used in tanning and as a mordant in dying. Bark of medicinal use.

Green leaves and nutritious fruits can be fed to animals during lean months. (But due to the high content of salt in the plant, animals feeding on it require water more frequently.)

Plant exudes a gum and a manna, locally known as misrilei, is said to be collect.

Afforestation - It is valuable for erosion control, especially for stabilizing sand dunes, because it grows fast and resists being buried by shifting sand. It has an extensive root system and sheds twigs abundantly, forming a compact litter that improves the sand's water holding capacity.

Wind break - It is an outstanding species for use as shelterbelts in arid zones. Being evergreen, they are effective year-round.

Firebreaks - Salt drip from the tree kills all ground vegetation beneath the leaves and litter from the trees is too salinized to burn. Thus, strips of tamarisk can be grown to stop wildfires, a major hazard during hot, dry summers in arid areas. They will also halt the spread of fires along highways or railroads caused by sparks or cigarettes.

Miscellaneous Information

Tamarisk cause salinization of the upper soil layers (even on nonsaline soils). Their extensive roots extract all soluble salts from the soil, excrete the slats on the leaves and return them to the topsoil with litter fall. They commonly reduce the growth of nearby plants because of this and also because their roots collect moisture and plant nutrients over a large distance. The effect is significant when crops are grown close to shelterbelts or shade trees. They may, for example, reduce crop yields for up to 50 m.

Tamarisk transpire abundantly, and when their roots penetrate deeply they may cause great losses of otherwise useful groundwater. For this reason, they are held in low esteem in the western United States. In areas with shallow water tables, their deeply penetrating roots use up more groundwater than other phreatophytes tested in Arizona.

In one year a single tanarisk tree can produce more than 500,000 seeds. Most germinate, producing jungle-like growth, in less than 5 years.
