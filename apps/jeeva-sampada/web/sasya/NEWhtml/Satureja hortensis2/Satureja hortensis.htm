Taxonomic Details

Name : Satureja hortensis L.
Family : Lamiaceae
Classification :
 Class : Magnoliopsida 
 Subclass : Lamiidae
 Order : Lamiales

Synonyms : Satureja pachyphylla K. Koch (1843), S. laxiflora K. Koch (1849), 
Clinopodium hortense Kuntze (1891). 
Common Names: 
English : Savory, Summer Savory, Annual savory

Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

An erect pubescent annual, aromatic herb growing to a height of 25-35 cm. The genus consists of hardy annual or perennial aromatic herbs and sub-shrubs (1).

Climate and soil
Grows well in most subtropical and temperate climates on light, rich, well-drained soils and prefers alkaline soils (2).  

Availability :

Distributed in the warmer regions of both the hemispheres (1). Satureja hortensis is native to the eastern Mediterranean region and widely escaped from cultivation elsewhere. It was probably grown as a condiment in ancient Rome. Since then, it has been planted in Europe, Asia (the former Soviet Union, Afghanistan, India, Sri Lanka), South Africa and the Americas (United States, Argentina). In South-East Asia, it is cultivated in the Philippines on a small scale for the fresh-herb market and similarly in the mountains of Java (Indonesia) (2).


Conservation/ Threat Status : Wild and cultivated.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Plant is erect, pubescent, 25-35 cm height, with pinkish branches. Stem obtusely angular to subterete, without furrows and with short hairs. Leaves decussately opposite, sub-sesssile, oblong-linear or lanceolate, 1-4 cm x 1-5 mm with deep pitted glands on both sides, strongly aromatic (1).

Back to Top

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Inflorescence consisting of lax to densely congested verticellasters each with 2-20 bisexual flowers; very short pedicel; bracteoles longer than calyx, thinly long ciliate; calyx tubular and 2-lipped, 3-4.5 mm long, 10-veined, glabrous inside, thinly pilose outside, with five sub-equal triangular teeth; corolla tubular and 2-lipped, 4-10 mm long, tube widely funnel-shaped, lower lip 3-fid, upper lip emarginated, light violet to white, in the throat sometimes red-dotted, thinly pubescent outside. Stamens 4, curved, inserted in the upper half of the corolla tube under the upper lip, shorter than the corolla; pistil with a 4-lobed ovary and a style with 2 equal branches, each ending in a small stigma (2).  

Fruit
Fruit composed of 4 nutlets, enclosed within the persistent calyx. Nutlet sub-globose, about 1 mm long, smooth, glabrous and blackish-brown in colour (2).

Breeding system and varietal improvement: Through seedlings.

Phenology:
Flowering starts 75-80 days after germination. 

Pollinators:

Floral and seed biology: Seeds remain viable for 1-2 years and more if stored in air-tight containers (2).


CULTIVATIONASPECT:
Seed germination:
Germination is slow and takes 2-3 weeks.

Seedling development:
Initial development of seedling after germination is fast (2).

Related taxa:
Winter savory (S. montana Linn.) an evergreen, bushy and woody perennial shrub, is similar to S. hortensis in both properties and uses, with smaller pink or white flowers and a stronger flavour (3). Propagated by division or cuttings, in hills but from seeds in plains sown at same time of summer savory. Dies in plains after the commencement of rain. Flavour stronger than S. hortensis. 

Leaves contain ursolic acid, essential oil (0.15-0.23%) from fresh flowering herb is orange yellow with an aromatic flavour resembling origanum oil. Main constituents: carvacrol (28.65%), dipentene (14%), unidentified alcohol (10%), p-cymene (27%) (Chem. Abstr., 1953, 47, 8320; Guenther, III, 742; Parry, I, 257). Oil adultered with origanum oil and thyme oil (1).  

Chromosome number:
2n=46, 48.

Selection of planting material:
Seeds are used for planting.

Planting:
Sow the seeds in spring where the plants are to grow, in well-drained soil in full sun (3). Sowing rates range from 3-10 kg seeds/ha. They are sown 0.5-1.5 cm deep at a row distance of 25-30 cm. Adequate soil preparation is necessary before planting (2) .

Management after planting:
Thinned when plants are having 4-6 leaves. Fields must be kept weed-free and fertilizer application with adequate supply of potassium is recommended. Excessive fertilizer application leads to lodging during flowering stage (2).  


MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:

Leaf miners (Phytomyza horticola), caterpillars (Apterona crenullela, Arctia caja), aphids (Aphis fabae), cicads (Cicadella atropunctata) and beetles (Cassida viridis).

Diseases:
Mint rust (Puccinia menthae), an important disease of mints seen on the stems and leaves, but not as on mints. 
Damping off (Pythium sp.) occurs in young plants. 
False mildew (Perenospora spp.) and grey mould (Botrytis cinerea) (2).

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Summer savory, either fresh or dried, is mainly used for flavoring. For the fresh product, all aerial plant parts are used, but for the dried herb, only leaves and flowering tips. Its main traditional culinary use in Europe is as a flavouring for vegetables, especially beans and peas. In several countries (e. g. Germany, the Netherlands) the vernacular name is the local equivalent of ‘bean herb’. Summer savory blends well with other herbs. Alone or in combination with other herbs it is quite widely used to flavour baked products, meat and meat products (e. g. sausages), egg dishes, processed vegetables, salads, soups, gravies and pickles. Summer savory contains an essential oil with a sharp and bitter flavour. At low concentrations only it is reminiscent of the herb itself. The oil is used in the food industry to flavour processed foods, and in the liqueur and perfume industries. The maximum permitted level in food products is about 0.04%. Sometimes an oleoresin is produced, but in commercially insignificant amounts. Summer savory has also bee planted as bee-feeding plant (2). The aromatic leaves have warm bitter taste. Fresh or dried leaves are used for flavouring meat, egg and vegetable dishes and for other culinary purposes. Savory of commerce consists of dried leaves and the flowering tops, the best class comprising only leaves (1). Infusion of leaves treats gastric upsets, indigestion and loss of appetite. Tea is tonic. Spreading the flowering shoots between clothing repels moths (3).

Parts Used: Dried leaves and flowering tops.


Harvesting Practices: Tops of the plants are cut when they come to flower. Plants are cut at ground level or above the bottom branches (2).  

Post Harvest Handling
Tops and leaves are then dried in shade for further use (Gollan, 64). After drying, they are threshed, cleaned and then stored in closed containers to maintain colour and avoid loss of essential oil. Freeze dried savoury loses its flavour and odour when stored in glass jars or paper bags (2).


Produce and Products: Essential oil is obtained by steam distillation of the whole dried herb. Oleoresin is also obtained. 


Chemistry: Dried savory has a fragrant, aromatic odour, and a warm, aromatic, slightly sharp and somewhat camphoraceous taste. The odour and taste are sometimes likened to those of thyme (Thymus vulgaris L.). Fresh green herb (leaves and flowering tops), collected at the end of the flowering period, contains per 100 g: moisture 72 g, protein 4.2 g, fat 1.65 g, carbohydrates 11.5 g, fibre 8.6 g and ash 2.1 g. Dried leaves are reported to contain per 100 g edible portion: water 9.0 g, protein 6.7-7.7 g, fat 5.2-5.9 g, carbohydrates 53.4-54.6 g, fibre 15.3 g, ash 8.7-9.6 g (Ca 2132 mg, Fe 38 mg, Mg 377 mg, P 140 mg, K 1051 mg, Na 24 mg, Zn 4 mg), vitamin A (as β-carotene) 5130 UI, niacin 4 mg, and vitamin C 12 mg. The energy value is 1130-1500 kJ/100 g. In the United States the regulatory status ‘generally recognized as safe’ has been accorded to summer savory (GRAS 3012), summer savory oil (GRAS 3013) and summer savory oleoresin (GRAS 3014). Carvacrol (C10H14O) is a colourless to pale yellow liquid, with a pungent, thymol odour, imparting a fresh, slightly sharp, phenolic sensation. The main volatile components are γ-terpinene, p-cymene, α-pinene, α-terpinene and myrcene. The seeds contain 42% oil and 24% protein, linolenic acid (60-65%), linoleic acid (18-24%), oleic acid (4-12%), palmitic acid (2-4%) and stearic acid (1-3%). The weigth of 1000 seeds is 0.5-0.8 g (2).  

Pentosans constitute 11.95% of leaves on dry weight basis. Other substances in essential oil – labiatic acid, ursolic acid and β-sitosterol. Fresh leaves and stem contain 0.10 and 0.17% ursolic acid; leaves contain 0.4% ethereal oil; antioxidant property due to labiatic acid (Hill, 464; Muenscher & Rice, 130; Lowman, Fmrs’ Bull. U.S. Dep. Agric., No. 1977, 1946, 15; Winton & Winton, IV, 245; Manjanatha et al., Indian Perfum., 1964, 8, 73; Garber, Qualit., Plant. Mat. Veg., 1962, 9, 33; Chem. Abstr., 1959, 53, 9390; 1953, 47, 7164; 1962, 56, 13310).

It is a source of the ‘Oil of Savory’, which is a golden yellow with characteristic thyme odour. Constituents are: monocyclic terpene hydrocarbons, 64% of which is sylvestrene, 36% is carvacrol and traces of thymol. Oil is used for flavouring processed foods (Uphof, 472; Steinmetz, II, 410; Manjanatha et al. Indian Perfum., 1964, 8, 73; Chem. Abstr., 1957, 51, 14907; 1961, 55, 3726; Biol. Abstr., 1966, 47, 356). Seeds contain, on dry basis, 42% of fixed oil and 24% protein. Fatty acid composition: saturated (palmitic and stearic) – 4%; oleic –12%; linoleic – 18%; linolenic – 62%. Superior to linseed oil as a drying oil (Earle et al., J. Amer. Oil. Chem. Soc., 1960, 37, 48) (1).

Other uses: Medicinal uses
Summer savory is used in traditional medicine as a carminative, expectorant, astringent and tonic in the treatment of gastro-intestinal disorders (cramps, nausea, indigestion, diarrhoea), and soar throat, generally in the form of a tea. It has also been used as an aphrodisiac. Fresh summer savory is rubbed on the skin to treat insect bites, e. g. bee stings (2). Plant is anticatarrhal, antispasmodic, astringent, carminative, digestive, diuretic, laxative, stomachic, sudorfic and vermifuge. Used in menstrual suppression and flatulent colic and is a stimulating tonic after fever. Its juice heated with a little oil of roses is used as a remedy for ear troubles. A tea from fresh or dried leaves is an expectorant and carminative (Casius, J. Bombay, Nat. Hist. Soc., 1941, 42, 416; Wren, 276; Steinmetz, 1957, 1031). Oil of Savory has antifungal and antibacterial activities (1).
