Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Love-lies-bleeding (Amaranthus caudatus L.) are herbaceous annuals with simple leaves, and flowers in heads or spikes. The spikes are sometimes several centimetres long. They have dry red bracts that surround the flower which allows them to retain their freshness for some time. For this reason the plant is a symbol of immortality.

Love-lies-bleeding is a grain originating in South America, where it was also domesticated. In the past two centuries grain amaranth has been grown in scattered locations, including Mexico, Central America, India, Nepal, China, and Eastern Africa.
(1, 2, 6, 8, 9, 11)
Availability : 

Status :Cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A. caudatus is an annual plant of 0.4 to 3 m, height. It has a taproot, and very ramified side roots. The leaves are petiolate, oval, opposite or alternate and green or purple. The particulate, monoecious inflorescence ranges from erect to decumbent, with attractive colours—green, yellow, orange, pink, red, purple and brown. (1, 2, 6, 8, 9, 11)

The flowers are small, unisexual, staminate or pistillate; the males have three to five stamens and the females have a monospermous superior ovary. The fruit is in a pyxidium. (1, 2, 8, 9, 11)

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

 

Breeding System :

The cultivated species of grain amaranth are monoecious. Amaranth is a predominantly self-pollinated crop, with varying amounts of outcrossing. By growing amaranth in isolation, it is possible to control the amount of outcrossing for the development of true-to-type lines from segregating accessions within only a few generations of selection. The percentage of allogamy ranges between 10 and 50 percent, even within individuals of the same population. Dehiscence occurs at intervals and is a common characteristic among the wild species.

A. caudatus has a wide genetic variety and diversity of plant forms, ranging from erect to completely decumbent. It shows great variation in seed colour; earliness; protein content; types of ear; adaptation to soils, climates, precipitation and temperatures; disease resistance; and colouring content.

Genetic investigations have been conducted in India to learn more about the traits which control yield, including harvest index, yield per plant and weight per 1000 seeds. And the mode of inheritance of grain protein percentages has been studied. In general, there seem to be few cross ability barriers exist between crosses of A. cruentus L., A. hypochondriacus, L. and A. hybridus L. However, crosses with A. caudatus L. and any of the above species often result in non-viable progeny. However, in few accassions of A. caudatus L. crossed with two accessions of the Aztec grain type of A. hypochondriacus L. produced progeny that included segregates which were shorter and earlier maturing than the commonly available varieties of A. caudatus L. that are presently being grown in Peru.

There have also been some unique investigations to identify the mode of inheritance of traits that affect the nutritional characteristics of the grain. Work in Japan has focused on the identification of factors related to the unique starch characteristics of the grain.

(2, 6, 8, 9, 11)

Phenology :

Amaranths is generally grown during the summer and rainy season. They respond differently to various thermo and photoperiodic conditions, vegetative growth is put forth during long days and begin to flower as the days shorten. (2)

Pollinators:

Crossing depends on the wind, the number of pollinating insects, pollen production. ( 2)

Seed Biology :

The seeds are small (from 1 to 1.5 mm in diameter), generally white, smooth, shining, slightly flattened, although sometimes yellowish, golden, pink, red and black; and there are 1000 to 3000 seeds per gram. Seeds are dispersed over great distances from the parent plant. ( 2, 6, 8, 9, 11)

Seedling Germination :

Generally, the seeds do not exhibit dormancy and, as they contain moisture, they may even germinate on the plant. Amaranth seeds normally sprout within 24 hours at warmer temperatures, and the hypocotyl elongates enough within 72 hours for emergence from a 1 cm depth, which corresponds to typical field emergence of 3-4 days at shallow (1-1.5 cm) planting depths. ( 2, 6, 8, 9, 11)

Cultivation:

Precipitation: Water requirements range from 400 to 800 mm.

Temperature: It is sensitive to cold and can tolerate only 4°C in the branching state, with 35 to 40°C as the maximum temperature.

Soils: It prefers easily worked, sandy soils with a high nutrient content and good drainage, although it can adapt to a broad range. The ideal pH is 6 to 7.

Culture: Different forms and systems of cultivation are observed, including: direct sowing; transplanting, with irrigation or on dry land; growing together with maize; interplanted, to separate fields from other crops; as a border; sowing as a horticultural plant close to houses and plots on small farmsteads; and extensive cultivation.

It is traditionally sown under dry conditions, on pre-prepared ground, with a density of 4 to 6 kg of selected seed per hectare, in furrows spaced 80 cm apart and fed with a constant stream of water. When the plant reaches 20 to 25 cm, the first weeding is done, and also thinning if the seedlings are clustered together or need to be moved to spaces with a greater availability of water, Cultivation work consists of one or two weedings, light earthing up to prevent collapse from the weight of the inflorescences and control of the main pests and diseases. The plants are also sown in seed beds for subsequent transplanting to irrigated land.( 2, 6, 7, 8, 9, 11, 12)

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests: ( 2, 8)

  • Tarnished plant bug: The insect most likely to affect yields is the tarnished plant bug, (Lygus), a sucking insect which often reaches high populations in the seed head during the critical seed fill stage.

  • Flea beetle: Flea beetles damage young leaf tissue.

  • Aamaranth weevil: The adult amaranth weevil feeds on leaves, but the larval stage is more damaging because they bore into the central tissue of roots and occasionally stems, causing rotting and potentially lodging.

Diseases: ( 2, 8)

Damping-off of young seedlings can be a problem under some conditions, caused by Pythium and Rhizoctonia and stem canker, caused by Phorma or Rhizoctonia.

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used and Uses ( 2, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11)

Grain: Grain amaranth has been used for food by humans in a number of ways. The most common usage is to grind the grain into a flour for use in breads, noodles, pancakes, cereals, granola, cookies, or other flour-based products. It is clear that starch behavior is important in such applications. The grain can be popped like popcorn or flaked like oatmeal. The grain is also cooked whole and the grains are also used to make soups, custards. stews, desserts, drinks. bread and cakes. Agro-industry makes flour which is used up to 20 percent as a wheat substitute in breadmaking. It is also used to make an instant chocolate powder. syrups and sweets. Amaranthus grain is commonly high (around 15%) in a high-quality (favorable amino acid composition) protein which can be extracted and used in functional applications. The protein has advantages of good flavor profile and apparent hypoallergenicity. High protein and fiber content and low saturated fats in the grain has made its use by the health food market. Amaranth, like most grains, has potential for use in industrial products. Amaranthus oil, up to 8% of the grain, is a by-product of wet-milling for starch extraction, and can be used as a specialty food oil. The composition of Amaranthus oil varies widely, and high squalene content has been reported in some genotypes.The oil fraction of the grain is unusually high in squalene, a chemical that sells for thousands of dollars per ton. Various Amaranthus genotypes can be used for extraction of red pigments which have potential as natural dyes and food colorants.

Leaves:Amaranth as a forage. The leaves, stem and head are high in protein (15-24% on a dry matter basis). In areas where corn silage yields are low due to moisture limitations, grain amaranth may become a suitable silage alternative after further research. The leaves are eaten in salads.

Harvesting Practices

Harvesting takes place before the plant is fully mature so as to forestall seed fall. Harvesting is done when its lower leaves show signs of yellowing and there is some basal dehiscence and dry seeds. It consists of cutting the plants 20 cm from the soil with sickles and forming small sheaves which are left to dry above the furrows. To remove the seeds, they are beaten with sticks on sheets spread over the ground or on well-trodden earth and then sifted or winnowed to separate the grain from the chaff. Using this technique, farmers can obtain from 500 to 1500 kg per hectare. (2, 7, 8, 9)

Chemistry (2, 8)

Amaranth, like quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa), has very small, micro-crystalline starch granules, about one-tenth the diameter of maize (Zea maize) starch. The physical characteristics of the starch grains have been cited as being of potential value for both industrial and food product uses, though none has been commercialized to date.

The grain's nutritional value is high and can be as much as to 12 to 16 percent protein, while the balance of amino acids is very good. with a fair proportion of these containing sulphur: lysine, methionine and cysteine.

Processing Techniques (3, 5, 8)

The starches were isolated from grains by using an alkali wet-milling method.

Alkali process- In this process, the proteins in the amaranth grains are dispersed by an aqueous solution of caustic soda or ammonium hydroxide. The amaranth grains are crushed and sieved, the flour is slurried with an aqueous solution of ammonium hydroxide, or the grains themselves are directly soaked in a dilute aqueous solution of caustic soda in large vats. The alkaline slurry is screened in vibrating screens, the underflow is fed into a series of centrifuges where the separation of starch from the proteins occurs. The starch milk from the centrifuges is washed with water, filtered in vacuum filters and tunnel-dried. The protein solution from the centrifuges is dried in spray driers.

Wet-milling process-This process is universally adopted for the manufacture of all types of cereal starches. It makes possible a complete separation of the major components of the kernels.

The raw material comes to the wet-milling plants in the form of shelled grains. The grains are screened to remove all large and small pieces of grains, chaff, sand and other foreign matter.

The screened grains are steeped in steeping conical staves in which water and sulphur dioxide are sent in. Sulphur dioxide helps in the prevention of the growth of putrefactive organisms, dispersion of proteins and in maximizing the recovery of starch. During steeping, washwater from a previous operation, concentrated to attain a soluble level of 0.10-0.20 per cent, is sent counter-currently through the steeping staves. The steep water coming out of the staves contains 40-80 g/l of total solubles and is sent to concentration plants for preparing animal feeds, mixed feeds, or fermentation nutrients.

After steeping, the grains are crushed in attrition mills and the pulverized grains are sent through a set of hydroclones under pressure. The heavier endosperm and fibre particles pass out of the bottom of the hydroclones, while the lighter germ is drawn off the top of the vortex.

The underflow from the hydroclones is sized in screens and ground in Buhrstone or Entroleter impact mills. The pulverized material is passed through screen pumps where fibres are removed as continuous mats which are washed with water, slurried, squeezed and dried. The defibred pulp, known as mill starch, is concentrated in centrifuges where protein is removed as a overflow and starch is obtained from the underflow. The starch stream is passed through a series of hydroclones to reduce the protein content to the minimum level of 0.3 per cent. In the last hydroclone, the starch is washed with softened fresh water. The starch milk from the cyclones is dewatered on moving belt filters or in basket centrifuges, and dried in flash driers.

Other Uses  

Amaranth is used as a food for people with allergies to other grains. The seed of "grain" amaranth is not a grain from a cereal plant, but is rather a pseudocereal from a dicotyledonous plant. It is unrelated to any other food crops that are commonly consumed, which makes it less likely to cause problems to people who have built up allergies due to repeated consumption of the same foods. Recent studies have linked amaranth to reduction in cholesterol in laboratory animals.(8)

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country
Grain amaranth is a new crop that is in its adolescence. The cultivation and utilization of grain amaranth will continue to increase as more information is developed to exploit the market niches for high quality protein foods. The physical characteristics of the starch grains have been cited as being of potential value for both industrial and food product uses, though none has been commercialized to date.

An increase in production indices, followed by support for industrial processing in rural areas which would increase producers' incomes, would stimulate local consumption and if there was a surplus, exports or processed products is also possible. Markets are relatively small and undeveloped, in part because there is a general lack of familiarity with amaranth in the public and private sector. Special markets such as the starches or other seed components could lead to increased marketing opportunities. (2)

 

Export Potential
 Export of Amaranth starch has not been reported from India.
