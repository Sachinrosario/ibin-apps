 Other Biological Details
HABIT & HABITAT
A large deciduous tree, distributed in central and peninsular India, chiefly in mixed deciduous forests. It grows up to about 1100 m in Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh and sub-Himalayan tract. The tree in flower is a beautiful  sight. Occasionally  planted for its timber and gum.

This tree species is suited to arid and semi-arid regions with the rainfall ranging from 500 to 750mm.  This species requires to be grown in well-drained red soils which are light in texture, devoid of  lime and rich in iron.  It  grows better on well drained alluvial and sandy loam to loamy soils.  The tree is a moderate light demander.

Availability :  

For a long time the origin of kino was unknown. It has been ascertained to be the product of a lofty tree, growing upon the mountains of the Malabar coast of Hindustan, named Pterocarpus Marsupium, belonging to the Nat. Ord. Leguminosae. It has an erect, very high trunk, rarely straight.

Globally the species is distributed in India and Srilanka.  Within India it is found in the hilly regions throughout the Deccan Peninsuala (Maharashtra, Andhra pradesh, Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu) and extending to Rajasthan, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Bihar and Orissa.

The species is generally distributed in Dry and Moist Deciduous forest areas in plains of India, Nepal, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka.  It has a wide distribution.


Status : 


Rare in mixed deciduous forests and overexploited.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES


Large deciduous tree, up to 25 m tall ,bark thick, gray, longitudinally fissured, exfoliating in small irregular scales, red juice exudes from the bark on incisions. Leaves pinnate, leaflets 5-7, elliptic-oblong, coriaceous, glabrous, shining, base rounded to truncate, apex obtuse emarginate, margin entire, petiole 4 cm across, pedicel up to 3 cm.  pubescent, Flowers up to  6 cm, corolla golden-yellow, standard 1.2x1cm, wings 1.1x0.6 cm keels 9x6 mm; staminal sheath 4x6 mm, filaments 5 mm, stamens monoadelphous (10), becoming   diadelphous (9+1), ovary tomentose, 7 mm, style 6 mm, pod flat, 4 cm diameter, orbicular, stipitate, dark reddish brown, wings broad, seeds 1 or 2.

The heartwood is strong, tough, very hard and moderately heavy and is golden brown or reddish brown on exposure with darker streaks. Aqueous extract of wood is yellowish blue and fluorescent.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Breeding System :

Phenology :
Fl.& Fr.: June-December

In central India, flowering occurs from August to October and fruiting  from October to December.

In southern India, the tree flower from May to October and fruits from July to February.


Pollinators:

Seed Biology :


The pods can be collected by lopping the branches with ripe fruits or from the ground.  The pods must be dried properly as inadequately dried pods are susceptible for insect attack.  They can retain their viability for one to two years.

Seedling Germination :
The seeds are dibbled on raised or sunken beds and lightly covered with soil.  Germination starts within about 20days and is complete in about 45days.  Germination is about 60%.  The young seedlings can be pricked out from the seed beds and planted into polybags.  Shade should be provided during summer if needed.  No insect and fungus attack has been noticed in the nursery.

Direct sowing: The pods and dead leaves are placed alternately in a pit.  The pit is kept flooded with water.  After a few days the pods start germinating.  The germinated pods are picked up and sown.  Pods can also be soaked for 48hrs in cow dung slurry.  Soaking the clean seeds after extraction from pods prior to sowing reduces the germination period to a few days.  Seeds can be directly sown in June, or sown in polybags, or the entire pods are dibbled adopting a spacing of 10 x 10cm.   Germination percentage vary from 40 to 90 percent.

Cultivation:
The species prefers to grow on undulating areas, in moist sites with red loamy soil.

One year old seedlings can be transplanted to the main field, between June-July.  Plant establishment among transplants is low and is about 36 percent.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Heartwood, gum kino from stem, leaves, flowers

Uses

Kino occurs as small, angular, dark, brownish-red, shining pieces, brittle, in thin layers ruby-red and transparent, inodorous, very astringent and sweetish, tinging the saliva deep-red. Soluble in alcohol, nearly insoluble in ether, and only slightly soluble in cold water

  • The tree yields a gum, kino, exuding from the bark which contains 75% tannic acid.  The gum as it exudes has the appearance of red currant jelly, but hardens in a few hours after exposure to the air. Kino is the juice of the tree obtained by making longitudinal incisions in the bark; it flows abundantly, has a red color, and by drying in the sun, cracks into irregular, angular masses, which are then placed into wooden boxes for exportation.  
  • Kino gum is used in diarrhoea and dysentary. The gum is bitter, styptic, vulnerary, antipyretic, anthelmintic and liver tonic. It is useful in spasmodic gastralgia, vitiated conditions of pitta , boils, gleet, urethrorrhea, odontalgia, diarrhoea, psoriasis, wound and ulcers, helminthiasis, intermittent fever, hepatopathy and opthalmia.  
  • Kino is a pure and energetic astringent, and may be used to fulfil all the indications for which catechu is employed. It is not considered so efficient in chronic dysentery as catechu, but is preferred internally in menorrhagia, and as a topical application in leucorrhoea, relaxed sore throat, and aphthae of the mouth or fauces. An infusion thrown into the nostril has suppressed hemorrhage from the Schneiderian membrane; and the powder on lint has suppressed a hemorrhage from a wound in the palate
  • Wood also contains a colouring matter and yield an essential oil and a semi-drying fixed oil.
  •  Wood has application in dying, tanning and printing  industry and is of potential use for paper industry.  Bark-employed for dying. Contain a brownish-red substance which gives reddish colour to tassar silk.  


Harvesting Practices

Gum is collected usually in the dry weather by making a fish-bow type of blaze in the main truck of the tree. A vertical incision and several oblique ones running down into it are cut so as just to expose the wood. The kino flows out fairly quickly in a liquid form and collected in a bamboo –containers placed below the vertical incision.  

Trees over 1.8m girth are usually tapped.  A single tree may yield as much as 1.4kg of liquid gum or 0.45kg of dry gum.  The tree may be tapped on alternate sides, once in five years without damage.

Produce and Products
Paper, kino gum, dye

Chemistry
The bark contains 1-epicatechin and a reddish brown colouring matter. The heartwood yields flavones-liquiritigenin. Marsupol and other iso-flavonoids are present. The tree yields a gum, kino, exuding from the bark which contains 75% tannic acid; kino also contains a non-glucosidal tannin, kino tannic acid, kinonin and kinored in addition to a small quantity of catechol (polycatechin), protocatechic acid, resin, pectin and gallic acid. Carpusin is also present. Phytosterols, alkaloids and sesquiterpenes have been detected.

By the action of dilute mineral acids on aqueous solutions of kino, kino-tannic acid is precipitated; by boiling a solution of this acid for some time a red precipitate of kino-red is obtained.  By destructive distillation kino affords pyro-catechin, and when melted with caustic alkalies, protocatechuic acid and phloroglucin; thus yielding the same products as catechu.  By the action of ether a small quantity of a crystallin astringent principle can be separated and which is known as catechin. The tannin of kino, sometimes called mimotannic acid, or catechu tannic acid.  A red gummy matter is also a constituent of kino.

Kino burns without fusion or softening, with but little flame and frothing, leaving a scanty gray ash. Boiling water dissolves a large proportion of it, forming, when cold, a permanent, intense, blood-red solution, which yields with ferric chloride a dark-green, coarsely flocculent precipitate, which is so abundant as to render the whole liquid gelatinous. Acetate of lead produces a gray precipitate, and tartar emetic gradually a lake-red, muddy jelly. Cold water, which partly dissolves it, forms with it a clear, cherry-red solution, leaving a crumbly, grayish residuum. Alcohol dissolves about two-thirds of it, and forms a deep, brownish-red tincture, which is not disturbed by water. By long standing the tincture gelatinizes, and becomes less astringent. Proof-spirit is a less complete solvent, but the tincture is less apt to gelatinize. Its solubility in water is facilitated by alkalies, but its astringency is thereby lost, and its general characters changed.

The chief constituent of kino is kino-tannic (coccotannic) acid, which is present in the amount of from 45 to 55 per cent.  It is closely related to the tannin from catechu.  On continued boiling of an aqueous solution of kino or kino-tannic acid, an insoluble, red phlobaphene, kino-red, is precipitated. This substance is also formed gradually by prolonged exposure of solutions of kino at ordinary temperature

Processing Techniques

The kino is removed, before it has time to solidify, to the drying sheds where it is poured into shallow drying pans  and evaporated in the Sun to the consistency of a hard, dark mass. When sufficiently dry , it is crumbled into small fragments in which condition , it is packed in wooden boxes for marketing.

Other Uses
  1. Wood: for building purposes - doors, window frames, rafters, beams and posts and as a substitute for teak after treatment, railway carriages, wagons, carts, boats occasionally in ship, electric transmission poles, pit-props in mines, in making agricultural  implements, drums, tool-handles, camp furniture, mathematical instruments, picture frames, combs and parts of textile looms. Mixed with other woods , used for the manufacture of wrapping paper.  Aquous extract of wood given to diabetics.
  2. Leaves are an excellent foddar, also used as manure in areca nut plantation. Useful in boils, sores and skin diseases.
  3. Heartwood is astringent, bitter, acrid, cooling, antiinflammatory, union-promoter, depurative, urinary astringent, haemostatic, revulsive, anthelmintic, constipating, anodyne, atterant and rejuvenating. It is useful in vitiated coditions of kapha and pitta  elephantiasis, inflammations, fractures, bruises, leprosy, skin diseases, leucoderma, erysipelas, urethrorrhea, diabetes, rectalgia,rectitis, ophthalmopathy, haemorrhages, verminosis,diarrhoea, dysentery, odontalgia, gout, rhematoid, arthritis, cough, asthma, bronchitis and grayness of hair.
  4. Flowers are bitter, sweet, cooling, appetising and febrifuge and are useful in vitiated conditions of pitta , anorexia and fever.
