Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Mallotus philippensis is a small evergreen tree widely distributed in the sub-Himalayan tract and outer hills ascending up to an elevation of about 1,400 m and also in the central Indian peninsula, it is scarce in Andaman islands. It is a characteristic species of bhabar-Dun sal and western light alluvium plains sal forests and is one of the pioneer species coming after the clear felling of moist evergreen and semi ever- green forests. it is quite a common tree in sal and some mixed and scrub forests. It is a tree of subtropical climate. The maximum shade temperature varies from about 380 to 400C while the minimum temperature drops down to freezing point in North-west India. Frost is common in its natural habitat in North India. The annual rainfall varies from about 750 to 2,500 mm. This tree can grow on a variety of soils ranging from gravelly to clayey loam. It prefers moist deep alluvial soil for its optimum growth (6, 7, 8, 9).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
The tree is small, 2-18 m tall much branched and with a short and often fluted bole. The bark is thin, dark grey and red inside. Leaves alternate or sometimes opposite at apex of branches, subcoriaceous, ovate, oblong or ovate-lanceolate, 5-23 cm long, 3-6 cm wide, base rounded or truncate and above with brown maculate 2 to 4 glands, apex acuminate, margins subentire, glabrous adaxially, grey yellow-stellate-tomentellate and with sparsely red glandular granules; basilar veins 3, lateral veins 4-6; petioles 2-9 cm long, slightly thickened on both ends, stellate-hairy. The fruit is a three lobed capsule, globose, 3-valved, 0.75-1.25 cm diam and is densely covered with reddish brown glandular pubescence (1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 9, 10, 11, 19).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Flowers dioecious in terminal or axillary, solitary or clustered racemes; male or inflorescences 5-10 cm long; bracts ovate, with 1 mm long; bract-axils with 1-5 male flowers; pedicels 1-2 mm long; male flowers: calyx-lobes 3-4, oblong, with 2 mm long, densely stellate-hairy and with red glandular granules; stamens 15-30, connectives slightly broad. Staminate flowers 2-3 mm in diameter, green, slightly fragrant; elliptic to obovate, filaments 1.2-1.5 mm long, whitish to light green, anthers 0.5-0.7 mm long, light yellow. Female or Pistillate inflorescences up to 21 cm long, side branches up to 5.3 cm long; bracts triangular 0.5-1.0 mm long. Pistillate flowers 4 mm in diameter, yellow to red, scented; pedicel 0.4-3 mm long; sepals 3-6, ovate, 0.5-1.8 mm, yellow-green; ovary pubescent, ovary 2- or 3-locular, 1-1.2 mm pale light green with dark red dots, style 0.4-1 mm long, yellow, stigmas 3.3-5 mm long, stigma densely plumose-elevated, pale yellow to brown (1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 10, 11, 12, 13, 19).

Breeding System :
Flowers are dioecious in nature. Male and female flowers on separate tree, hence it is a cross-pollinated crop.

Phenology :
The tree is never quite leafless and the new leaves appear soon after the shedding of the old ones in winter. Flowering commences in July and the male trees are in full flower in October. The female flowers appear almost simultaneously. The fruits ripen from March to June depending upon the climate (6, 7, 9).

Pollinators:

Seed Biology :
The seed is globose, black, smooth, 3.5-4.4 mm in diameter and with a moderately thick testa. About 23-28 seeds weigh one gram. The seed does not retain fertility for long. Fresh seed should preferably be used. Seeds (containing not more than 6% moisture) can be stored in gunny bags or tins in a dry place without appreciable deterioration for 6 months (6, 7, 9).

Seedling Germination :
The seed germinates on being fully soaked after the commencement of monsoon rains. Germination is epigeous. The primary root is long, moderately thick, tapering and with numerous lateral roots and the stem is erect, woody, green and tomentose. Seedling growth is somewhat slow in the first season and comparatively faster during the second season. Under forest conditions, the seedlings attain a height of about 10 and 30 cm at the end of first and second growing season respectively. The seed fails to germinate in exposed locations where no shade is available (6, 7, 9).

Cultivation:

Natural reproduction is through seed and coppice. The seed falls in May-June and germinates during the following rainy season. The seed on the forest floor is damaged by insects and fungi. Only seeds that get covered under the soil will germinate. The seeds lying exposed on the ground generally fail to germinate and the radicles of those which germinate dry up before getting a hold in the soil. Seedling regeneration can establish only in areas where sufficient moisture is available and the tiny seedlings are not exposed to direct sunlight. Grazing and fire also destroy seedling regeneration. The tree exhibits good coppicing power and the coppice shoots grow much faster than the seedlings.


Mallotus philippensis can be raised either by direct sowing or by planting out nursery raised seedlings; stump planting gives better results than planting out entire plants.

Direct sowing is done in June-July in lines cleared of bushes. The lines are spaced about 3 m and the soil along them is well worked. The seed is only lightly covered. The seed requires good soaking before germination which commences in about five weeks and may continue for more than five months. The lines should be kept clean of weeds and bushes. Care should be taken to protect the seedlings against the livestock.

Nursery: Sowing is done in nursery beds in March-April where irrigation is available and in June under rain-fed conditions. The seeds are sown in lines about 20-25 cm apart. Putting the seeds in cooling boiled water and allowing to soak for 24 hours improves germination. The seed is only lightly covered with soil. The nursery beds are irrigated after sowing and regularly throughout the summer season. The nursery beds are kept shaded during summer months. Exposure of the seedlings to hot sun may kill them. The nursery beds should be kept clean of weeds as they may shade and kill the young seedlings. The seedlings are spaced about 10 cm in lines at the end of monsoon rains.

Planting: Planting is done in July-August. The seedlings are uprooted from the nursery with balls of earth. Planting is done in 30 cm3 pits dug in advance. Planting of stumps prepared from 2-3 years old seedlings is also successful. Spacing adopted is generally 3 X 3 m. Planting on exposed sites and along dry ridges having shallow soil should be avoided. Light shade helps in the establishment of seedlings, along with this care should be taken against grazing (6, 7, 8, 9).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

A large number of insect pests have been recorded on this tree. The larvae of several families, namely, Geometridae, Gelechiidae, Lymantrijdae, Lycaenidae, Noctuidae, Pyralidae and Lasiocampidae defoliate the tree. Beetle defoliators belonging to scarabacidae have been recorded. Larvae of family Gracilariidae mine the leaves. The insects of Coccidae family feed on the sap of foliage, twigs and stems. A large number of wood borers attack the standing, felled and converted trees (6, 7).

Diseases:

Several rot causing fungi attack this tree viz., Fomes conchatus, F. rimosus, Hexagonia discopoda, Polyporus adustus, Polystictus hirsutus, P. stemheihianus, Stereum hirsutum, Ganoderma applanatum, G. leucopheus and Trametes personii (6, 7).

Economic Potential and Uses

Oil obtained from the seeds of Mallotus philippensis is also called as Kamala seed oil. Seeds yield 35-36% of a viscous, dark brown to pale yellow oil or semi-solid fat depending upon the solvent used for the extraction. Kamala oil obtained by extraction with petroleum ether forms a good substitute for tung oil in the formulation of rapid-drying paints and varnishes. It can be employed in coating compositions giving either glossy or wrinkled finishes. The oil may be employed the formulation of hair fixers and ointments. Medium alkyd resins superior to some of the commercial alkyd resins have been obtained from kamala oil fatty acids. Kamlolenic acid and its hydrogenated derivatives have also been used in the preparation of macrocyclic compounds useful in perfumery, diaryl diketones useful as antioxidants or as high melting waxes. A stand oil named Friol, has been prepared from a 1: 4 mixture of kamala oil and linseed oil. Friol is more rapid drying than double-boiled linseed oil and forms films which have better gloss. It is an excellent vehicle for quick- drying paints, varnishes and wrinkle finishes. The seed cake left after the extraction of oil may be used as manure (6, 8, 12, 14).

Parts used

Fruits, seeds and leaves.

Uses

Kamala dye: Kamala was formerly employed extensively in the dyeing of silk and wool. The red pubescence is separated from ripe fruit by beating and shaking, it may also be obtained by stirring the fruits vigorously in water. Kamala dye available as a granular, reddish brown resinous powder, almost without odour and taste. Kamala may be employed as an anti-oxidant for ghee, vegetable oils and shortenings. It prevents the development of rancidity and in the case of ghee, retards the loss of vitamin A. It has been used for colouring stuffs and beverages. It is also used as a sindhur or kumkum by women in India.

The leaves are lopped for fodder particularly for sheep and goats. The oilcake in combination with saw dust can be used for making insulating boards and cork substitutes, the residual oil in the cake acts as a binder. Wood of kamala tree is whitish to light reddish grey. It is available in large supplies of small and irregular logs and used as fuel wood. It is suitable for rafters, tool handles, match boxes and small turnery articles, like bobbins, cotton reels, penholders and rluers (6, 8, 12, 14).

Harvesting Practices
The seed can be collected in April-May. Only ripe fruits should be collected. The seed should not be swept from the forest floor as it is often insect attacked. The fruits are dried in the sun, thrashed and winnowed to get clean seed (6, 7).

Produce and Products

From the pubescence of the fruit, red dye is obtained which is being used for colouring stuffs and bevereges. A fatty oil of industrial importance is extracted from the seeds and the seedcake is used as manure. The leaves of kamala tree are used as fodder. The leaves and bark contain tannin; the latter (tannin content, 6-10%) imparts a deep reddish colour to leather. The root is reported to yield a red dye. Wood is employed for local construction works.

Chemistry

The principal component acid of kamala oil is a solid hydroxy acid, kamlolenic acid (18-hydroxy-octa-deca 9, 11, 13-trienoic acid or 18-hydroxy a-elaeostearic acid). The major portion of kamala oil is composed of complex trigiycerides formed by the condensation of carboxyl groups of various constituent fatty acids with the hydroxyl groups of both glycerol and kamlolenic acid, the rest of the oil consists of simple triglycerides of the component fatty acids.

Processing Techniques

Oil can be extracted from the seed kernels by solvent extraction, owing to the high viscosity of the oil and its tendency to polymerize, direct expression of seeds in a hydraulic press or in an expeller does not yield oil, only a small proportion of the oil can be expelled in a hydraulic press at 70-80°. The oil can be extracted with light petroleum (b.p. 40-60°) which recovers 50% of the total oil in the seed; the residual oil can be extracted with benzene, ethyl ether or ethyl acetate. A high vacuum is used for stripping the solvent, as the oil polymerizes even at ordinary temperature. A process which uses linseed or other suitable vegetable oil for the extraction of kamala oil has been patented. The seed kernels are ground with twice the amount of vegetable oil to form a thin uniform paste, heated for an hour and filtered hot; the residue is treated a second time in the same manner. The two extracts are combined and cooled when kamala oil separates out as a solid fat and is recovered by centrifuging. The solvent oil retained by the cake is pressed out in a hydraulic press or an expeller (6, 8).

Other Uses
Kamala is largely used in India externally for cutaneous troubles, and is most effective for scabies. It has been successfully employed in herpetic ringworm, and possesses pronounced inhibitory action on the succinic dehydrogenase of tapeworm and is considered effective as a taenifuge. Kamala is used in external applications for parasitic affections and is reported to possess lithontriptic and styptic properties. Oral administration in the form of emulsion reduces fertility in female rats and guinea pigs. Alcoholic extracts of the fruit show anti-bacterial activity against Micrococcus pyogenes var. aureus. The kamala powder posses purgative properties and full doses occasionally causes nausea. The activity of the drug is attributed to rottlerin and isorottlerin. Kamala The drug is used also in veterinary practice. The root of the tree is used in cutaneous eruptions, also used by the Arabs internally for leprosy and in solution to remove freckles and pustules (6, 8, 12, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18).

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

Kamala tree has long been valued as the source of dyeing marerial, known in the trade as Kamala. Kamala Powder or Kamala dye and used in dyeing of silk and wool. More recently the oil extracted from the seeds has attracted considerable attention as a substitute for tung oil in the formulation of quick-drying paints and varnishes. The tree is not cultivated on a plantation scale any where at present, It is widely distributed in forest lands throughout India and the collection of ripe fruits is not organized. No reliable data exist on the production of kamala dye in various parts of the country. Such information on the availability of seeds is also incomplete (6).
