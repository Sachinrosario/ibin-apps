Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Fruits are used in preparation of cooling beverage ( Jain 1991;Arora & Pandey, 1996)
Fruits are used in preparation of a beverage which is popular as refreshing summer drink. (Anonymous, 1956)
Method of preparation: The popularity of ‘phalsa’ fruit is due to its attractive colour ranging from crimson red to dark purple and pleasing taste. The juice when extracted gives a deep crimson red colour, besides a pleasing flavour, which makes the juice very popular. It is rated very high in the indigenous system of medicine. Besides this, the juice is extremely refreshing and is considered to have a cooling effect especially in summer (Kirtikar and Basu, 1933), when it is used extensively both as a fresh fruit and also in the form of a home made syrup. Commercial processes like bottling of ‘phalsa’ juice or syrup, if developed, can ensure their ready sale prompting a considerably larger production of this fruit (Hays, 1960).

The colour in ‘phalsa’ fruit is due to water-soluble pigments viz. anthocyanins which lend an attractive colour to the juice.

Anand (1960) suggested the method of extraction of juice from ‘phalsa’ fruit, to disintegrate the fully ripe, sound fruit, in two third its weight of water in waring blender, heating the crushed mass quickly to 800 C, cooling it and finally pressing it through a piece of muslin cloth to get a clear crimson colur juice. The weight of the juice thus obtained approximately equals the weight of the fresh fruit.

Amba Dan (1973) reported a method of extraction of ‘phalsa’ juice. He suggested heating the crushed mass of ‘phalsa’ fruit to 60-700C for 10-15 minutes, which gives water-like thin and clear juice. Various heat treatments were employed to extract the maximum juice from ‘phalsa’ fruits. It was found that heating the crushed ‘phalsa’ fruit to 500C gave the highest recovery of the juice with an appropriate quantity of anthocyanins acids, sugars, minerals etc. (Khurdiya and Anand, 1981).

Produce and Products:


Chemistry: The juice in ‘phalsa’ fruits may vary from 55 to 85 % (Singh, 1967). Khurdiya (1979) reported 67.50 percent juice from the ‘phalsa’ fruits. Waskar (1985) reported 62.88 % juice from ‘phalsa’ fruits.

The carbohydrate content of the fruit has been found to vary from 6.88 % (Gangwar and Tripathi, 1972), 14.4 % (Gopalan et al, 1971), 23.74 % (Khurdiya, 1979) to 25.80 % (Waskar, 1985).

Khurdiya and Anand (1981) reported that the stablility of colour in ‘phalsa’ juice was highest at 30C followed by 200C and room temperature (31-360C). The organoleptic score of the juice remained acceptable for a period of 100 days at 30C, 10 days at room temperature.

Jain et al (1984) reported that ‘phalsa’ squash packed in glass bottles when stored at low temperature was superior to remaining treatments and maintained its natural colour, taste and flavour for a period of six months. ( Waskar et al., 1991)

A ready-to-serve (RTS) beverage containing 35% ‘Phalsa’ juice can be prepared by heating to 850C and filling, hot into clean, sterilized, colourless glass bottles (200 ml), sealed and cooled immediately in the air.

The extracted juice cannot be consumed as such due to its high acid content. The juice thus has to be converted into an RTS beverage for a better sugar/acid blend. An RTS beverage with a Brix/acid ratio of 25.0 was liked by a panel of seven judges as the best. This composition was subsequently used to carry out experiments on storage studies ( Khurdiya and Anand 1981). 

Processing Techniques: 

Preparation of beverages:

A ready-to-serve beverage from ‘phalsa’ fruit was formulated and standardized. It should contain 25 percent juice and brix-acid ratio as 25:1 (Khurdiya and Anand, 1981).

Amba Dan (1973) suggested a method of preparation of syrup from ‘phalsa’ fruit juice. After extraction from the fruit the clear juice be mixed with equal amount of sugar and preserved with sodium benzoate.
