Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

An aromatic, perennial, small sub-shrub. Hyssop is grown as a house-hold plant, not as a commercial crop. In Europe it is grown for its oil.

Availability :

Native of central and southern Europe and west Asia, it is also found in temperate regions (1). Distributed in the Himalayan region from Kashmir to Kumaon, and usually seen at higher elevations (2500-3500 m) of Himalaya and also in the temperate Europe and Asia. Five species occur in Europe; but only one species, H. officinalis is used.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES: A sub-shrub, 30-60 cm tall and 20-30 cm wide. Root mainly fibrous. Stem tough, woody, low down brown, green and angular higher up, branched. Leaves narrow, dark green, sessile oblong to lanceotate, pointed, 2.5 cm long and 2 mm wide, opposite. Leaves are somewhat bitter though mint-flaovured (1). Leaves and flowering tops have an aromatic odour and a warm pungent bitter taste.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR: Flowers produced in axillary tufts on the terminal branches, pale blue, pink or white, tubular, 2-lipped, about 6 mm long, in long narrow spikes (1).

Fruit
Fruits are dark brown, narrow and smooth (1).

CUTLIVATION ASPECT:
Selection of planting material:
It is propagated through seeds but can also be grown from cuttings and divisions.

Planting:
Hyssop comes up well in light rich soil and the hill stations of North India are suitable for their cultivation. Grows easily in sunny areas, dry and well-drained soil. Plant in spring about 30 cm apart, growing it from divisions, seed sown in spring, or tip cuttings taken before it flowers. Replant every 4-5 years (1) .

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Most important product of Hyssop is the volatile oil obtained by the steam distillation of the leaves and twigs. Fresh leaves sparingly in salads; with strongly flavoured meat, in soup, sausages, in fruit recipes; an ingredient of Chartreuse liquor (1). Fresh shoot contains around 0.3% essential oil. Oil is used as a flavouring agent, especially is the French liquors. It is also used as an ingredient in blended perfumes. The flowering tops and leaves of hyssop are also used as flavouring agents in such beverages as teas, tonics, and bitters, and to a limited extent in vegetable dishes, soups, salads, and candied products. Bees feed freely on the plant and the odour of the honey obtained from this source is remarkably good. The essential oil is widely used in, perfumes, soaps, creams, and other cosmetics (2).

Parts Used: Leaves


Harvesting Practices: For oil distillation plants are harvested just after flowering. 

Post Harvest Handling
They are dried in the shade and steam distilled. 


Produce and Products:


Chemistry: Major components are α-pinene, camphene, 1-pinocamphenol (about 50%). Hyssop’s fragrant flowers, stems and leaves yield, by steam distillation, a essential oil with an exceedingly fine, highly aromatic odour, much appreciated by perfumers, it's value being even greater than oil of lavender. Constituents of hyssop that contribute to its healing qualities include up to 2% volatile oils comprising mainly of pinocamphone, isopinocamphone, pinenes, camphene and terpenine; a glycoside called hyssopin; up to 8% tannins; flavonoids; isolic acid; oleonolic acid; a bitter principle called marrubiin; resin and gum as well as 50 other compounds (2).

Other uses: Medicinal uses
Fresh leaves, dried flowering tops, for cold, bronchitis, as a gargle for sore throats, encourage appetite, black eyes and swellings. Hyssop treatment – for bruises around the eyes, apply crushed leaves dipped in boiling water and apply as hot it can be tolerated, around the eyes until swelling subsides (1).  
Hyssop is used to relieve symptoms of influenza due to its expectorant and diaphoretic qualities, and as a carminative to relieve flatulence or a nervous or upset stomach. Typically it can be used to treat scalds, minor burns or bruises. It has a warming energy and a pungent, bitter taste, and is one of the 130 herbs used to flavour the liqueur Chartreuse. Traditionally, it was strewn on floors and shelves to repel insects, or added to pot pourri and laundry rinses. It has also been used to kill head lice and intestinal worms. It was once popularly combined with figs to treat constipation. As a medicinal plant, hyssop has also been used as a carminative, emmenagogue, stimulant, stomachic, and tonic. Leaves have been used as a poultice to remove discoloration around bruised eyes and as a remedy for asthma, rheumatism, sore throats, wounds, ulcers, and tumours. It admirably promotes expectoration, and in chronic catarrh it's diaphoretic and stimulant properties combine to render it of especial value. It is usually given as a warm infusion, taken frequently and mixed with Horehound. Hyssop tea is well adapted to improve the tone of a feeble stomach, being brewed with the green tops of the herb, which are sometimes boiled in soup to be given for asthma. Extracts of the herb have demonstrated antiviral activities, particularly against the Herpes simplex virus that causes cold sores. It's other properties include; sedative, pectoral, anthelmintic, antiasthmatic, anti-infectious, anti parasitic, peripheral vasodilator, anticatarrhal, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobal, decongestant, diueretic, mucolytic, nervine and sudorific. The essential oil increases alertness and is used as an uplifting and gently relaxing nerve tonic, suitable for nervous exhaustion linked with overwork and anxiety, or for depression. It is of particular benefit in helping to ease feelings of grief and guilt. Warm and vibrant, hyssop oil can promote alertness and clarity of thought. When used in a fragrancer it can protect rooms from infection. Hyssop blends well with camphor, celery, fennel, lavender, orange, rosemary and tangerine (2).
