Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

A perennial, aromatic, rhizomatous herb(3).

Climate and soil
Requires sunny or moderately shady locations. Soils should be fertile, moist but not swampy. Sandy clayey soils rich in organic matter and with a good drainage are preferred. Wild or semi-wild types occur in old clearings, thickets and forests. In the tropics galanga occurs up to an altitude of 1200 m.

Availability :

Exact origin is unknown. Oldest reports about its use and existence are from southern China and Java. At present cultivated in all South-East Asian countries and in India, Bangladesh, China and Surinam (1). Indigenous to India, shows exuberant growth and fine health along the eastern Himalayas and in south-west India, cultivated throughout the Western Ghats and also cultivated (3). India exports several varieties of galanga in different forms (2).


Conservation/ Threat Status : Wild/semi-wild and cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Robust, tillering, perennial herb up to 3.5 m tall, with subterranean, creeping, copiously branched rhizome. Rhizome subterete, 2-4 cm in diam., hard, fibrous, shiny, light red or pale yellow, fragrant. Pseudostem erect, formed by the rolled leaf sheaths. Leaves alternate, distichous, lowest and uppermost one smallest; sheath densely pubescent at apex, ligule truncate, 1 cm long, densely pubescent; petiole 1-1.5 cm long, hairy; blade oblong-lanceolate, (20-)50(-60) cm x (4-)9(-15) cm, base cuneate, apex with short point, subglabrous, glossy green, densely white-dotted.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Inflorescence terminal, erect, many flowered, racemose, 10-30 x 5-7 cm, pubescent; bracts ovate, up to 2 cm long, each subtending a cincinnus of 2-6 flowers; bracteoles similar to the bracts but smaller; flowers fragrant, 3-4 cm long, yellow-white; calyx tubular, about 1 cm long, white; corolla tube terete, about 1 cm long, lobes 3, recurved, oblong-lanceolate, 1.5 cm x 0.6 cm, margins ciliate, greenish-white, labellum (central staminode) petaloid, spatulate, 1.5-2.5 cm x 0.5-0.75 cm, white veined with lilac, undulate crenate with a stalk-like base and a recurved apex; lateral staminodes represented by 2 subulate lobes at the base of the labellum, 7-8 mm long, reddish; stamen-1, erect with incurved anther, 2-2.5 cm long; style slightly longer than stamen, stigma obtriangular. 

Fruit
Fruit a globose to ellipsoidal capsule, 1-1.5 cm in diameter, orange-red to wine red.

Breeding system and varietal improvement: Propagated by rhizomes.

Phenology:
In India flowering starts in the latter half of April-May and seed ripen in November.

Other botanical information
A. officinarum (Lesser galangal) can be distinguished from A. galanga as follows: its rhizomes are smaller and dark brown to black, 8-12 mm in diam., with 4-6 mm long, finely ridged internodes; the plant is smaller, about 1-1.5 m tall. 
Other species of Alpinia like A. calcarata, A. conchigera, A. mutica, A. nigra, A. rafflesiana Wall. apud Baker, and A. scabra Baker are sometimes substituted for this genuine drug (4). 

A. malaccensis Rosc. is a stout leafy herb, 1.8-3.0 m in height, with a perennial rootstock, frequently occurring in the terai region of eastern Himalayas in West Bengal, in Assam and Meghalaya, and in the cool and shady ravines of eastern ghats of Andhra Pradesh and in the Hassan district of Karnataka at altitudes of 900-1200 m. leaves oblong lanceolate, up to 90 cm long, narrowed and one-sided at the base; flowers white, with the lip variegated with deep red and yellow, in simple racemes; capsules globose, yellow, hairy; seeds ovoid. Rhizomes on steam distillation yield an aromatic oil (0.25%), containing methyl cinnamate (74-81%) and paraffin. Fresh leaves yield a light yellow essential oil (0.07-0.16%) containing d-α-pinene, β-pinene, cineol, methyl cinnamate and allocinnamic acid. In Indonesia, rhizomes employed to cure sores. Fruits used with salt as an emetic. 

A. nigra, a stout gregarious herb, 1.2-1.8 m in height, with tuberous rootstock and somewhat compressed stem, occurring commonly in wet places all over India, forming dense jungles in sub-Himalayan region. Rhizomes are aromatic and medicinal. Provide an inferior variety of galangal. Yields essential oil (0.05%) containing caryophyllene oxide (23.1%), geraniol (19.9%), eudesmol (19.4%), citronellyl acetate (16.5%), citronellol (6.8%), β-caryophyllene (5.5%), α-pinene (3.8%), linalool (2.9%), l-α-phellandrene (1.6%) and geranyl acetate (0.2%). Juice of rhizome is used in gout and colic (Ram Rao, 404; Chopra et al., 1956, 13; Chem Abstr, 1977, 86, 60388).

A. zerumbet (Pers.) Burtt & R.M. Smith. 
Syn. A. speciosa (Wendl.) K. Schum.; Zerumbet speciosum Wendl. (Fl. Br. Ind.) in part.
A handsome, rhizomatous, perennial herb, up to 3 m in height, occurring in the eastern Himalayas from West Bengal eastwards, and frequently cultivated in gardens for its foliage and showy flowers; also planted in hedges (4). 

Chromosome number:
2n=48

Selection of planting material:
Long tips of rhizomes are used for propagation.

Planting:
Soil should be well tilled before planting. Alternatively holes, 35 cm x 35 cm and 15-20 cm deep, are dug, filled with manure mixed with soil, inorganic fertilizers and lime (for acid soils). One piece of rhizome is planted per hole, and covered with mulch. Shoots from pieces of galanga rhizome emerge about 1 week after planting. About 4 weeks after planting 3-4 leaves develop. Rhizomes develop quickly and reach their best harvest quality in 3 months after planting. If left too longer, they become too fibrous and large clumps will hamper harvesting. Seeds rarely reach maturity. 

Management after planting:
Often trenches are dug to drain the field after rainfall, as rhizomes do not develop under waterlogged conditions. Usually planted along the borders of gardens, in rows at distances of 0.5-1 m square. Weeding and subsequent earthing up are carried out respectively 1-2 months after planting.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Principally used as a spice. Has strong pungent taste like a mixture of pepper and ginger. Its rhizomes are indispensable in everyday cooking throughout South Asia and South-East Asia. Flowers and young shoots are used as vegetables or spice. In Kerala, the rhizomes are used for seasoning fish and in pickling. They are also employed for insecticidal purposes especially against houseflies (4). Rhizome oleoresin (root extract) and rhizome essential oil (root oil) are used to flavour liqueurs, ice cream, pastry, etc. Fruits of galanga are used as locally substitute for true cardamom (1). Galangal oil is useful in respiratory troubles, especially those of children and is used as a flavouring agent in French liqueurs, in bitters of the angostura type and in some tobacco. It is used in Russia for flavouring vinegar and the liqueur 'nastoika': it is a favourite spice and medicine in Lithuania and Estonia. Tartars prepare a kind of tea that contains it, and it is used by brewers. In India, the oil is valued in perfumery (5). 

Parts Used: Dried rhizomes.

Harvesting Practices: Harvested 3 months after planting (during late summer or early autumn) for market purpose. Whole plants are pulled out, shoots cut off and rhizomes washed and cleaned. Rhizomes more than 4 months turn woody, fibrous and spongy and lose their value as spice. For local use plants are left in field, and as they tiller vigorously, small quantities of good quality rhizomes can always be harvested. For production of essential oil rhizomes are harvested when plants are more than 7 months old. No reliable data is present on the yield (1).

Post Harvest Handling
Harvested rhizomes are washed, trimmed, dried and marketed fresh or dried after packing (1). 

Produce and Products: Rhizomes are marketed fresh or dry. Dried product is ground before use. Ground rhizomes are not traded in bulk as they may be adulterated. Essential oil is also a product.


Chemistry: Composition of galanga rhizomes per 100 g dry matter is: moisture – 14g, total ash – 9g, matter soluble in 80% ethanol – 49g, matter soluble in water – 19g, total sugar 9g, total nitrogen – 3g, total protein – 16g. Essential oil content – 0.2-1.5% (dry wt.), fresh rhizomes yield about 0.1% of oil on steam distillation with a peculiar strong and spicy odour. Camphor, 1,8-cineole (20-30%), methyl cinnamate (48%) and probably d-pinene, are the oil components (old literature). 1’-acetoxychavicol acetate, a component of newly dried rhizomes is active against dermatophytes. Together with another compound i.e., 1’-acetoxyeugenol acetate, have anti-tumor activity in mice. The same compounds isolated from roots showed anti-ulcer activity in rats. Oil shows potential insecticide property. Galanga root, root oil and root oleoresin are given the regulatory status ‘generally regarded as safe’ (GRAS) in US. Galanga root (GRAS 2498), root essential oil (GRAS 2499), root oil (GRAS 2500)(1). The root contains a volatile oil (0.5 to 1.0%), resin, galangol, kaempferid, galangin and alpinin, starch, etc. The active principles are the volatile oil and acrid resin. Galangin has been obtained synthetically (5).

Other uses: Medicinal uses
Rhizomes are bitter, acrid, thermogenic, aromatic, nervine tonic, stimulant, revulsive, carminative, stomachic, disinfectant, aphrodisiac, expectorant, bronchodilator, febrifuge, anti-inflammtory and tonic (3). They have wide range of application in traditional medicine like skin diseases, indigestion, colic, dysentery, enlarged spleen, respiratory diseases, cancer of mouth and stomach, treatment for systemic infections and cholera, as an expectorant, after childbirth (1),in vitiated conditions of vāta and kapha, rheumatoid arthritis, inflammations, stomatopathy, pharyngopathy, cough, bronchitis, asthma, hiccough, dyspepsia, stomachalgia, obesity, cephalagia, diabetes, tubercular glands and intermittent fevers (3). Also used as aphrodisiac, for other stimulating properties and as veterinary medicine. Rhizomes showed antibacterial, antifungal, antiprotozoal and expectorant activities (1). Galangal's anti-bacterial effect acts against germs, such as streptococci, staphylococci and coliform bacteria. This plant is used to treat loss of appetite, upper abdominal pain, and sluggish digestion. It relieves spasms, combats inflammation and has stress reducing properties. In Asia, this herb is also used for arthritis, diabetes, stomach problems and difficulty swallowing. It is especially useful in flatulence, dyspepsia, nausea, vomiting and sickness of the stomach, being recommended as a remedy for sea-sickness. It tones up the tissues and is sometimes prescribed in fever. Galangal is used in cattle medicine, and the Arabs use it to make their horses fiery. It is included in several compound preparations, but is not now often employed alone. The reddish-brown powder is used as a snuff for catarrh (5).

Economic potential to the country

Export potential:
Data on production, consumption and trade are scarce and not reliable because of no distinction between A. galanga and A. officinarum Hance. India is a major supplier along with Thailand and Indonesia (1).  

Export Potential
However, its volatile oil attracts more international interest because of its high medicinal value (2).
