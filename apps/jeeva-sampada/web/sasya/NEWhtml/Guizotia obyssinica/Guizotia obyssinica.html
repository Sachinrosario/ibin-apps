Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Niger is herbaceous in nature and is said to be indigenous to tropical Africa and more specifically to Ethiopia. India is considered to be the chief Niger producing country in the world, with an area of 4.8 lakh hectares, and with an annual estimated production of 1.2 lakh tonnes of seed. It is disrtributed in the states of Madhya Pradesh, Bihar, Maharashtra, Orissa, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andhra pradesh, Arunachal pradesh, Gujrath, Rajasthan and Uttar pradesh, of which the largest area of 2.38 lakh hectares is in Madhya Pradesh (1).

A moderate rainfall of 1,000-1,250 mm suits this crop and, as such, it is not cultivated in regions of heavy rainfall. Light red soils and brownish loams with sufficient depth and good texture are considered suitable for this crop. It is often grown on poor soils of coarse texture. It can be grown on well drained.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A branched annual herb, 0.5-1.5 m tall. Young stems glandular-hairy. Leaves mostly opposite, but upper leaves sometimes alternate, sessile, clasping stems, lanceolate, serrate or linear, oblong, serrate , somewhat scabrous, 5-20 x 1-3 cm, tip acuminate. The plant is a stout, erect, stem is smooth, leafy annual herb (9). Capitulum borne on leafy shoots in leaf axils, 2-5 together. Leaves involucre with ovate, biseriate scales, flowers yellow, conspicuous, in solitary or clustered heads to 2 cm across, arranged in corymbs.

Inflorescence is called as capitulum, which is 1.5-2.5 cm in diameter. The capitulum is peduncled, axillary and terminal heterogamous. Ray-florets are pistillate, seriate, fertile; ligule 2-3 toothed and yellow. Disc florets are hermaphrodite, fertile, limb campanulate. Florets yellow, calyx reduced to a short rim of scales, stamens 5 epipetalous with syngenesious anthers, ovary inferior, bicarpellary, single celled and single ovuled with basal placentation. Style is bifid at the end and achenes are glabrous, dorsally compressed, tip rounded, pappus absent (3). Outer involucral bracts 5, leaf-like, ovate, hairy, about 1 cm long, inner bracts lanceolate, chaffy, peripheral ray florets about 8 in number, shortly ligulate, female, about 15 x 6 mm, several-nerved, 3-4 lobed. Corolla of both disc and ray florets yellow and hairy outside at base (9).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Back to Top

 

Breeding System :
Niger is completely cross-pollinated crop with lot of variation in the mode of branching, shape of leaves, colour of stem and time of flowering. Cross-pollination takes place through insects and wind.

Phenology :
Niger is grown as kharif crop and is sown in the months of June, July and August. The plants flower in about 45 days after sowing. Flowers are yellow in colour.

Pollinators:
Insects and wind are the major sources of pollinating agents.

Back to Top

Seed Biology :
Niger is propagated through seeds. Seeds are achenes, shining black, angled structure, quite glabrous, about 12 mm in length. The seed bears a fairly thick adherent seed coat. For rising the crop in one hectare, 7-8 kg of seeds are required. Before sowing soak the seeds in water for six hours and dry them under shade to bring back to original moisture content. This will help in uniform germination and better root growth. Achenes 3.5-5 mm long, 3-4-angled, black, shiny, broadening upwards, pappus absent.

Seedling Germination :
Seed can be stored for a year or more without deterioration. Germination begins 2 days after sowing. In about 7 days plants should be thinned to stand about 13–24 per 30 cm square.

Cultivation:

Niger is grown either as a pure crop or is grown mixed with minor millets. Two to three ploughings are enough to prepare the land for sowing. The field is made weed-free and levelled by planking before sowing. The crop is sown from June to August, either behind a country-plough or with a four-coultered drill. A spacing of 30 cm between lines and that of 10-15 cm between plants in the line are generally adopted. A seed-rate of 7-8 kg per hectare is adequate to maintain a good crop-stand and a pure crop. About a fortnight after sowing, thinning is done to regulate spacing between plants. One or two hoeings are required for controlling weeds. In general, the Niger crop is not manured. But the application of 20 kg of N and 20 kg of P2O5 per hectare is recommended for obtaining higher yields of the crop. When sown as a mixed crop, Niger shares the manuring given to the main crop (1).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

1. Niger caterpillar (Perigaea capensis)

Symptoms: Feed on leaves and defoliates the plants.

Control measures: Spray the crop with 0.07% Endosulfan @ 500 litres/ha.


2. Tobacco caterpiller (Spodoptera litura)

Symptoms: Caterpillars feed on the green leaves.

Control measures: Spray the crop with 0.07% Endosulfan @ 500 litres/ha.


Diseases:

1. Alternaria leaf spot (Alternaria spp)

Symptoms: Small brown circular spots are formed on the leaves and later increase in size and number. The infected leaves turn yellow and drop immaturely.

Control measures: Spray 0.3 % Zineb two times.


2.Powdery mildew (Oidium spp)

Symptoms: A powdery weft of mycelium develops on the surface of leaves.

Control measures: Spray 0.3 % karathane once at the initiation of the disease, again 15 days later.


3. Root rot (Rhizoctonia bataticola)

Symptoms: Roots turn black and rot. Infected plants suddenly wilt and die.

Control measures: Treat the seeds with 0.2 % Ceresan or 0.3 % Thiram before sowing (1).

Economic Potential and Uses

The seeds yield a yellow, edible, semi-drying oil with little odour and a pleasant nutty taste. It is used for as an illuminant and for soap manufacture. It is used to a limited extent in paints, for which the Ethiopian seed is superior to the Indian as it has a higher linoleic acid content. It is used as a substitute for olive oil, can be mixed with linseed oil, and is used as an adulterant for rape oil, sesame oil etc (11, 12). The crop is grown for its seed, which has oil, about 37 to 43 per cent of the seed weight. The oil is used for culinary purposes, for anointing the body, for lighting and for manufacturing cosmetics. The oilcake is a well-known cattle feed. The crop sheds a large quantity of dry leaves in the field and thereby adds organic matter to the soil (1).

Uses

The seeds are fried and eaten in India and are used in chutneys and condiments. Seeds can also be used fried or as a condiment. Whole plants are used as green manure in the pre-flowering stage. Seed is commonly used as food for cage birds. Plants are used as a bee plant (13).

Harvesting Practices
The crop matures in November and December. It should be allowed to stand until flowers have withered. Further delay will cause heavy loss of seed through shedding. Crop can be harvested by hand or machine. When harvested by hand, crop is cut with sickles, tied in bundles and dried by the sun for a week or so, during which time some of the late-formed seed will mature. Threshing consists of beating dried stalks with sticks on a threshing floor. Seeds are easily separated then they are cleaned of all earth and weed seed by winnowing and sieving. Seeds are bagged and shipped to oil factories (13). The average yield of Niger is about 200 kg per ha, but under favourable conditions and with best management practices it yields up to 600 kg.

Processing Techniques

PRE-TREATMENT

The crops are dried in the sun for 2-3 days, bundled and taken to the yard or are threshed in the field. The seeds are removed by threshing. The simplest method is by hand which does little damage to the seed, but is time consuming. In Ethiopia, oxen are sometimes used to tread out the seeds or to pull small threshing sledges. Small pedal operated threshers, similar to those used in S.E. Asia for rice are also used. After threshing, the seeds are cleaned by winnowing and sieving. Clean Niger seed can easily be stored in 200 litre oil drums fitted with lids (15).

OIL EXTRACTION

  • In India niger seed is crushed cold by village ghanis, rotatary mills, expellers and hydraulic presses.

  • In Europe the extraction is made by cold or hot pressing or by combination of both

  • In Ethiopia oil extraction is by roasting seeds, crushing them in a mortar and then treating the meal with water (2).

The expressed oil is pale yellow, odourless and has a nutty taste. Due to the high linoleic acid content it has poor keeping properties.
