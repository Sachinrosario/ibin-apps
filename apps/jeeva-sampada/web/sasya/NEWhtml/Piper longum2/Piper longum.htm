Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Piper longum, a slender aromatic climber, popularly known as Indian long pepper or tippali is generally used for is several medicinal properties. The economic part is the dry spikes of female types. Long pepper is also known and popular in parts of Africa, mostly in Islamic regions of North and East Africa. It can be found in the complex spice mixtures of Morocco. Probably came to Europe much before the now dominant black pepper. Indian long pepper is mostly derived from the wild type (8).

Climate and soil
Requires high rainfall, high humidity with an optimum elevation range of 100-1000 m MSL and partial shade of about 20-25 percent for ideal growth (6). It thrives in a variety of soils. Successfully cultivated in fertile, light, porous and well-drained forest soils rich in organic matter. Laterite soils with high organic matter content and water holding capacity, lime stone soil and well drained fertile black cotton soil is also suitable for cultivation (2).  

Availability :

It is native of Indo-Malayan region. It is growing wild in the tropical rainforests of India, Nepal, Indonesia, Malaysia, Sri Lanka, Rhio, Timor and Philippines. It is considered to be indigenous in the wet and warmer parts of India, grows in small scattered areas mainly in Assam, Khasi hills, lower hills of West Bengal, eastern Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Andaman and Nicobar islands and also in the evergreen forests of Western Ghats of Kerala, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu (4).

Conservation/ Threat Status : Wild and cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES: A slender, perennial creeping undershrub, dioecious. Vegetative branches creeps and spreads on the ground, fruiting branches erect, young branches puberulous, hairs minute, multicelluar, dioecious, older branches totally glabrous; leaves distinctly dimorphic, those on creeping shoot cordate, glabrous, petiole very long, grooved; leaves approx. 7x5 cm, petioles 2-3 cm. Leaves on the fruiting branches oblong, lanceolate, dark green with short petiole, base unequally cordate, with pronounced auricle, tip acuminate, 3-4 pairs of lateral ribs arise right from the base, lower side puberulous or downy when young, petioles very short or even absent (1).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR: Flowering is throughout the year; flowers are dioecious. Inflorescence is a cylindrical spike, erect, about 2-4 cm long, creamy white to yellowish white when young, with unisexual, small or minute achlamydeous densely or closely packed flowers forming small clusters of grey berries, peduncle about 1-1.5 cm long, downy; male larger and slender, about 6-10 cm long, yellow on maturity with longer stalks (2.5 to 7.5 cm), ovoid, yellowish orange, sunken in fleshy spike. Bracts peltate, orbicular, glabrous, pedicelled. Female spikes are with short thick stalk varying from 1.5 to 2.5 cm length and 0.5 to 0.7 cm thickness, sharper, sessile and blunt at the tips, erect and red when ripe. Flowers are unisexual, numerous, laterally fused and closely packed on the spike borne on separate male and female plants. Reopened female spike is the part of commerce. Female spikes arising singly from leaf axil, is cylindrical, short and stout with multiple fruit. Male spikes also arise from the base of the leaf, is single, long cylindrical and of no economic value. Male flowers – minute, perianth none; stamens –2, with short broad filaments. Female flowers – perianth none, stamens none, ovary obovate, sunk with single erect ovule, style absent, stigma 3-4 lobed, short, pappillate. Spikes on ripening turn green to black (1).

Fruit
Fruit is small ovoid drupe, about 2 mm diameter, embedded in the fleshy spike, fused laterally, pungent, aromatic, spicy, shining dark green when immature and blackish green when fully mature.

Breeding system and varietal improvement: Propagated by layering of mature branches or by suckers planted at the beginning of the rainy season. 

Phenology:
Flowering and fruiting: Flowers throughout the year.

Pollinators: Wind and insects

Floral and seed biology: Seeds are small.

CULTIVATION ASPECT:

Released varieties:
Viswam is the only released variety in the country so far, developed by Kerala Agricultural University through clonal selection and is recommended to grow as intercrop in irrigated coconut and arecanut gardens. 

Chromosome number:
P. longum = 24, 26, 48, 52, 60, 96.

Selection of planting material:
Suckers or rooted vine cuttings of 15-20 cm long with 3-5 cm nodes are taken as planting material (6). 100% establishment of cuttings observed (AICRP M & AP) similar to suckers.

Planting:
March to April is the best time for raising nursery. Field needs 2-3 ploughings followed by one or two harrowings and leveling considering the slope of land to facilitate drainage of excess of water. The crop cannot survive in water logging condition. Raised beds of size 3 m x 2.5 m should be dug with pits at 60 cm x 60 cm distance. Rooted cuttings/suckers are planted in each pit. Planting of long pepper is done on the onset of monsoon in the month of May-June in West Bengal and Kerala. Terminal shoots are usually used for planting. Rooting takes about 15-20 days after planting. Cuttings can be directly planted in the field or rooting can be initiated in the nursery before they are transplanted in the field. Cuttings can be easily rooted in pot mixture and planted in polythene bags and irrigated in alternate days. They will be ready for planting by the end of May (in two months). Planting is done in such a way that three to four nodes remain inside the soil and two nodes kept above the soil line. Hard wood cuttings of Sesbania grandiflora or Erythrina varigata or both are planted near the sprouted cuttings of long pepper for providing support and shade. In south India, it is also successfully cultivated as an intercrop in irrigated coconut and arecanut gardens.
Manuring: Requires heavy manuring. 20 t cowdung/FYM/ha is applied in pits at time of field planting (5).  

Management after planting:
Sprouts start usually within 15-20 days of planting. 
Irrigation: Utmost essential during summer months, 1 or 2 irrigations in a week depending upon the water holding capacity of the soil. Even in the monsoon period if there is a failure of rain for quite sometime, irrigation has to be given. In irrigated crop, fruit production continues even in summer months.

Manuring: Heavy organic manuring (20-25 t/ha/year) as split application will give a good yield during the economic period of three years. During the first year, organic manure can be applied in pits at the time of planting. In subsequent years, manuring is done by spreading in beds and covering with soil (6). About 20 t/ha FYM or any other organic manure is applied at the time of land preparation. In the subsequent years also a similar quantity of FYM or organic manure is to be applied before the onset of monsoon. No chemical fertilizer has been recommended so far in this crop.

Intercultural operations: During the first year regular intercultural operations have to be done to prevent weed growth. Generally two or three weedings are sufficient. Thereafter the weeds can be removed at the time of manuring. When the crop covers the broad interspaces at the time of manuring the weeds can be removed and manure can be spreaded in heads and earthed up. Once the crop grows and covers the field, no serious problem of weed is noticed (5).  

Berebere, a classical Ethiopian spice mixture, almost resembling Indian masalas is used to spice mutton dishes – really a hot mixture, the main ingredient of which is the long pepper.


MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
Mealy bugs – Insect attacks healthy roots and sucks its sap.
Symptoms: Infected roots show stunted growth and yellowing. 
Control: Application of systemic insecticide Rogar is recommended. Severe attack of Helopeltis theivora – Feeds on tender foliage. (Abraham 1990). 
Control: Application of neem kernel suspension at 0.25%.

Diseases:
Phytophthora leaf rot, stem rot and anthracnose are important diseases of long pepper. Control: Spraying of 0.5% Bordeaux mixture at 15 days interval and soil drenching of 1.0% Bordeaux mixture at monthly interval reduce the loss caused by these diseases effectively.
Necrotic spot and blight of leaves – Caused by Colletotrichum sp. and Cercospora sp. in summer months which sometime causes total or partial crop loss. 
Control: Bordeaux mixture (1%) spray during May and subsequently during rainy season.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Properties and uses
Fruits are used as spice in foods, beverages, in pickles, preserves, liquors and medicines. They have a pungent pepper-like taste and produce salivation and numbness of the mouth. In Chota Nagpur the root is used to ferment rice beer. In Andaman Islands, the leaves are chewed like betel leaves (1). Its hot and sweet taste goes well with spicy cheese specialties or wine sauces. In India, the long pepper is mainly used in pickles. It can be found in the complex spice mixture of Morocco. It is also of importance for the cuisine of Ethiopia, where long pepper is usually found in the traditional meat stews together with black pepper, nutmeg, clove and turmeric.

Parts Used: Dried spike and root.


Harvesting Practices: Long pepper takes 5-6 months for bearing. Spikes can be harvested after two months (in January) since its formation while they are blackish green and unripe, as it is the most pungent stage (6). After the third year the vines become less productive and should be replaced (Ridley, 314-15) (1). Harvesting over matured or ripened fruits reduce the quality of the produce as well as it does not break easily after full drying. In Kerala, 3-4 pickings are made depending upon the maturity of the fruits. Yield of dry fruits in first year is about 100-150 kg/ha and it attains up to 750-1000 kg/ha in the third and fourth year. Thereafter, yield starts declining and gradually becomes uneconomic after fifth year. Therefore, it is usually cultivated as a 4 to 5 year crop. Besides fruits, roots and thicker basal stem portions are also collected before crop is abandoned. These are cut into small pieces of 3.0-5.0 cm long and dried. On an average about 500 kg roots are obtained per hectare (3).  

Post Harvest Handling
Drying: Harvested mature unripened fruits are dried in the sun. It takes about 4-5 days for drying and fully dried fruit breaks easily. Fresh and dry fruit ratio is about 5:1. Dried fruits are stored in moisture proof container (6). Produce should not be stored for more than a year. Thicker parts of stem and roots are dried for making piplamool and graded depending on the size of roots and stems.


Produce and Products: Piplamul: Dried Roots and thicker parts of stem, used as an important ingredient in Ayurvedic and Unani systems. For roots it is grown as a perennial in small plots of 25-50 cents and the roots are collected for 10-30 years, the first harvest commencing from 8 months after planting. Stems close to the ground are cut and the roots dug up, cleaned and heaped in shade for a day, after which they are cut into pieces of 2.5-5 cm long. On an average 500 kg of roots are obtained per hectare. The produce is graded and packed in gunnies (Parthasarathy & Narasimha Rao, Andhra agric. J., 1954, 1, 299). 

There are three grades of Piplamul, Grade I with thick roots and underground stems fetching higher price than Grade II or III, which comprise either thin roots, stems or broken fragments. Commercial drug consists almost entirely of transversely cut pieces (length 5-25 mm, diam. 2-7 mm), which are cylindrical, straight or slightly curved, and some with distinct, swollen internodes showing a number of leaf and rootlet scars. Surface of the pieces is dirty light brown in colour (1).


Chemistry: Fruits contain volatile oil, resin, alkaloids (4-5% piperine), a terpenoid substance, piplartine (m.p. 124-125˚) and two liquid alkaloids. The first alkaloid is closely related to pellitorine producing marked salivation, numbness and a tingling sensation of mucous membranes of the mouth. It showed in vitro antitubercular activity against Mycobacterium tuberculosis H-37 Rv strain; inhibited the growth of the bacillus in 20μg/ml concentrations. Sesamin (C20H18O6, m.p. 122˚), dihydrostigmasterol and piplasterol are also present (Atal & Ojha, Econ. Bot., 1965, 19, 157; Indian J. Pharm., 1966, 28, 80; Information from ICMR, New Delhi) (1). On the other hand, long pepper contains less essential oil than its relatives (about 1%), which consists of sesquiterpene hydrocarbons and ethers (bisabolene, beta-caryophyllene, beta-caryophyllene oxide, each 10 to 20%; alpha-zingiberene, 5%), and, surprizingly, saturated aliphatic hydrocarbons: 18% pentadecane, 7% tridecane, 6% heptadecane (7). Medicinal properties are attributed to the alkaloid piperine and piplartine.

A sample of dried fruit of P. longum on steam distillation gave 0.7 % of essential oil with spicy odour resembling that of pepper and ginger oils, and has the following characteristics: acid val. 7.2; sap. val. 8.9; sap. val. after acetylation 12.8; and sol. in 20 vol. of 95 % alcohol. The oil contained: n-hexadecane – 0.7; n-heptadecane – 6.0; n-octadecane – 5.3; n-nonadecane – 5.8; n-eicosane – 4.7; n-heneicosane – 2.5; α-thujene – 1.7; terpinolene – 1.3; zingiberene – 7.0; p-cymene – 1.3; p-methoxy acetophenone – trace; and a monocyclic sesquiterpene (Handa et al., Parfum. u. Kosmetik, 1963, 44, 233) (1).  

Chemistry of piplamool
The drug has a peculiar odour and a pungent bitter taste producing numbness on the tongue. It contains piperine (0.15-0.18 %), piplartine (0.13-0.20 %) and traces of yellow crystalline pungent alkaloid. Other constituents include triacontane, dihydrostigmasterol, a sterol, reducing sugars and glycosides. Two alkaloids piperlongumine and piperlonguminine have also been identified in the roots (Parthasarathy & Narasimha Rao, loc. cit.; Atal & Ojha, loc. cit.) (1).

Other uses: Medicinal uses
Pippali is widely used in Ayurvedic and Unani system of medicines in Indian System of Medicines. Whole spike and piplamool (dried roots and thick stem) are used. Unripe fruit is used as an alternative, analgesic for muscular pains and inflammations, vermifuge, carminative, sedative, anti-diarrhoeic, anti-dysenteric against fevers, leprosy jaundice and as an immunostimulant and tonic; used after childbirth to check post-partum haemorrhage, treat respiratory tract diseases (6). The fruits are also used as carminative, sedative in insomnia and epilepsy, as general tonic and haematinic, as cholagogue in obstruction of bile duct and gall bladder; as an enumenagogue and abortifacient; as anthelmintic and in dysentery and leprosy. Ripe fruit is sweetish, pungent, heating, stomachic, aphrodisiac, alternative, laxative and anti-dysenteric (1).  

Pungent root is considered as heating, stomachic, laxative, anthelmintic, carminative, improves the appetite, useful in bronchitis, abdominal pains, diseases of the spleen, tumour, ascites and causes biliousness. the roots and stems are used for diseases of respiratory tract like cough, bronchitis, asthma etc., as counter-irritant and analgesic when applied locally for muscular pains and inflammation; as snuff in coma and drowsiness. 

A decoction of immature fruits and roots in chronic bronchitis, cough and cold. Fruits and roots are used in palsy, gout, rheumatism, lumbago, an antidote for snake-bite and scorpion sting, as counter-irritant and analgesic when applied locally for muscular pains and inflammation; internally as carminative; as sedative in insomnia and epilepsy; as general tonic and haematinic; and for miscellaneous purposes as anthelmintic, in dysentery and leprosy (Atal & Ojha, loc. cit.). It forms one of the ingredients in various compound preparations used for anorexia, piles, dyspepsia and also in snuffs used in coma and drowsiness (Chopra et al., 1954; Wealth of India, 1969). A compound preparation of P. longum is also said to be a good remedy for leucoderma (Prasad, 1966). The plant is considered by tribals (Santals) to be useful in splenetic disorders, cholera, dysentery, consumption, puerperal fever and diarrhoea (Jain & Tarafder, 1970) (4).
