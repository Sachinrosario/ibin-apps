Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT                                                                       
An annual herb with ellipsoidal tubers, occurring wild on the tableland of Mahabaleshwar and a few other neighbouring areas in Bombay State of India and extending downwards on the west coast.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

Tubers of the size of an orange, sometimes larger, with white flesh inside ; stem leafy, 3-4 ft. high with oblong-lanceolate leaves, 12-20 in. long and 3-4 in. broad ; flowers yellow, borne on a central spike, which usually possesses a long peduncle.

Harvesting Practices
Fully developed tubers contain a higher concentration of starch than immature ones ; the starch content is higher in tubers harvested during February-March than in those harvested during the monsoon period. For ensuring high starch yields, only fully developed tubers should be harvested, necessitating a two-year rotation.

Processing Techniques
For the preparation of starch, harvested tubers are washed to remove adhering soil and fibrous roots are chopped off. Cleaned tubers are then graded, in hand operated wooden rollers and the graded material collected in a trough, through which water is continuously flowing. The starch is washed, sieved through cloth and washed again ; the process of sieving and washing is repeated 3-4 times. The starch is then spread evenly on clean sheets and dried in the sun. An average yield of 13.6% (on fresh wt.) of starch, of which c. 8% is of superior quality has been reported. The product is devoid of taste or colour and is suitable for human consumption; it compares favorably with imported arrowroot starch. An inferior product obtained by two washings is suitable for making adhesives and for sizing textile fibers.
The tubers have been occasionally utilized for the manufacture of starch. During World War II, when arrowroot starch was scarce or unavailable, the Forest Department, Bombay, leased out H. caulina areas to a few firms for the manufacture of starch (Information from the Chief Conservator of Forests, Bombay).

Other Uses

The plant bears abundant foliage, and the possibility of utilizing the leaves for paper-making has been suggested.

The tubers are rich in starch (10.9-18.3% on fresh weight) and constitute a valuable raw material for starch manufacture.
