HABIT & HABITAT:                                                                               
It is small to moderate-sized tree, rarely up to 20 inch in height and 1-5 inch in girth, found along almost the entire coasts of India and the Andaman Islands. But generally it is found in the back waters, Creek, Mangrove forest, Mangrove swamps, Midlands, along Rivers and Streams, Salt marsh along coast, Salt Marshes, Swamps, Tidal creeks and Tidal swamps along the coast.

BioticZone: Coastal, Konkan plain and West Coast.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES                                                                        
Bark dark brown or black, shining, lenticellatc; leaves elliptic-oblong to obovate, 5.0-10.0 cm. x 2.5-5.0 cm., somewhat fleshy; flowers solitary, large, dark rose-coloured; berry cushion-shaped, 5-6 cm. in diam., dark green, seated on enlarged persistent calyx and tipped by the tapering style; seeds many.

The tree occurs in creeks and mangrove swamps Hooded by the tide and like most oilier trees found in such situations, is surrounded at the base by peg-like breathing roots or pneuniatophores. These are up to 60 cm. in height and 7-5 cm. in diameter and have a soft, firm and spongy texture. After shaping them and boiling in water, the pneumatophores have been used as a substitute for cork or pith as floats for nets, in linings of insect-boxes, for inner soles of shoes and for making models and artificial flowers.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used: 
Fruits, Stem bark and twig bark.

Uses: 

  1. Fruits when ripe, have a cheese-like taste and are eaten raw or cooked. When young or unripe, they are sour and are used for flavoring curries and chutneys by the Malays; they are also reported to be used for pre- paring vinegar.

Chemistry
Fruits yield 11% pectin (ZMB). Wood yields 52.7% brown pulp (8.5% lignin, 17.6% pentosan). Emodin and chrysophanic acid may be the coloring matter in the crude drug (Perry, 1980). Bark from Africa assayed at 17.1% tannin, of the pyrogallol class. Indian stem bark assayed 9–17%, twig bark 11-12%. Wood yields two coloring principles, archin (C15H10O5) and archinin (C15H14O12 )

Other Uses
  1. Reported to be used as a vermifuge and the juice of unripe fruit is given in cough. 
  2. Fruits yield 11 per cent pectin on dry basis; a clear jelly can be obtained from them. 
  3. Stem bark and twig bark are reported to contain 9-17 and 11-0-11-9 per cent tannin respectively and are suit- able for direct tanning. The tannin is considered to be of the pyrogallol class; it penetrates hides quickly and gives dull buff, soft and supple leather
  4. In indigenous system of medicine, the fruit is used as a poultice in sprains and swellings.
  5. Wood of S. caseolaris has not attracted much attention in India, because of the non-availability of large sizes, probably due to the neglect of proper silvicultural treatment to improve the growth of the tree. In Sarawak, Brunei, Philippines and the neighbouring areas, how-ever, it is considered a useful timber and has been put to many uses. The wood from these places is described as grayish or dark brown in colour, straight- or slight interlocked-grained, fine- and even-textured, fairly hard and heavy (av. wt., 801 kg./cu.m.). It can be seasoned fairly well in boards, but logs and heavy planks are liable to internal cracking. It lasts well in the ground and the heartwood is said to be very resistant to teredos.
  6. The wood, however, contains small quantities of salt which corrodes iron, making it necessary that all nails and screws used with it should be of copper or galvanized iron. With this precaution, the wood is used for piles. bridges, boat- and ship-making, paving blocks, interior construction such as doors, ceilings and floorings, furniture, cabinet-work and musical instruments. 
  7. Pulping studies on the wood from Australian trees yielded by sulphate process 52-7 per cent of easily deft-bred (fibre length, 1-07-1.08 mm.; diam., 0.027-0.028 mm.) brown pulp (lignin, 8-5; pentosan, 17.6%), suitable for the production of kraft paper. Chemical examination of the wood from Indian trees gave two colouring matters, archin ( m.p. 248°, 2%) and archinin(m..p.- 197°, 1.5°o) and a colourless, crystalline,water-soluble phenolic compound, archicinc (m. p. 257°, 0.05%). Archin and archinin have been shown to be identical with 1:3:8-trihydroxy-3-mcthyl anthra-quinone and 1 :y-dihydruxy-3-m(.'tliyl aiithraquinolu; respectively.
