DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:
Stem simple or branched, angular-ribbed, densely hairy. Leaves decussate, elliptic, oblong or ovate, 3-12 x 1.5-6.0 cm, acute, shortly acuminate or obtuse at apex, narrowed at base, entire or crispy-undulate at margin, glabrous or variously pubescent; petiole 0.5-1.5 cm long. Flowers in initially dense, finally elongated, 8-18 cm long spikes; peduncles 1.0-5.5 cm long; bracts lanceolate, 0.1-0.5 cm long, membranous, glabrous; bracteoles ovate, 0.1-0.3 cm long, appressed against base of perianth, spinescent. Perianth segments 5, ovate-lanceolate, 0.3-0.6 cm long, the outer longest, green with pale margin, 3 or more nerved. Stamens 5. Pseudostaminodes subquadrate. Utricle ellipsoid, 1-3 mm long, rounded at base. Seeds cylindrical, smooth.

Economic Potential and Uses
UTILIZATION:
Uses:
The plants used in dropsy, piles, boils, eruptions of the skin etc. Dried plant is given to children for colic and also used in gonorrhoea. Plant crushed in water and again boiled is given in pneumonia. Young leaves are served as spinach. Infusion of roots is given in mild astringent. 


Chemistry:
The water –soluble base, C5H11NO21 m.p. 292° isolated from A. aspera Linn. has been identified to be simple betaine and not achyyranthine, C6H11NO2.H2O, a betaine derivatine of N-methyl-pyrrolidine-3-carboxy acid. (Kapoor, V. K., 1966).

Seeds of Achyranthes aspera contains saponin A (a-rhamnopyranosyl (1®4)-  [b]-glucopyranosyl (1®4)-[ b]- galactopyranosyl (1®3)-oleanolic acid and saponin B [b]-galactopyranosyl (1®28) ester of  saponin A (Fig. 1). (Hariharan and Rangaswami, 1970).

Ecdysterone (polypodine A) most widely occurring phytoecdysone which have potential use as chemosterilants have been isolated from various parts of Achyranthus aspera seeds – 0.25 yields (g/kg), Stem and leaves – 0.04, roots – 0.09 (Banerje and Chadha, 1970, Banerji et al., 1971).

Two-oleanolic acid based saponin from fruits and ecdysone from roots. (Batta and Rangaswami, 1973).

From the unripe fruits of A. aspera two saponins (C and D) (Fig. 2(I) and (II)) have been isolated and their structure elucidated. Saponin-C has been shown to be b-O-glucopyranosyl ester of a-L-rhamnopyranosyl (1®4)- b-D-glucuronopyranosyl (1®3) oleanolic acid (I) and saponin D as b-D-glucopyranosyl ester of a-L-rhamnopyranosyl-(1®4) - b-D-glucopyranosyl (1®4). b-D-glucuronopyranosyl (1®3) oleanolic acid (IV). (Seshadri, 1981).

Linoleic (49.4), oleic (22.6), palmitic (18.6), stearic (4.4), behenic (1.8), arachidic (1.6), myristic (1.2) and lauric (0.4%) acids present in seed oil. (Daulatabad and Ankalgi, 1985), C. A. 1985, 103, 34909 p.

Isolation of a aliphatic dihydroxyketone from shoots and characterized as 36, 47- dihydroxyhenpentacontan – 4-one (Fig. 3) along with triacontanol. (Misra et al., 1991).

17-pentatria – contanol isolated from shoots. (Misra et al., 1992).

Two long chain compounds 27-cyclohexylheptacosan-7-01 (Fig. 4 (I)) and 16-hydroxy-26 methylheptacosan-2-one (Fig. 4 (II)) isolated from shoots and their structure was established. (Misra et al., 1993).

Pentatriacontane, 6-pentarriacontanone, 10-triacosanone and 4-tritriacontanone isolated from seeds. (Ali, M. 1993), C.A. 1994, 120, 265781h.

Two new compounds 4-methyl – heptatriacont–1- en-10-ol (Fig. 5 (I)) and tetracontanol–2 (Fig. 5 (II)) have been isolated along with b-sitosterol from the shoots of Achyranthes aspera Linn. and their structures have been elucidated by spectral data and chemical studies. (Mishra et al., 1996), C.A. 1996, 125, 5473r.

Two-bisdesmosidic triterpenoid saponins were isolated from the MeOH extract of aerial parts of Achyranthes aspera Linn. as b-D-glucopyranosyl 3b-O[a-L-rhamnopyranosyl-(1®3)-O-b-D- glucopyranuronosyloxy] machaerinate (1) and b-D-glucopyranosyl 3b [O-b-D-galactopyranosyl-(1®2) –O-a-D-glucopyranurono-syloxy] machaerinate (2). The other saponins were identified as b-D-glucopyranosyl 3bO-a-L-rhamnopyranosyl-1®3)-O-b-D-glucopyranurono-syloxy oleanolate (3), b-D-glucopyranosyl 3-b-O-b-Dgalactopyranosyl (1®2)-O-b-D-glucopyranuro-nosyloxy oleanolate (4), and b-D-glucopyranosyl 3b-D-glucopyranuronosyloxy oleanolate (5) (oleanolic acid-3b-hydroxyolean-12-en-28-oic acid) (Fig. 6). (Michl et al., 2000).


Pharmacology:
Stem bark extract showed significant abortifacient activity in mice. (Rastogi and Mehrotra 1970-1979).

Mixture of seeds saponinins increased force of contraction of isolated guinea pig and rabbit heart and this effect at lower dose (1-50 mg) was blocked by pronethalol. Saponins also increased tone of hypodynamic heart and force of contraction of failing papillary muscle. (Gupta et al., 1972).

Seeds used as emetic and in treatment of hydrophobia. (Batta and Rangaswami, 1973).

Benzene fraction of the benzene extract of the plant. A. aspera shows cent percent abortifacient activity in rabbit at the single dose of 50mg/kg of body weight.  The drug is neither estrogenic nor antiestrogenic or androgenic in mice. Abortion may not be due to the deficiency in prolactin, growth hormone or pituitary gonadotropins. The drug is non-toxic and non-teratogenic. (Pakrashi and Bhattacharya, 1977).

Unripe fruits used in treatment of certain diseases of respiratory tract due to saponins present in it. (Seshadri et al., 1981).

Contraceptive and hormonal properties of A. aspera was investigated in rats and hamsters. (Wadhwa et al., 1986).

A. aspera whole plant powder produce a significant dose-related hypoglycaemic effect in normal as well as alloxan diabetic rabbits. Aqueous and methanolic extracts decreased blood glucose levels in normal and alloxan diabetic rabbits. (Akhtar and Iqbal, 1991).

An essential oil and a long chain alcohol have been isolated from the shoots of A. aspera showed anti fungal activity against Aspergilluscarneus. The oil which has been characterized as 17-pentatriconatol. (Misra et al., 1992).

The EtOH extract and alkaloids AM-1 and AM-2 from stems of the medicinal plant A. aspera were biologically acitive. The extract and AM-1 were inhibitory toward Bacillus subtilis, Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Shigella dysenteriae. AM-2 was active against only B. subtilis and S. dysenteriae. (Raman et al., 1996), C. A. 1996, 125, 30024c.

n-Butanol extract of aerial part of A. aspera linn has shown statistically significant anti-implantation activity in female rats. (Tatke and Gabhe 1999). BA. 2000, 107, 253682.

Plant ash of Achyranthes aspera is extracted with water and the extract is used as preservative (antimicrobial agents). Foods treated with the extract is preserved longer than with conventional preservatives. (Yamahara, 2000). CA. 2000, 132, P307603 g.

Leaf extract of A. aspera has prothyroidic and antiperoxidative activity. Leaf extract at a dose of 200mg/kg wt/day for 7days in male rats enhanced the levels of both serum tri-iodothyronine (T (3)) and thyroxine (T (4)) thyroid hormones along with an increase in serum glucose concentration, body wt. and hepatic protein content. It decreased hepatic lipid peroxidation with out altering the free radical scavenging activities of antioxidant enzymeslike superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT). (Tahiliani and Kar, 2000).

Miscellaneous Information
Other information:

Aqueous extracts of A. aspera (1gm, 2gm and 5gm) of different parts in 100ml of tap water showed inhibition on germination and seedling growth of bajra. Radicle length, leaf sheath length, total seedling length fresh weight and dry weights, were progressively decreased with increase in the concentrate extracts. (Philomina and Rao 1999).

Abutilon indicum and Achyranthes aspera were identified for theromochemical gasification. The rate of gas production in theromoconversion process was 1.709, 2.212 m3 kg-1 respectively and a maximum biogas production of 20.131 kg-1 was observed for Achyranthes aspera, L. in a biochemical conversion process. (Subramanian and Sampathrajan, 1999, C. A., 131, 61055 A).

Review of various medicinal uses of A. aspera L. reported so for along with phytochemical screening. (Reddy, 2000). BA, 2001, 108, 318383.

Toxic principle present in aqueous leaf extract of A. aspera showed inhibition on seed germination, seedling growth leaf sheath elongation and root growth of pennisetum americanum. (Philomina and Rao, 2000).
