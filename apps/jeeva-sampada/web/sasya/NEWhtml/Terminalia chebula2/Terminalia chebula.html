Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Terminalia chebula is a moderate sized to large deciduous tree, which grows in the sub-Himalayan tract from the Sutlej eastwards ascending up to about 1,500 m elevation, and also in deciduous forests of Peninsular India and Madhya Pradesh. The tree adopts to wide range of climatic conditions. The annual rainfall varies considerably in its natural habitat. In areas of its natural distribution, the absolute maximum shade temperature varies from about 37° to 48°C, the absolute minimum temperature from about -1° to 15°C and the annual rainfall from about 750 to 3,250 mm. It grows on a variety of geological formations and soils. In the sub-Himalayan tract it grows on bhabar formation and in the outer Himalayan ranges it is found on dry slopes with shallow and sandy soil. In Madhya Pradesh it is common on metamorphic rocks. It grows on the Deccan trap in Maharashtra and on laterite in the Mahableshwar plateau. It is found growing on clayey as well as sandy soil (5, 6, 7, 8).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Terminalia chebula is a moderate or large deciduous tree, attaining 25-30 m in height and 1.5-2.4 m in girth, with a cylindrical bole of 4-9 m., a rounded crown and spreading branches. The bark is dark brown, longitudinally fissured with woody exfoliations. Leaf buds, branches and youngest leaves are covered with soft, shining rust coloured hairs. Leaves 7-20 cm, glabrous, opposite, elliptic-oblong, rounded, acute apex, ovate-oblong, obtuse or cordate at the base, shortly petioled (0.5-1 inch long), pubescent, usually with 2 glands near their summit. Fruits (Drupe) obovoid (egg shaped) or ellipsoidal from a broad base, yellow to orange brown, glabrous, sometimes tinged with red or black and hard when ripe, 3-5 cm long (2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10).

Flowers sessile, yellowish white with an offensive smell. Flowers all hermaphrodite in terminal spikes (2-4 inch long), bracteoles exceeding the flowers, linear, acute, hairy, conspicuous among the buds but soon deciduous. Calyx companulate, flat at the base, expanding a little towards the mouth, glabrous outside, hairy within, teeth 5, short sometimes obscure (2, 3, 6, 9, 10).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Breeding System :

Phenology :
The period of flowering differs with the locality. In North India, it flowers from March to May and in the Himalayan valleys as late as June. In Madhya Pradesh, two distinct flowering periods, such as April-May and July-August are reported. The new flush of leaves appears from March to May while the old leaves are shed from December to March (5, 6, 7).

Pollinators:
Pollination is carried by insects. 22 insect species were found over the Terminalia flowers viz., 6 Hymenoptera, 6 Lepidoptera, 6 Diptera, 2 Hemiptera and 2 Coleoptera. Pollen foragers were generally more efficient pollinators than nectar foragers, and bees were important in pollination (22).

Seed Biology :
Seeds are hard and pale yellow in colour. About 140-220 seeds dry fruits weigh one kilogram. The seed stores well for at least a year and one or two year old seed germinates well than fresh seed.

Back to Top

Seedling Germination :
The germinative power of the seed is poor. Germination capacity and germination energy of Terminalia bellirica seeds can be enhanced or increased by various presowing treatments viz., (including soaking in water, concentrated sulfuric acid and gibberellin (GA3), stratification and scarification). Germination is epigeous and it starts in 15 days and is completed in 3-4 weeks. The growth of the seedlings is somewhat slow; a height of about 10-20 cm and 30-60 cm can be expected for one and two year old seedlings (5, 6, 7, 25, 28).

Cultivation:

Not much work has been done on commercial cultivation of this tree. But results show that planting out of nursery plants is more reliable than direct sowing because of the poor germinative power of the seed and high rodent damage. Stump planting performs better than planting out of entire plants. Sowing in nursery may be done in July. The fruit stones are sown after removing the hardened fleshy covering. Pre-sowing seed treatment may be helpful and their soaking for 48 hours in cold water is recommended. One or two year old plants can be planted out with balls of earth. Planting is done in July-August in pits dug advance. An experiment showed that T. Chebula responds well to air-layering and rooting was best with 10 000 ppm IBA (Indole butyric acid) treatment. First report on the propagation of Terminalia chebula Retz. through patch budding and Cleft grafting were recorded. (5, 6, 7, 11, 21, 23, 26, 27, 29).

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests:

Terminalia chebula does not suffer from any serious pests. Few pests have been recorded on the species, but none of them causes any appreciable damage. Among them, the most important of them are: Ascotis infixaria., Hyblaea puera and Asura dharma (5, 6)

Diseases:

Terminalia chebula does not suffer from any serious diseases.

Economic Potential and Uses

The kernels on extraction yields 36.4 per cent of fatty oil. The oil in the kernel increased the motility, of the gastrointestinal tract of the mouse. The action was comparable with castor oil. The oil by itself is non-irritant but releases an irritant principle when incubated with lipase. This plant is valued more as a medicinal crop rather than oilseed crop. So oil has not gained the commercial importance, though it is reported to be used in locally for lighting and in medicine (6, 12).

Uses

Myrobalans are marketed in the form of whole nuts, crushed nuts (devoid of seeds, which contain very little tannin), and solid and spray-dried extracts which are used in tanning. Myrobalan is one of the principal bloom yielding tans due to its high ellagitannic acid content and is useful especially in the production of sole-leather. In addition to its use in tanning, the extract is used on an extensive scale for the internal treatment of locomotive feed-waters, and as an additive in oil-drilling compositions. Both myrobalans and the purified tannic acid prepared from them are widely used in ink-making. The myrobalan-pulp or spent-tan, obtained as a by- product during the extraction of tannin, can be used for making activated carbon, ellagic acid or furfural and as a filler in cardboard manufacture, in adhesive resins, and as a fuel, or for paper-making. Fruits are highly nutritious and should be cultivated to supplement human dietary requirements. Mature fruits of Terminalia chebula were compared with apples, the edible tissue of T. chebula contained 10.3 times more vitamin C and 14.5 times more protein. It is also found to be rich in macro- and micro nutrients. The minimum Recommended Dietary Allowance for selenium, potassium, manganese, iron and copper could be met if 100 g of the raw fruit was eaten.

The timber is not of much value and is used chiefly in South India for construction and as posts and beams. It is also used for carts, chiefly for the frames, axles and shafts. It is occasionally used for dugouts and might be tried for bottoms of railway wagons, railway keys, buffers, and brake-blocks. The wood has been found suitable for the manufacture of good-quality tool- handles. In combination with Phyllanthus emblica and Terminalia bellirica under the name Triphala fruits of Termlnalia chebula are extensively used as adjunct to other medicines in almost all diseases. Some of the uses of triphala are as follows (6, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 19, 20):

  • It is a strengthening rejuvenator.

  • It also nourishes the nervous system and increases both nutrient assimilation and digestive enzymes.

  • It regulates metabolism, reduces excess fat, builds blood, muscle and nerves, purifies the blood, and removes abdominal gas and distention.

  • It cleanses out toxins and accumulations of toxic mucus throughout the body, and prevents further accumulation.

  • Recently Triphala is generating significant interest around the world because each of the three fruits displays anti-HIV activity.

  • Over a period of time it revitalizes the whole body by detoxifying practically every system in the body, vitalizing the major and minor metabolic processes, and increasing the red blood cell count.

  • It also promotes digestion, appetite, and the assimilation of nutrients, especially Vitamin B which are so necessary for energy production and stress.

Harvesting Practices
The fruits ripen from November-March depending on the locality and fall soon after ripening. The crop varies from year to year. They are collected in the first half of the January from the ground as soon as they have fallen. These are dried and seeds can be stored for one year (5, 6).

Produce and Products

Produce and Products Oil is obtained from the seeds which is being used in medicine. Fruits have industrial importance and has wide number of uses. Leaves are good source of fodder and wood is used in the manufacture of agricultural implements. Apart from that fruits and seed oil have lot of medicinal value.

Processing Techniques

On a commercial scale oil is obtained from the seed by expression or by extraction by solvents the former being a common practice in the villages. There are three traditional methods of expressing oil from seed. (1) Boiling method (2) Chappa method and (3) Bullock Ghani method. In boiling method the depulped seed is boiled in water for some time and then dried. Thereafter it is roasted on a hot pan until the shell become crisp and friable. The whole seed is crushed into powder and it is boiled in water for some time after which the oil floating at the top water is separated by decantation. In the Chappa method the powdered kernel is steamed over a vessel of boiling water and pressed between the two limbs of chappa. In bullock ghani method the powdered kernel is sprinkled with little hot water and pressed in a Bullock-Ghani (6, 8).

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

This tree is grown mainly for fruit (myroblan) for its wide range of industrial uses. Timber is used for construction purposes, leaves for livestock and silkworm fodder. The plant is used extensively in the preparation of many ayurvedic formulations for infectious diseases such as chronic ulcers, leucorrhoea, pyorrhoea and fungal infections of the skin. Fruits have the tremendous potential as medicine. Retail market price of unripe fruit powder is Rs.300/Kg; and Ripe fruit powder is Rs.150/Kg. The estimated production of dried fruits in the country is 80, 000- 100, 000 tonnes, of which 50-60% is collected. They are marketed in the form of whole nuts, crushed nuts (devoid of seeds). The plant has not gained importance as an oilseed crop (6, 11, 12).

Export Potential

Small quantities of myroblan fruits were exported during 1970's and 1980's to UK, USA, Singapore, Afghanisthan, Belgium and Japan. There are no available records of seed oil exports from India.

Miscellaneous Information

For medicinal purposes, the fruits are usually picked green and dried black. In Indian medicine, six kinds of fruits are usually recognized: 'Halileh-i-zira', when the size is that of a cumin seed; 'Halileh-i-javi', when the size is of a barley corn; 'Halileh-i-zangi', when the size is of a raisin, 'Halileh-i-chini', when the fruit is greenish yellow and somewhat hard; Halileh-i-asfar, when it is very nearly mature and 'Halileh-i-kabul', or fully mature fruit. The second, third and sixth kinds of these are used in medicine; the fourth and fifth are good for tanning (6).
