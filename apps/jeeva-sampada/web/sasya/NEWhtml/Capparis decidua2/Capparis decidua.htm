 Other Biological Details
HABIT & HABITAT
Ker or kair (Capparis decidua) is a desert plant that grows wild without much care in the Thar desert of western Rajasthan, India. It is a bushy plant, prevalent in desert areas and grows even on rocky, gravel and sandy plains including sand dunes forming the main feature of desert landscape. Besides, wide distribution in arid and semiarid parts of Rajasthan, it is also present in parts of Uttar Pradesh, Punjab and Haryana in all types of waste lands (Raj Bhansali and Jindal, 2000). According to Chandra et al. (1994), natural habitat of ker is pediment plains all over the dry regions and its density ranges from 8 to 44 bushes per hectare (average 22/ha). The area under pediment plains in Jodhpur and Bikaner districts is 3,540 sq km giving an estimated production of 70,000 tones.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
Capparis decidua is believed to have been originated in India and has a chromosome number of x=7.  It is a straggling glabrous shrub, which has smooth green leaves on the young seedlings and shoots whereas the older branches are leafless. The leaves are small, acute, spinous, pointed and petioles are very short. Flowers are corymbs that emerge from the old branches or from the short lateral shoots; pedicels are cylindrical, the lower sepal is saccate, acuminate and the upper being smaller, ovate or oblong; petals are red, narrow and oblong. Red or orange red scarlet flowers appear in March-April; berries are of varying size (1 to 1.5 cm diameter), globose, green when immature turning to red on ripening and have sweet slimy pulp.

Phenology :
Flowering occurs twice a year, during February-March and July-August, after the plants attain an age of 5 years; flowering is more in February-March as compared to rainy season. Similarly, quality of fruit is superior during this flowering period. From March flowering, the green immature acrid fruits are harvested in May-June for pickling, dehydration and vegetable purpose. The fruits from second flowering are available in October, which are rarely used.

Cultivars

Since it exists only in semi-wild form, no varieties are known. However, great variation in fruit size and pulp content exists. Efforts are being made by the National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources to identify promising types of ker for further multiplication through collection of ker germplasm. Two types of fruits are observed viz., green and red.

Soil and climate

Soil
It is a very hardy tree and it thrives well even in light sandy soils or wastelands,
which are sandy and poor in organic matter and nutrients. It also grows well in saline alkaline soils. However, very high salinity does not permit it to establish. Gupta et al. (1989) have observed that in Jodhpur, Nagour, Shethavati zone of western Rajasthan, India where soils are heavy in saline depressions with 50 to 100 dSm'1, the ker is the main shrub. High salt concentrations in the top 10-28 cm of soil did not adversely affect plant growth.

Perennating spores of the arbuscular-mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) belonging to genera Glomus, Gigaspora and Acaulospora were recovered from the mycon-hizosphere soil in root samples of C. decidua.

Climate
Ker plants are widely spread in arid and semiarid region of Deccan plateau, Rajasthan and Punjab. It grows well in dry climate of tropics and subtropics and can withstand very high temperatures. In Thar desert of western Rajasthan during the month of May, sometimes temperature touches 50°C and it normally remains 45-48°C. The high temperature in these regions is associated with low and erratic rainfall varying from 100 to 400 mm per year.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES
Pests: Very few pests and diseases have been reported to attack ker bushes.

Diseases: In arid zone conditions attack of Colletotrichum and Alternaria stem rots have been observed in vegetatively propagated saplings transplanted in field. Mortality increased up to 45% in poorly drained light soil. Fusarium solani and Rhizoctonia root rots have also been recorded in young seedlings raised in polybags. A rhabdovirus in C. spinosa var. rupestris showing vein yellowing, and provisionally named caper vein yellowing rhabdovirus (CapVYV) was mechanically transmitted to Nicotiana benthamiana.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Fruits, Tender Shoots, Stem and Root bark, Wood

Uses

  • Ker fruits are mainly used in pickling, or as vegetable when immature. Ker pickles are highly delicious. 
  • Ripe fruits are sweet but acrid and enjoyed by children. 
  • Immature fruits are dried in Rajasthan for subsequent use as vegetable in off-season.


Harvesting Practices
Green unripe berries are harvested by picking. One person can harvest approximately 10 kg fruits per day. These green mature berries can be dried and recovery is about 20 per cent. Dried berries are utilized in making pickles and vegetables during festival days in desert areas.

Yield
On an average, 2-5 kg fruits (mature green berries) per plant per annum can be
harvested. However, with 10-20 kg estimated fruit yield per bush by giving proper
management practices, it works out to be 20-80 q/ha fresh fruits .

Chemistry
Fruit pulp is about 55 per cent of total weight which contains 56% moisture, 8.6%protein (crude), 5.02% true protein, total sugars 1.8% reducing sugars 1.1%, P 0.057%, K 1.026%, Ca 0.055%, Mg 0.055%, ascorbic acid 7.81 mg/100 g. Thus the fruit is rich in protein and mineral matter. Unripe fruits are rich source of proteins (18.6%) and vitamin C (8 mg/100 g pulp). Seeds are rich in oil (20.3%) of which 68.6% is unsaturated fatty acid.

Other Uses
  1. Fruit is useful in cardiac troubles and biliousness, tender leaves and shoots are used for paltering boils. Tender shoots, when chewed, relieves toothache and the bark is useful in cough, asthma and inflammation. Root-bark is used against intermittent fever and rheumatism.

  2. Oil extracted from seeds is useful for skin diseases and possesses high medicinal value. Top green shoots of ker are grounded to powder and used in affliction of the pain. The fresh young tender shoots after crushing and soaking in water are strained off. The residue is dried and allowed to solidify for application. It is eaten with butter in small quantity, which gives relief from pains of bruise. Ker has pronounced hypocholesterolaemic effect, which seems to operate through increased faecal excretion of cholesterol as well as bile acids. The dietary fibre influences total lipids, cholesterol, triglycerides and phospholipids of the liver to varying extents. Anti-hepatotoxic activity of p-methoxy benzoic acid has been reported in C. decidua.
