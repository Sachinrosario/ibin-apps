Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:

Sunn Hemp is an erect, herbaceous, shrubby annual. It is one of the most commonly cultivated fiber crops in India, ranking next in importance to jute as a fiber crop. It is cultivated nearly throughout India. The plant is probably indigenous to India, although it has never been found in a truly wild state.
Availability :

Plenty
Conservation/ Threat Status : Cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

The vegetative parts of sunn hemp is covered by short downy hairs. The stems are green, cylindrical and ribbed.

The leaves are simple, spirally arranged, with a phyllotaxy of 2/5; stipules minute, pointed, petiole short, about 5 mm long with pulvinus; lamina linear elliptic to oblong, entire 4-12 x 0.5-2 cm.

The root system is long with a strong tap-root and numerous well-developed lateral roots; nodules are freely produced and are much branched and lobed, upto 2.5 cm in diameter. Flowers are showy. Fruit is a softy hairy inflated pod. Seeds are small, flattened, kidney-sapped, dark grey to black, loose at maturity and mattling in pod.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

The inflorescence is a terminal open raceme to 25 cm in length with minute linear bracts. Flowers have shot pedicels with 2 small lateral pointed appendages near base of calyx, sepals 5, hairy, shortly united at base, lobes pointed with 3 lower sepals united at tips to form a boat like structure under keel and with two upper sepals similarly united. Corolla dark yellow; standard erect, about 2.5 cm in diameter, rounded, sometimes streaked purple on dorsal surface; wings shorter, keel twisted, very acute; stamens 10, almost free to base, 5 with short filaments, and long narrow anthers and 5 with long filaments and small rounded anthers; style long, bent, with rows of hairs along one surface and terminal stigma with tuft of hairs.

Fruit pod is about 3x1 cm, grooved along upper surface with a short pointed beak, pod light brown when ripe, containing 10 to 15 seeds per pod. Seed weight highly variable, ranging from 18,000 to 30,000 per kg depending on variety.

Sterility in sunn hemp is due to the failure of growth of pollen tube, so that fertilization does not occur. In Crotolaria juncea the suspensor is much larger and massive and like a carrot tap-root with epidermal cells protruding from the proximal half. The suspensor cells contain chloroplasts and the tubular peripheral cells have a haustorial role


Breeding system and varietal improvement:
The lowest flowers of the inflorescence open first and remain open for 2 days. In the bud the stamens with the linear anthers are longest and dehisce with the bud. The filaments of stamens with ovate anthers then elongate, pushing the liberated pollen to the orifice of the keel. The stigma lies at the orifice above the pollen mass. The wings and keel are articulated by a ball and socket joint. Insect pollinators alight on the wings and set ball and piston mechanism into operation, when the stigma followed by a mass of pollen is forced against the abdomen of he insect. He ovate anthers at the top of the keel at the end of the first day or following morning, and the pollen from them is liberated by further visits of the bees. Extensive cross pollination occurs and self pollination only takes place after the stigmatic surface has been stimulated by the bee or mechanically. According to Kundu (1964), seed setting is practically nil when the flowers are kept inside muslin bags. Thus sunn hemp may be considered as a highly cross pollinated plant, and the flower must be tripped as in alfalfa to be fertilized.

All of the sunn hemp varieties in India have been developed by selection at various stations rather than by breeding. Several interspecific crosses were made at Jute Agricultural Research Institute in an effort to develop strains resistant to top shoot bores and wilt disease. An attempt to cross C. retusa with C. juncea was unsuccessful. In reciprocal crosses when C. retusa was used as a female parent, the seed pods formed normally but did not set seed. Attempts to cross C. verrucosa and C. incana with C. juncea have like wise been unsuccessful.


Phenology:


Pollinators: Pollinators of sunn hemp plant are large bees such as Xylocopa spp. and Vegaectle spp

Floral and seed biology:

Seedling germination:

The field is ploughed 2-3 times, soon after the first summer rains in April and harrowed to give a good tilth. Generally no manure is added. If grown as a green manure to be followed by paddy, a dose of super-phosphate is added before sowing. The seeds are sown broadcast, though occasionally sowing by drills is adopted. They are sown thick to ensure a crop without much branching. The seed rate is usually 60-80 lb per acre, in certain parts of Bengal, the seed rate is as high as 120 lb per acre, while in some parts of Madras it is only 25 lb per acre. For green manuring the seed rate is usually 80 lb per acre.

If the germination is satisfactory, no further is required until the crop is ready for harvesting. The field is not generally irrigated.


MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
The two worst insect pests of sunn hemp moth (Utethesia pulchella L. and the top shoot borer (Laspeyresia pseudonctis Meyr.) Both species cause tremendous damage to sunn hemp in India each year despite the use of some semi resistant varieties.

The genus Utethesia is found in South Floida, India and Brazil. The adult is commonly known as the bella or sunn hemp moth; however, this insect is injurious only in the larval stage. It first attacks the leaves but prefers the seed pods.

The top-shoot borer causes an 11.5% loss of plants as recorded by the sunn hemp research station, Pratapgarh, India. The larvae of this species bore into the apical tip of the plant, causing branching and cessation of growth. The possible control would be the use of resistant varieties like 'K-12', 'M-19' and 'M-35' or diazinon spray.

The caterpillars of the genus Argina do extensive injury to sunn hemp, feeding principally on the leaves. The sunn hemp caterpillar (Argina cribaria (lerck) is a major pest in Madras; however it may be effectively controlled by dusting with 5% BHC.

The southern army worm feeds heavily on the leaves of sunn hemp in south Florida and may be controlled by an application of chlorinated camphene (toxaphene) dust at the rate of 40 kg/ha of 5% dust.

The stink bug (Nezara viridula L.) and related species puncture the young seed pods and suck the juice from the immature seed. They may be controlled by dusts containing 3% of the gama isomer of benzene hexachloride plus 5% DDT and 40% sulfur. Another control measure comprises spraying with BHC containing 0.4 kg of the gamma isomer per hectare.

The flea hopper (Halticus bractatus [Say]) and the cotton flea hopper (Psallus seratus [Reuter], the latter a major pest of cotton, feed on the juices of tender leaves; and the cotton flea hopper often destroys the immature buds. Both the winged adults and the wingless young are alike in their feeding habits. The flea hoppers may be controlled by a dust consisting of 20% toxaphene and 40% sulfur plus an inert diluent applied at the rate of 10-15 kg/ha. The flea hoppers may also be controlled by 5% DDT dust applied at 10-15 kg/ha or by dieldrin applied in the form of a low gallonage spray at the rate of 0.1 kg/ha. The sap sucker (Ragmus importunitas) also may be controlled by any one of the methods mentioned above.

The flea beetle (Longitarsus belgaumensis jaq.) is highly susceptible to DDT. A 5% dust applied at the rate of 10-15 kg/ha will control this insect.


Diseases:

Wilt disease of sunn hemp in Brazil is caused by Fusarium vasinfectum ATK while Fusarium udum var. Crotolariae Pedwick causes considerable damage in India. Both types attack the young plants, causing wilt and necrosis, or the older plants, causing yellowing of the leaves and eventual necrosis.

The second serious disease of sunn hemp is anthrachose or stem break (Colletotrichum curvatm Briant and Martyn) found in India and Southern Rhodesia. The use of disease free seed, seed treatment with a fungicide, clean planting and rotations are recommended for controlling the disease. Also recommended are seed treatment with a fungicide, early planting and spraying of Bordeaux mixture.

Many strains of sunn hemp often are attacked by a virus which causes yellowing and crinkling of the leaves, weak stems that lodge easily and low fiber yields

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
fiber

Sunn hemp is essentially a cardage fiber and is used in the manufacture of ropes, twines, cords and marine cordage. It also finds application in the manufacture of sail-cloth, canvas, matting, sacking and rope soles of shoes and sandals etc. In ship building, the fibers are employed for closing the seams and for similar purposes The fiber is also used for making cigarette paper in India, the UK, US and other countries The tow from retted fiber of sunn hemp is used for marine caulking . The lower grades of fiber, tow, old rope, and crude decorticated fiber have long been used for the manufacture of high grade paper either alone or blended with flax pulp. Being resistant to deterioration in water, sunn hemp is suitable for making fishing nets.

PULP

The whole dry stems of sunn hemp may provide a new economic crop for use in paper. USDA research at the Northern Regional Research Laboratories, Peoria, ILL, has shown that the whole stems possess good pulping characteristics with a high macerate yield pulp that appears suitable for a wide range of end uses. Pulp of the woody stems also give a fair yield of short-fired pulp with the individual fibers averaging about 0.7 mm in length. This pulps is similar to that of bagasse and is suitable only for lower grade cheap paper. However, when this pulp was mixed with 20% bamboo pulp, the resulting bled made or paper possessing good qualities of strength, flex and tear

Parts Used:

Stem, leaves, seed.


Harvesting Practices:
The plants are normally cut close to the ground with a sickle, although sometimes they are pulled out of the ground. There seems to be considerable difference in opinion as to the optimum time to harvest sunn hemp for fiber. For paper making purposes the highest yields of dry stems occur late, but this does no affect the quality for paper.

Plants are harvested coinciding with the early pod formation in Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh when the fiber quality is highest, in Madras harvest takes place at full flower; in Bihar during the green-pod stage; and in some areas of Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat, Maharashtra and Bengal, harvest takes place only after the seeds have fully formed.

 

Produce and Products:


Chemistry:
fiber is chiefly composed of cellulose (80%), there is a relatively thick layer of lignin around each individual fiber cell

Processing Techniques:
The harvested plants are tied into bundles of 50 to 100 stems and left on the field for 2-3 days, so that, the leaves may wither and drop off. In some areas, the stems are retted only after drying. In U.P., Bihar, Bengal and some parts of Madras retting is carried out without any preliminary drying or stacking. Immediate retting is preferred in humid areas, although pre-drying is stated to yield fiber of better quality.

The method and duration of retting differ from place to place, depending upon the temperature of retting water, and the stage at which the crop is harvested. Retting should be carried out in clear, stagnant or slow-flowing water; where clear water is not available during the retting period, as in some parts of U.P., the stalks should be dried and retted in protected clear water tanks. Before the bundles are immersed in water for retting, the tops and root portions are cut off. If roots are retained, retting will not be uniform, and the matted fibrous mass from the root portions affects the market value other product. The bundles are weighted down by stones, sand bags, logs of wood or even mud and kept submerged. Retting in stagnant and muddy pools affects the colour and luster of the fiber, while retting in flowing water yields a clean fiber of good quality, although it takes a longer time.

The method of fiber extraction from individual stalks is preferable to extraction from whole bundles. In the latter case the fiber becomes some what tangled leading to loss during the cleaning operation.

The fiber is extracted by breaking the retted stalks near the root end and stripping the fiber upwards. If the retting is satisfactory, the fiber peels off smoothly. With dry material and manual operation, the per capita output is 6-8 lb per day of 8 hrs. The process is expensive and there is need for introducing labour-saving machinery. After stripping, the fiber is washed by lashing in water dried in the sun. The dried fiber is twisted and sometimes folded, and made into small bundles or hanks before storing or marketing. Tight and compact twisting, as practiced in certain areas, adversely affects fiber strength and entails much labour in unfolding for cleaning and grading.


Other uses:
The woody stems of sunn hemp remaining after water retting and removal of the bast fiber are most frequently used for fuel.

The sunn hemp plants are used for green manuring by uprooting or cutting close to the ground when 2 - 2 ½ months old, and ploughed into the soil. It is usual to bury the entire plant, but it would be more economical to utilize only the tops and leaves for this purpose, and leave the stems for obtaining fiber.

Sunn hemp stalks are dried and used as cattle fodder.

The leaves of sunn hemp range from 25% to 30% crude protein ad, if not utilized for fertilizer or forage, would provide an excellent material for dehydrated leaf meal which could be used as a high protein live stock and poultry feed supplement.

The seed of sunn hemp are a secondary source of income in India after the fiber and are always in good demand. The seed of sunn hemp contain a large amount of protein-about 35% or more. Considerable research has been carried out on the use of these proteins as adhesives for plywood.
