HABIT & HABITAT

An erect evergreen spinous shrub growing 3-5m at a medium rate, often forming gregarious patches. Occuring on the Himalayas from 6,000-10,500 ft. and also on Nilgiri hills and in Sri Lanka.

The plant prefers light (sandy), medium (loamy) and heavy (clay) soils and can grow in heavy clay and nutritionally poor soils. The plant prefers acid, neutral and basic (alkaline)soils. It can grow in semi-shade (light woodland) or no shade. It requires dry or moist soil.



Availability :  
A shrub indigenous to India and Ceylon.  It is found in U. P, Jammu and Kashmir and Tamilnadu in India.
 
Status : 
In Woodland, Dappled Shade, Shady Edge.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

Evergreen Shrub 3-5 m in height with obovate to elliptic, sub-acute to obtuse, entire or  toothed leaves. Bark pale yellowish-brown, deeply furrowed. Flowers hermaphrodite-golden-yellow in  compound, often corymbose racemes and oblong-ovoid to small sub-globose, bright red berries tapering into a short style and stigma

The roots of these species are yellowish brown, cylindrical, more or less knotty, hard and tough. With the bark intact they are cut into pieces of varying length and a maximum diameter of 45 mm. The bark is internally dark brown and soft, breaking away into a powdery mass. The drug in powdered form is bright yellow with a slight odour and a bitter taste.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Breeding System :

The flowers are hermaphrodite (have both male and female organs).  The plant is self-fertile. 
 
Phenology :

Flowering& Fruiting: April-November

Pollinators:
Pollinated by Insects.

Seed Biology :

Seedling Germination :

Seed - best sown as soon as it is ripe in a cold frame, it should germinate in late winter or early spring. Seed from over-ripe fruit will take longer to germinate. Stored seed may require cold stratification and should be sown in a cold frame as early in the year as possible. When they are large enough to handle, prick the seedlings out into individual pots and grow them on in the greenhouse or cold frame for at least their first winter. Once they are at least 20cm tall, plant them out into their permanent positions in late spring or early summer, after the last expected frosts. The seedlings are subject to damping off, so be careful not to over water them and keep them well ventilated.

Cuttings of half-ripe wood, July/August in a frame. Very difficult, if not impossible.

Cuttings of mature wood of the current season's growth, preferably with a heel, October/November in a frame. Very difficult, if not impossible.


Cultivation:
Prefers a warm moist loamy soil and light shade but it is by no means fastidious, succeeding in thin, dry and shallow soils. Grows well in heavy clay soils.

Plants can be pruned back quite severely and re-sprout well from the base.

Hybridizes freely with other members of this genus. Most plants cultivated under this name are B. chitria., B. coriaria., B. glaucocarpa. and, more commonly, B. floribunda.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts used
Bark, berries, flower buds, roots and stem

Uses

  • The  species is reported to yield one of the best yellow dyes in the country.   The dye is readily soluble in water and alcohol.  The colour changes from yellow to brown on addition of alkali.  The dye is used for the manufacture of Morocco leather.
  • Roots and stem also yield a yellow dye. Dye is used in tanning and colouring leather. Berberry is one of the  best tanning dyes in India.
  • The main properties and uses of this dye are: It is soluble in water and alcohol; the yellow colour is altered to brown by the action of certain alkalis; this dye is used for imparting colour to fabrics and leather.

Produce and Products

Chemistry:

The alkaloids in the bark and root bark of Berberis aristata are berberine, berbamine, aromoline, karachine, palmatine, oxyacanthine and oxyberberine.

Other Uses

1.  A decoction is used as mouth-wash for treating swollen gums and toothacke.

2.     Acidic flower buds are made into a sauce.

3.     Berberine, universally present in rhizomes of Berberis species, has marked antibacterial effects. Since it is not appreciably absorbed by the body, it is used orally in the treatment of various enteric infections, especially bacterial dysentery. It should not be used with Glycyrrhiza species (Liquorice) because this nullifies the effects of the berberine. Berberine has also shown antitumour activity.

4.     Chief source of the drug,  Rasot (alcoholic extract of the bark yields berberine chloride and palmatine chloride).

5.     Dried berries are edible. Fresh berries are laxative and anticorbutic and useful in the treatment of piles, sores and eye diseases, particularly conjuctivitis.  Fruit - raw or cooked. A well-flavoured fruit, it has a sweet taste with a blend of acid, though there is a slight bitterness caused by the seeds. The fruit is much liked by children. It is dried and used like raisins in India. The fruit contains about 2.3% protein, 12% sugars, 2% ash, 0.6% tannin, 0.4% pectin. There is 4.6mg vitamin C per 100ml of juice. The fruit is about 7mm x 4mm - it can be up to 10mm long. Plants in the wild yield about 650g of fruit in 4 pickings.

6.     Mixed with the bark of Cinnamomum tamala Nees and Eberm and honey, prescribed in leucorrhoea.

7.     Plant is an emmenagogue, useful in the treatment of Jaundice and enlargement of spleen etc.

8.     The dried stem, root bark and wood are alterative, antiperiodic, deobstruent, diaphoretic, laxative, ophthalmic and tonic (bitter). An infusion is used in the treatment of malaria, eye complaints, skin diseases, menorrhagia, diarrhoea and jaundice.

9.     The wood is used as a fuel.
