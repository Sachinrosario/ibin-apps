Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Fast growing tree found in Subtropical regions like Himalayan foothills and Central Indian hills, in the tropical high rainfall areas of Western Ghats, West Bengal and Assam (wet tropical), tropical medium rainfall areas of Indo-Gangetic Plains and the Peninsula (moist tropical), dry areas of Rajasthan and Peninsular India (dry tropical), suitable for open lands and along pathways. Commonly found throughout the greater part of India.

It is found apparently wild on Sivalik hills. It grows wild in the dry forests of Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu and Karanataka.

It survives great heat – even shade temperatures up to 440 C, it grows on almost all kinds of soils. It thrives better than most other trees on dry, stony, shallow soil with a waterless subsoil, or in places where there is a hard calcareous or clay pan near the surface. It does not tolerate inundation. It will not grow on seasonally waterlogged soils or in deep dry sands where the dry-season water table lies below 18 m. the optimum pH is 6.2 or above, although neem will grow well at pH 5, bringing surface soil to neutral pH by its leaf litter. It does not grow well on saline soils, Arid, semi-arid zones.

Light demander, seedlings can tolerate shade. Frost sensitive, drought hardy, intolerant to water logging or excess soil moisture, susceptible to fire damage.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

A large evergreen tree, 12-18 m in height and 1.8-2.4 m in girth, with a straight bole and long, spreading branches forming a broad crown, bark grey or dark grey, rough, reddish brown inside, with numerous oblique furrows and scattered tubercles. Leaves imparipinnate, alternate, 20-38 cm long. Leaflets 8-19, alternate or opposite, ovate-lanceolate, oblique or sub-falcate, falcate-lanceolate, glossy, bluntly serrate.

Flowers white fragrant, in branched glabrous panicles shorter than the leaves, actinomorphic, bisexual; bracts minute, lanceolate, caducous. Calyx puberulous outside, divided almost to the base, lobes rotund-ovate, minutely ciliolate. Petals obovate-oblong, faintly puberulous outside, ciliolate. Staminal tube glabrous, little shorter than petals, obconic, the laciniae and a little shorter than them, apiculate. Ovary glabrous, 3 celled, the cells opposite to the petals, ovules two in each cell, collateral; stigma 3 toothed, included in the tube. Drupes (shape of an olive), glabrous, yellow, afterwards purple, kernel cartilagenous, 1 celled, 1 seeded, cotyledons plane convex, fleshy, notched at the base.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:


Breeding system and varietal improvement:

Phenology:

In dry localities it becomes almost leafless for a brief period during February - March and the new leaves appear during April. The flowering is spread over January - March in the southern parts of the country and later towards the North. In Kerala,flowering starts in the beginning of January; in February - March in Karnataka, Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh; in the first week of April in central India getting progressively delayed in northern India and Punjab, going up to the first week of May in the sub-Himalayan areas. The tree often has a restricted bloom during August - September in the plains of Uttar Pradesh. The fruiting follows a similar pattern.

Floral and seed biology:

Seed Germination & Propagation: Under natural conditions, the seed ordinarily falls to the ground during the rainy season and germination occurs within a week or two. It reproduces naturally with tolerable freedom.

Although the tree can be raised easily in the nursery and transplanted for afforestation purposes, direct sowings are reported to be more successful than transplanting. For raising transplants and to protect the radicle from insects the seeds should be covered with thin layer of soil, and sparingly watered. The seedlings are fit for transplanting during the first rains, when they are 7-10 cm in height and the taproot is 15 cm long; if larger seedlings are required they may be retained for another year in the nursery and transplanted early in the rains of the second season. The stem and roots may also be pruned. In frosty localities they are protected by screens. Weeding without water also helps in promoting growth. Loosening the soil to prevent caking and to promote aeration is also beneficial.

Seed germination is enhanced by passage through baboon guts. Seeds should be sown immediately after ripening (viability is very low.). seeds germinate within 3 weeks.
Also it can be propagated by planting root suckers and stem cuttings.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts Used:

Tree has long been used as fuel in India and Africa. Plantations have been raised for firewood.

Wood: Sapwood is greyish white; heartwood is red when first exposed, fading to reddish brown. The wood is dull to somewhat lustrous, especially on the radial surface, aromatic but without any characteristic taste, moderately heavy (wt. 705-945 kg/cu m), rather uneven- and narrowly interlocked-grained and medium to somewhat coarse textured. The wood is durable even in open situations. Wood with specific gravity varying from 0.56 to 0.85.

Wood excellent for construction work and furniture making. Idols of god are made from this wood, particularly in Southern India. Neem poles are straight and strong and seldom attacked by termites. The wood is used for furniture, carts, axles, yokes, naves and felloes, boards and panels, cabinets, bottoms of drawers, packing-cases, ornamental ceilings, ship- and boat-building, helms, oars, oil-mills, cigar boxes, carved images, toys, drums and agricultural implements. It is suitable for timber bridges up to 5 m span. Trunks and chests are made out of this wood and are pest-proof.

Timber is substitute for mahogany.

Harvesting Practices:


Produce and Products:

Neem bark contains 12.14% tannin, which is comparable to the conventional tanning chemicals.

Neem cake as fodder

Neem seeds yield oil (40%) used for burning and is antiseptic for animal wounds. Used as lubricant for machinery. Neem oil useful ingredient for soaps and disinfectants and used in pharmaceuticals and cosmetics.

Pulp surrounding the seeds is promising substitute for generating the methane gas.

Neem cake an excellent fertilizer, rich in plant nutrients and antiseptic.

Gum - The bark exudes a clear, bright, amber-coloured gum, known as East India Gum, which blackens with the age

Other uses:

Commonly cultivated as a roadside tree, avenue tree and shade tree on farms and near village community centers.

Energy – Neem seeds contain up to 40 percent oil, which is used as a fuel for lamps and as a lubricant for machinery. 

Fruits - Neem berries edible.

Windbreak, shade - Tree used as windbreak and shade for cattle. In the arid tropics it is a shade tree and used along avenues on soils characterized by kankar pan or lime concretions.

Soil improvement – Leaves and twigs have been successfully used as a mulch and fertilizer in Sri Lanka, India and Burma. Neem cake (the residue left after extracting oil from the seeds) is reportedly an excellent fertilizer, several times richer in plant nutrients than manure. Neem has successfully reclaimed arid wastelands, particularly in India.

Industrial Chemicals – Neem bark contains 12-14 percent tannins. In recent trails in India, these compared favourably with conventional tanning chemicals. Neem oil is a useful ingredient in soaps and disinfectants and in pharmaceuticals and cosmetics.

Insect repellent – The seeds and leaves yield azadirachtin, a compound that appears to be a promising new insect repellent. It is a systemic pesticide that is absorbed into a plant and works from within. Japanese beetles and many other insect pests (even the desert locust) will starve before they will eat plants treated with neem seed extracts. (Azadirachtin also said to be nematode repellent.)

About 100 promising plant species have been identified to control paste. Neem trees, known as the ‘village dispensary’ also posses chemical ‘weapons’.

Fodder - The twigs and leaves can be fed to cattle in conjunction with other feeds. Camels also eat the leaves. In Andhra Pradesh, they are regularly fed to cattle and goats to increase the secretion of milk, immediately after parturition.

Medicine - Almost every part of the tree is bitter and finds application in indigenous medicine.

Agro-social industry

Leaves - Leaves are used as fodder. Leaf paste reduces blood sugar.
Flowers and fruits are edible.
Seed - Seed oil, known as margossa oil, is used in soaps, toothpaste, lotions. It is a cure against skin diseases.
Due to presence of azadirachtin, leaves and seeds act as insecticide.
In small oral doses, the seed or fruit oil controls diabetes.

Miscellaneous Information

 The tree increases the fertility and water-holding capacity, as it has a unique property of calcium-mining which changes the acidic soils into neutral.

It also can be propagated from root- and shoot-cuttings. Application of growth regulators (IBA and NAA) induces root formation.

Somatic embryogenesis and plantlet regeneration is obtained.
In vitro production of azadirachtin from leaf callus and old flower callus has been reported.
