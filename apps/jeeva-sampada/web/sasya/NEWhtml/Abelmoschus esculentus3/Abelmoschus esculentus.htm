Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:

An annual erect herb, the plant is considered to be of African or Asian origin. It is grown as a garden crop or home yard plant through out the tropical and sub-tropical parts of the world. It is raised as an intercrop with sugarcane and gives good returns. It is also an important intercrop in coconut groves (1).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

The plant is 0.3-2.1 mt. In ht., covered with hair. Leaves are cordate, palmately 3-5 lobed, coarsely toothed, flowers are yellow with crimson centre, capsules also called pods, 12.5-30.0 cm long, pyramidal, oblong (horn-like), green or creamy green, with longitudinal ridges, smooth or hairy. Seeds many, rounded, striage, hairy (1).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

The plants start flowering after 40 days of sowing in the early varieties and 50-60 days or more in themid-season varieties.(1) Flowers solitary in the leafaxils, peduncled, acuminate in bud. Calyx shortly dentate, spathaceous during the anthesis, falling off together with the corolla, hispid externally, appressed pilose within, 2-3 cm long. Bracteoles 7-12, linear-lanceolate, acute. Petals 5, obovate, with an obtuse or broadly rounded apex and a fleshy base, glabrous. Staminal tube straight erect, yellowish white, densely bent with anthers throughout its length, 2-2.5 cm long. Style-branches short, stigmas as many as ovary cells. Seeds 0.5-0.6 cm in diameter.(4)

In Okra, syngamy and triple fusion take place simultaneously. In a mature seed, the embryo occupies most of the space, the cotyledons curl, and remnants of endosperm persist in the funicular region and a layer or two between the folds of the cotyledons.(2)

Breeding system and varietal improvement:
The cream coloured flowers open in the morning, and fade into a pink in the evening. Bhindi is self pollinated, its flowers, being ornamental, attract insects which may cause some out crossing, hence segregation of varieties is necessary. To maintain purity of seeds, bhindi varieties should not be grown within about 600m of one anther(1).

Floral and seed biology:


Seedling Germination : Bhindi requires a well-prepared seed-bed, 405 ploughings are necessary to bring the land to a proper tilth. Farmyard manure is applied at the rate of 50-60 cartloads per hectare. Seeds are sown broadcast or dibbled at the rate of 2-3 seeds per hole. They may also be drilled in shallow furrows. The seed rate ranges from 6 to 33 kgs per ha, and on an average 12 kg of seed are sufficient to sow one hectare. The seed rate is higher in saline soils. Seeds may be pre-soaked in water for 12 hours. Pre treatment of the seeds with GA, 10ppm, +PA, 10ppm and NOA, 50 ppm for 6 hrs increases the crop yield remarkably. Plants obtained from seeds treated against major insect pests, with carbofuran, 4 or 5 parts /100 parts of the seeds grew vigorously, flowered earlier by 2-3 days and yielded more fruit. It is also sown on flat beds in rows45 cm apart in areas which are not waterlogged. Closer spacing increases the total yield/hectare, but wider spacing gives more yield/plant. To get a continuous supply of fruits it is usual to sow the seeds in batches, in different beds at intervals of 2-3 weeks during the sowing season.

A germination standard of 60% has been obtained for bhindi seeds. A study of the effect of various weather factors on germination revealed that higher maximum and minimum temperature and longer days are congenial for realising a higher percentage of germination, for imparting earliness in flowering and fruit production and for maximum yield. Therefore, to reap the full benefits of the crop, sowing in the months of March-May is ideal. In some places, seedlings are raised in nurseries and transplanted in the garden.

Bhindi response to judicious manuring. Organic manures are preferred to inorganic fertilizers. Soil application of phorate gave significant increase in plant growth and seed yield.(1)

MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
Bhindi is infested by several pests causing considerable damage to the crop. The fruit borers or spotted bollworms, Earias insulana Boisd. Syn. E. fabia stoll and E. vitella (Fabr.) infest the fruits as well as the shoot-tops, making the fruits unfit for human consumption. Endrin (0.02%) or Lindane (0.5%) may be used with advantage in three applications. Organic phosphates Diazinon and Parathion (0.02%) proved efficacious against the pest.

Aphids and jassids are also seen infestnig bhindi. Jassids suck the cell sap resulting in loss of vigour of the plants. Spraying of Endrin (0.15%) at fortnightly intervals during the pre-fruiting period is recommended, depending on the severity, of the jassid attack.

The leaf hopper, Amrasca biguttula biguttula Ishihara syn. A. (Empoasca) devastans (Distant), is an important pest of bhindi. It feeds on the juice of the plant in the early stages of growth. Application of two rounds of Thiometon (0.1%), sprayed at fortnightly intervals when the crop is about a month old, effectively controlled the pest.

A buprestid leaf - minor bettle, Trachys herilla Obenb., has been observed infesting the leaves of bhindi in Haryana resulting in partial defoliation. Another beetle, Podagrica orbiculata (Motsch) has been reported us a serious pest on bhindi from Sehore (Madhya Pradesh).

Bhindi is one of the hosts of the citrus mite, Eutetranychus orientalis (Kleine). Other mites infesting the plants are Tetranychus eocaledonicus Andre and the red spider mite, T. (Telarius) cinnabarinus boisd. Out of the seven acaracides tested, Chlorphenamidine tramid and Tetradifon gave over 80% control 14 days after a single spray. 'AE-1' and 'Long Green Bhendi' are the less susceptible varieties.

For controlling the pest complex of bhindi, soil application of Disulfoton (solvirex) 5% granules at 1 kg ai/ha at the time of sowing, or seed treatment with carbofuran WP at 6% ai for affording protection against sucking pests upto a period of 40 days after sowing is recommended.

Root galls due to infestation with root knot nematodes, Meloidogyne incognita (Kofoid and White) var. acrita, M. hapla Chitwood and M. javanica (Treub) were observed on the bhindi plants. The plants become sickly andbear reduced number of fruits. These and the reniform nematode, Rotylenchulus reniformis Linford & Oliveira, were controlled by seed treatment with carbofuran at 3 or 6% active ingreident. The soil is treated with nematicides (Nemagon) 3 weeks before sowing to check nematodes. Application of neem cake and addition of saw dust to the nitrogen fertilizer also controlled the root-knot disease. Inter-cropping with Tagetes erecta Linn. helped in checking the population of root infesting nematodes(1).


Diseases:

Bhindi is subject to a number of fungal, bacterial and viral diseases.
Powdery mildew caused by Erysiphs cichoracearum is an important disease affecting the leaves, resulting in their falling off early. Foliar spray or soil-drenching with PP675 (22 kg/ha) a systemic fungicide, is recommended as a control measure. Benlate, another systemic fungicide is highly effective against powdery mildew. Sulphur dust and wettable sulphur (0.5%) may also be used.

Plants grown in winter often suffer from wilt of the seedlings, caused by Fusarium solani (Mart.) Appel and Wollen. Emend. Snyder and Hansen, Growing the plants in light soils is an effective control measure (1).

Leaf spot of bhindi is caused by Cercospora malayensis Solh. And Stevens (C. hibisci Tracy & Earle). 'Nigeria' is a disease - resistant variety. Another leaf spot, caused by an ascomycetous fungus, Leptosphaerulina australis McAlp., has been reported on bhindi from Hyderabad. A pod and leaf spot, caused by Ascochyta abelmoschi Harler, was observed in Solan.

Black spot of bhindi fruits is another major disease caused by Dreschslera nodulosa (Berk. & Curt). Subram. & Jain Syn. Helminthosporium nodulosum Berk. & Curt., forming scattered lsions on ridges of fruit surfaces, resulting in the rotting of the fruits.

A damping off disease, caused by Rhizoctonia solani Kuhn, is quite serious in Karnataka. Seed-dressing with Thiabendazole (0.1%), Benlate (0.1%), Or Thiride (0.2%) is one of the control measures. 'Red Ghana', 'Set 7-1', 'BH 27', '1C 12096' and '1C 17252' were found resistant to this disease.

A leaf blight of bhindi, caused by Alternaria dianthi Stevens and Hall, has been reported from Pune. In the severely affected stage the leaves curl up and become brittle, and the necrotic areas turn dark brown to black giving theplant a withered appearance. In Chota Nagpur, a root and collar rot, caused by Pythium aphanidermatum (Eds.) Fitzp. and Ozonium texanum Neal & Wester var. Parasiticum Thirum has been found to result in serious damage.

Yellow, vein mosaic is the most harmful and serious disease affecting the bhindi crop. White fly, Bemisia tabaci (Gennadius) is the insect vector which transmits the virus. The main symptoms are clearing of the veinlets, followed by chlorosis of the veins, vein swelling, slight downward curling of leaf margin and twisting of petiole and a general retardation of growth. The recommended control measurse are eradication of Abelmoschus manihot subsp. Tetraphyllus Borssum, hollyhock (Alcea rosea cav.) and Cineraria sp., which are wild alternate hosts of the virus; roguing diseases plants at the earliest stage of infection, spraying the crop once in three weeks with fish-oil resin soap, and keeping fields clear of weeds.(1)

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
fiber -

The stalks yield a bast fiber with potential for commercial exploitation. It can be spun into yarn and used for rope, twine and sacking. The fiber can be spun on jute-mill machinery and 4.5 kg yarn can be used with 50% jute admixture for sacking warp or hessian weft. It can be used in 85% admixture with 15% jute in sacking cloth. The fiber is also suitable for the manufacture of paper and cardboard. In West Africa, the fiber is used for fishing lines.

MEDICINAL -

Immature pods are emollient, demulcent and diuretic, and are employed in the form of a decoction in catarrhal affections, ardor urinae, dysuria, dysentry and gonorrhoea. The vapours from the hot decoction are inhaled for irritated throat and in troublesome cough of phthisis. An ethanolic extract of the pods was effective against gram-positive bacteria.

The fruits and leaves may cause skin irritation. The leaves are employed externally as emollient poultice. In Malaysia, an infusion of the roots is used in syphilis. The seeds are stimulant, cordial and antispasmodic. An infusino of the roasted seeds is credited with sudorific properties. Fatty fraction of the fresh watery extract of the seeds causes destruction of cancerous cell growth in vitro.

FOOD -

The tender pods are used as a vegetable. They are eaten boiled or in the form of sliced and fried pieces. They are also used for thickening soups and gravies because of their high mucilage content. Sometimes they are sliced and sun dried for off-season use. They may also be preserved by drying, quick freezing, canning or by barelling in brine, or prickled. In West Africa the flowers are eaten in soups. The tender leaves are sometimes boiled and eaten as spinach. The ripe seeds are roasted and used as a substitute for coffee. They are also used in curries and chutneys. The seed flour is reported to be used in Egypt as an addition to maize flour.(1)

Parts Used:

Stem, leaves, seed, pod, flowers.


Harvesting Practices:
After fruiting, the stalks are generally allowed to go waste or are used as fuel. If, however, they are collected green and retted, a useful fiber can be extracted. The stalks are cut, dried in the sun and tied into bundles.

The fruits of summer crop are ready to be picked 40-50 days after sowing. Harvesting fruits for consumption is recommended 6-9 days after flowering as then they are tender, have maximum protein content (2.08%) and less crude fiber. Harvesting on the third or fourth day of fruit formation resulted in a longer fruiting period. Also, these tender pods stand transport better, and keep better under refrigeration.

For seed purposes a part of the garden / field may be set apart and the pods allowed to develop to maturity, or one or two pods may be retained on individual plants. About 300 plants left for seed are sufficient to sow one hectare. Harvesting for seeds is best if done 30 days after anthesis when the pods are fully mature, dry and have started cracking.(1)

Chemistry:
The fiber is white, light cream or yellow in colour, silky, strong and pliant but somewhat coarse and stiff. Analysis of the fiber gave the following values : Cellulose, 81.89, lignin, 16.98; fat & wax, 0.43, nitrogen, 0.14; and ash, 0.76%(1).

Processing Techniques:
fiber : The harvested bundles of fibers are kept in clear tank water for eight days and stripped. The presence of mucilage in the stem facilitates fiber separation. The extracted fiber requires thorough washing to remove the mucilage, otherwise the fiber obtained is stiff and brittle. An average yield of 2.5 quintals/ha of fiber has been reported.

Gum : A vegetable gum, in dry powder form, is produced from Okra on a small scale in India. The crushed pods (free from seeds) yield a thick viscous solution in water, from which a grey fibrous mass of mucilage is obtained on precipitation with ethanol.

Oil : Ripe, moisture-free seeds contain 16-22% of an edible oil. The extraction of the oil on a commercial scale presents certain difficulties. The seeds are small, almost spherical and the shell is hard and difficult to separate from the kernel. The yield of oil by pressing is low and a large part of it is retained in the cake, whereas solvent extraction gives better yield of the oil which is of better quality. Solvent extracted oil is bright yellow with a greenish tings and fragrant odour. The oil can be refined, bleached and deodorised by the usual methods.

Mucilage (from leves) -

A thick colourless extract is prepared by pounding lower portions of the stem along with a portion of the root in water and straining through a cloth. The extract is added to cane juice at the boiling stage, the albumins present in the extract coagulate and carry the suspended impurities of the cane juice to the surface as a scum. The gur from the clarified juice is hard and crystalline with a golden-yellow colour.(1)


Other uses:
Okra gum, similar to gum tragacanth, gum arabic and gum Karaya, provides body and protective action for emulsions. In the food-processing industry, okra gum, a pure food product, is expected to meet FDA requirements. Its vegetable derivation and rheological properties make okra gum ideal as a combination flavouring and bodying agent in vegetable soups and gravies. It has a potential use as an additive to dried egg white and as a meat tenderiser. It may also be used as an emulsifying agent because of its excellent protective colloid properties. Its use in soup mixes and salad dressings is also suggested.

A mucilagenous preparation from the fruits has found application as a plasma replacement or blood volume expander.

A cereal-flour adhesive, consisting of a dry mixture of cor flour (95%) and okra gum (0.5-5.0%) has been patented as an adhesive suitable for manufacture of corrugated boards.

Mucilagenous extract of the green stems is commonly employed in India for clarifying sugarcane juice is gur manufacture.

The root mucilage is used in China as a sizing for paper. Leaf mucilage is used as a substitute for soap.

The refined oil extracted from seeds has good keeping quality and can be used as a substitute for common edible oils, especially cotton seed oil.

The seed cake is rich in protein and is suitable for use as animal feed, though the fiber content is undesirably high. The meal prepared from decorticated seeds contains as high as 40-50% protein and has been recommended for use in plastics. The hulls may be used as a feed stuff (1).

Miscellaneous Information

 In Okra, the induction of callus from ovules and seeds, and somatic embryogenesis has been reported in callused hypocotyl explants, but has only recently been regenerated through the organogenic pathway.

Cotyledons were the most effective explants for callus production, roots produced the least callus and callus explant produced somatic embryos upon transfer to liquid medium containing 1 mg/12,4-D. In additional time course and concentration experiment embryogenic callus was induced by a 1 mg/12,4-D treatment of explants for 7 to 14 days. However, large number of embryos were not produced in liquid culture from either time course study upon transfer to hormone-free or kinetin containing media. SEM and LM confirmed the presence of somatic embryos in liquid culture. Somatic embryos and associated embryogenic callus produced roots upon transfer to hormone-free, reduced salt, or 0.1 mg/1,2-4D kinetin containing media.

A consistent regeneration system for Okra is required for the exploitation of somaclonal variation for biotic and abiotic stress resistance, crop yield and asexual propagation. Additionally such a system is necessary for the possible improvement of Okra through plant transformation techniques.(2)
