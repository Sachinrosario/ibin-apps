Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Small erect annual shrub. Also known as ajowan, bishop's weed is an aromatic spice closely resembling thyme in flavour (1).

Climate and soil:
Mainly grown as a winter crop in subtropical climate and as a summer crop in temperate climate. Cold loving crop and thereby comes up well under temperate to subtropical conditions grown as winter crop in rabi season in India. It requires temperature between 15-27°C with a relative humidity of 65-70% during growth period and a warm weather during seed development. Crop has a moderate tolerance to drought and wide climatic adaptability. It can be grown in any soil type, rich loamy soil being good for successful growth. It is grown as a rainfed crop in heavy soils and in light textured soils it requires irrigation. It produces good yields in soils having a pH range of 6.5 to 8.2.

Availability :

Mediterranean region & south-west Asian, a native of Egypt. Mediterranean region & south-west Asian. Cultivated in Europe, Egypt, Pakistan, Iran and Afghanistan.
Conservation/ Threat Status : Cultivated

CULTIVATION ASPECT:

Seed germination:
It germinates in about 10-12 days and requires about one month to complete germination.

Released varieties:
Pant Ruchika, Gujarat Ajwain-1, RPA-68, Lam. Sel.1 and Lam. Sel.2.

Selection of planting material:
Seeds are the planting material.

Planting:
Land is to be ploughed twice during September and should be brought to a fine tilth. Seeds sown during September/October in furrows at 20 cm. Seeds are too small and hence are mixed with fine soil or sand in the ratio of 1:10 to facilitate sowing. Seeds are broadcasted or drilled in rows 45 cm apart and if moisture in soil is insufficient, field is to be irrigated immediately after sowing (2). Ridges and furrows are prepared 60 cm apart. Full FYM ½N and full P & K applied as basal and mixed well in the soil. Plant-to-plant spacing 20-30 cm. Plants are thinned at 4-6 leafed stage to maintain one plant per hill at 30-45cm spacing. Seed rate: 4 kg/ha.

Management after planting:
Manure & Fertilizer schedules: FYM @ 10-15 t/ha, N, P2O5, K2O @ 80:30:30 and 50 kg/ha half dose of N and full P2O5, K2O and sulphur should be added as basal dose, remaining dose of N should be applied in two equal splits at 30 DAS and 60 DAS. Irrigation schedules: 5-6 irrigations depending on the climate and soil type at an interval of 15 days. Inter-cultivation/weeding: About 2-3 weeding and hoeing are required to keep the field weed free.


MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
Aphids: Caused by Aphis craccivora and Myzus persicae. Tiny insects, nymphs suck sap from the plant.
Symptoms: causing shrivelling and drying of seeds
Control: Spraying of 0.3% Dimethoate (30EC) or Phosphamidon (85 WSC) or 0.1% Malathion (50EC) in the way of early hours during flowering stage.

Diseases:

Symptoms: Brown to black spots on the collar region of the plant. Drying and death of plant occur in severe conditions.
Control: Spray 0.2% Mancozeb repeat after 10-15 days.

Damping off
Control: Seed treat with Emisan 2g/kg of seed before sowing.

Powdery mildew: Caused by Erysiphe polygoni
Symptoms: Appear at flowering stage in cloudy weather (February-March)
Control: Dusting sulphur 20-25 kg/ha or spraying wettable sulphur 0.2% or Karathane 0.1% or Calaxin 0.05% at 10-15 days interval.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Bishop's weed imparts a specific aroma and taste to a wide variety of foods. It has also excellent preservative and medicinal properties (1). Commercially cultivated for seed and herb, which is of medicinal value. Whole ajowan seed, powder and oil used as adjunct alone or combination with other spice oils and condiments for flavoury food, in pickles, as antioxidants, preservative in confectioneries beverages and mixtures. Thymol is used as and ingredient of deodorant, mouthwashes, toothpaste and many pharmaceutical preparations. Ajowan oil is also used in preparation of lotions and ointments in cosmetics industries and as spice in many food preparations.

Parts Used:

Fruit (seeds)

Harvesting Practices:
Harvesting is done in February-March when the flower beds turns to brown. Crop becomes ready for in 130-170 days depending upon variety and season. The umbels are harvested at fully maturity when a few umbels turned brown; care to avoid shattering.

Harvested manually using sickle. Keep the bundle up side down for drying and threshed winnowed to separate the fruits. Gives an yield of 400-600 kg/ha under rainfall and 1200-2000 kg/ha under irrigate condition.

Post Harvest Handling:
Seeds are dried and stored.

Produce and Products:
Ajowan oil is colourless to brownish with characteristic odour and burning taste. Ajowan powder is also a product.

Medicinal uses
Ajowan has long been used in indigenous medicines. Essential oil of seed is highly valued ingredient in the formulation of Unani and Ayurvedic medicines and employed as an antiseptic, aromatic and carminative. The thymol, which is crystallized from oil, is sold in Indian market as ajowan ka-phool or sat-ajowan. Valued for medicinal value. Ajowan is used as traditional around heals and have hypotensive proportion.

Chemistry:
Major quality components: Ajowan has its characteristic odour and taste due to the presence of the essential oil (2-4%). Twenty seven compounds are identified in the seed of which thymol (61%) is present in greatest quantity. Ajowan oil is colourless or brownish yellow liquid possessing a characteristic odour of thymol and sharp burning taste. Principal constituents of the oil are phenols, mainly thymol (35-60%) and some carvacrol. Thymol easily crystallises from the oil and is solid in market as ajowan-ka-phool or sat-ajowan. Ajowan oil has long being used in the principal source of thymol.

Composition of volatile oil, which determine the odour and flavour: phenols, terpens and p-cymene. Seed yields 2-4% oil and 26% fatty oils. Characteristic odour of ajowan oil is due to the high content of thymol.

Moisture: 8.9%, protein: 15.4%, fat: 18.1%, crude fibre: 11.5%, carbohydrate: 38,6%, mineral: 7.1%, calcium: 1.42%, phosphorus: 0.3%, iron: 14,6mg/10g, calorific value: 379.4%/100g (Reference: Pruthi 1958).

Economic potential to the country

Mainly cultivated in humid south eastern parts of Rajasthan viz, Chittorgarh district and Kota, Bundi, Baran, Jhalawar, Pali, Alwar, Sikar districts and Gujarat. Also cultivated small scale in UP, MP, Punjab, Tamil Nadu, West Bengal and Andhra Pradesh. Traditionally, India has been one of the most important sources of bishop's weed. Main producing states are Rajasthan and Gujarat (1).
Area under cultivation: 27120 ha.
Productivity: 211 kg/ 566 kg (Rajasthan)
Production: 10208 tons (1997-98).

Export potential:

Iran, Iraq, Afghanistan, Pakistan and India. Export amounts to 962 tons (1999) with 200 kg oil (valued at Rs 3.23 crores; for oil at Rs 1.61 lakh).
