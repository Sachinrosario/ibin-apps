Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

Amomum subulatum Roxb., the large cardamom is also known as the greater Indian cardamom or Nepal cardamom is a tall, perennial herb having subterranean rhizome which gives rise to leafy shoots and spikes. It is a shade loving plant and is grown under the shade of forest trees. The large cardamom of the commerce is the dried fruit of this genus. Amomum species are known in the North East India and South East Asian countries. Aframomum species are known in African regions of Sierra Leone, Guinea Coast, Madagascar and Tanzania. Fruits of these two genera are larger than small cardamom. Normally Amomum subulatum Roxb. is considered as large cardamom. It grows in swampy places along sides of mountain streams (1).

Climate and soil
The plants are grown along small springs, in moist and shady sides of mountain streams and along hilly slopes, at an elevation of 765 to 1675 m above the MSL. Large cardamom requires a temperature range of 14-33˚C and an annual precipitation of 200-250 cm, well distributed throughout the year(1).Large cardamom is generally grown in forest loamy soils of slightly acidic nature and rich in organic matter and available nitrogen, low in available phosphorus and medium to high in available potassium. pH varies from 5.0 to 6.0 in most of the areas (2). Gentle to medium slopes, steep slopes should be avoided. Cardamom plants grow in very shallow soil (less than 10 cm depth) but for good growth soil to a depth of 30 cm or more is essential. It is observed that major parts of roots spread on the surface soil (0-5 cm) and to cover about 90-150 cm around the plant base. Do not tolerate drought over two months. Frost is harmful but better tolerance compared to small cardamom. Wind indirectly affects cardamom by depleting soil moisture. Plant is also affected by falling of shade trees and its branches. Tillers of unhealthy plants are damaged due to wind (6).  

Availability :

Indigenous to moist deciduous ever green forests of sub-Himalayan tracts, believed to be a native of Sikkim and spread to the neighbouring areas like Darjeeling, Assam, East Nepal, Bhutan etc. Occurs in lower mountains, often along watercourses (3).

Conservation/ Threat Status : Wild and cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES: A perennial herb, having subterranean rhizomes with aerial leafy shoots - the pseudostem. Leaves are simple, short, distichous, linear and lanceolate with prominent midrib, margin entire with parallel venation and glabrous on both sides with green to dark green colour. Leafy greenish to maroonish coloured pseudostem is formed by long sheath like leaf stalks encircling one another (7).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR: Inflorescence is a dense spike 5-7.5 cm in diameter, on a short peduncle bearing 40 to 50 flower buds in acropetal sequence (Rao et al. 1993 a) (1). Flowering stocks short arise near the base of the stem. Flowers are medianly zygomorphic, bisexual, epigynous, bracteate, perianth 3+3 differentiated into calyx and corolla, with yellow-white, emarginated labellum, much longer than corolla segments. Stamens with very short filaments and entire anther appendage (3). Average life period is 20 years. The bearing period is 6-10 years with steady yield throughout (2).

Fruit
Fruit is a globose, triangular, trilocular many-seeded capsule, about 2.5 cm in diameter. Capsule wall is echinated and is reddish brown to dark pink, densely covered with soft, irregularly toothed wing-like outgrowths (3).

Breeding system and varietal improvement: Large cardamom is essentially a cross pollinated crop due to heterostyllic nature of its flowers, though they are self fertile (2).

Phenology:
Flowers from April/May to July/August. 

Pollinators: Bumble bees (Bumbos sp.) and Honey bees (Apis dorsata) are the chief pollinators. 

Floral and seed biology: Dried capsules are an average 25 mm long, oval to globose; greyish brown to dark red brown. 40-50 seeds are present in a fruit, held together by a viscous sugary pulp, and are of nearly the same size of that of small cardamom. 

CULTIVATION ASPECT:

Planting:
The best season for planting is July-August and it can be continued up to September depending on the rain. However, planting should be avoided during heavy rains. Depending on the topography of the land, the cardamom plantations are raised in sloppy lands in terraces made. In case of medium and above, the slopes are to be made into terraces and then only cardamom can be planted. If it is a virgin forest the under-growth is to be cleared and over-head shade regulated. Since cardamom is a shade loving plant, there should be adequate shade. Wherever shade is less, suitable species of shade trees may be planted. Vegetative method of propagation is most common is by splitting the rhizomes which are known as slips containing 2 matured shoots and one or two emerging shoots. Plants propagated by the rhizome slip come to yield earlier. Propagation through seedlings raised in nursery sown in primary nursery and replanted in secondary nursery in June, July and after about 12 months of seed will be ready for planting in the main yield after about 12 months. The other method is by raising seedlings in the nurseries. Well matured capsules from high yielding plants are to be collected, dehusked immediately after harvesting. Seeds are thoroughly washed in water to remove mucilage coating and dried under shade for 2-3 days before sowing. Cardamom nursery consists of two stages viz, the primary and secondary nursery. Seedlings are first raised in primary nursery then transplanted to the secondary nursery for good seedling growth and subsequently planted in the main field. Before sowing in the primary nursery land is ploughed to a depth of 15 to 25 cm, break the clods and make the topsoil to fine texture. Make beds of size 5  1 m and about 15 to 20 cm height or of convenient length and spread a thin layer of humus rich soil over the beds. Beds are prepared in similar manner and humus rich jungle soil or cattle manure to be applied to beds for good growth of seedlings. Sow the seeds in line at 10-15 mm apart and cover with thin layer of fine soil and mulch the beds with paddy straw/grass. Irrigate the beds daily. Remove the mulch when uniform germination is observed and the seedlings protected from direct sun and rain by providing shade pandals. Regular watering and weeding are to be attended and the inter spaces between the rows of seedlings may be mulched to preserve moisture and check the growth of weeds. Seedlings are to be transplanted to the seedling nursery in the month of June-July. Primary seedlings are transplanted to the beds of the same size in rows spacing of 10 cm between plants and 15 cm between rows. In the month of June-July overhead products are to be erected as done with primary nursery before transplanting. After planting the beds should be mulched with paddy straw or dry lean, irrigated regularly and kept free from weds. The seedlings will be ready for planting in the main field after about 12 months (4).  
Spacing: High to mid altitude with robust cultivar varieties: 1.5 1.5 m. Medium altitudes with robust varieties: Medium altitudes with robust varieties: 1.25 x 1.25 m. Medium and low altitudes: 0 .90 x 0.90 m. Pits may be taken preferably in straight line giving a suitable spacing depending on the variety to be planted and also the fertility status of the soil. Spacing adopted locally varies from 90 cm  90 cm to 150 cm  150 cm. The spacing of 150 cm  150 cm is found to be more suitable for better performance. Pits are to be opened in April-May, with a size of 30 cm  30 cm  30cm. After the receipt of few showers the pits may be filled with surface soil. Well rotten cattle manure, compost or leaf mould may be well mixed with the topsoil before planting. It is also advisable to put little quantity of rock phosphate, 100 g per pit and mix with the topsoil before planting.

Management after planting:
Weeding: Done twice a year, first during May-June and the second during October-November. Herbicides can also be used for controlling weeds. Removal of dried leaves, unproductive shoot etc. may be done in September-October, before harvesting. 

Mulching: Soon after planting the plant base should be mulched with dried leaves. Mulching will improve the physical condition of the soil and also will preserve the soil moisture. Mulching will also help better root formation resulting in better growth. Mulching of the fallen leaves from the shade trees is done during the onset of winter i.e. Nov-Dec. as it is very essential to preserve soil moisture in the absence of insufficient rainfall. Weeded materials can be used for mulching after it is dried.

Fertilizer and manure needs: In virgin soil it is not necessary to fertilize cardamom. But due to continuous cultivation, the fertility status of the soil will be depleted and it is to be enriched by application of fertilizers. Fertilizer dose of 40 kg N, 80 kg P2O5 and 40 kg K2O per ha is beneficial. Organic manures like compost or cattle manure can be given @ 3 kg per clump. Fertilizer is applied in two splits. First application during May-June and the second application during December after giving irrigation/receipt of little shower.
Intercropping : Can be profitably intercropped with arecanut, coffee, and to an extent with rubber.
Earthing up (Tillage) to the base of the clump followed by mulching is beneficial. Irrigation is necessary, once in every 15 days starting from Dec. to March if there is a continuous spell of about 2 months, otherwise may result in huge mortality of young plants (9).


MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
Large cardamom is infested by 21 insect pests but extensive damages are seldom observed. 
Only sporadic incidence of Leaf eating caterpillar and hairy caterpillar (Eupterote sp.) in Lower Dzongu, Phodong (N. Sikkim), Soreng, Hee, Chako (W. Sikkim), Kewzing (S. Sikkim), Assam-linzey, Dalpachand and Rongli (E. Sikkim) were recorded (2).

Leaf eating caterpillars: Cilelea plumbiola Hmp., Eupterote fabia, Glyphepterix sps.
Control: Spray with 0.05% Quinalphos or Fenitrothion.
Leaf eating beetles: Chrysomela chlorine, Basiolepta femoratum
Control: Spray 0.05% Quinalphos.
Aphids: Pentalonia nigronervosa, Micromyzus kalimpongensis, Ropholosiphum maidis, Ropholosiphum padi L.
Control: Spray 0.05% Dimethoate or Monocrotophos.
Leaf hopper: Kolla mimica
Stem borer: Glyphipterix sp. The stem borer and its caterpillar stage borers into the central core of the aerial stem resulting in the death of the central spindle.
Control: Spray Ekalux 0.1%.
Thrips: Rhipiphorotrips cruentatus Hood

Scolytid beetle: 
Control: 0.05% Quinalphos
Root grubs:
Control: Phorate 10G 20-40 g/Plant at the root zone.
Mealy bugs also infect the crop (8).

Diseases:
Seedling rot/collar/spike rot: Caused by Fusarium oxysporum
Control: Spray nursery with a systemic fungicide like Bavistin, eliminate all sources of inoculum in the field, avoid water stagnation, drench with Bavistin (0.3%) and spray twice a year with Dithane M-45 or similar fungicides.
Leaf spot: Caused by Pestalotiopsis versicolor (Speg) and P. royenae
Control: Collect the affected leaves and burn and spray a suitable fungicide such as Bavistin or Dithane M-45. 
Leaf rust: Caused by Phakospora elettariae (Racib)
Control: Spray Plantavax/vitavax 0.25% concentrates. 
Leaf blight: Caused by Collectotrichum spp.
Control: Spray copper oxychloride or Bordeaux mixture
Capsule rot: Caused by Pestalotiopsis spp.
Control: Spray Dithane M-45

Note: All leaf diseases can be controlled by spraying fungicides such as Bordeaux mixture (1%), carbendazim (0.50%), mancozeb (0.25%) or drenching with thiram (0.25%).

Viral diseases:
Chirkey - first reported during 1958.
Symptoms: Similar to mosaic disease with pale streaks on unfolding leaves which turn pale brown leading to leaf drying and withering. Flowering and fruit setting of the affected plants is considerably reduced. Affected plants do not produce capsules, show stunted growth and become sterile. 
Epidemiology: Disease is transmitted through aphid vectors – Rhopholosiphym maidis, T. padi, Brachycaudus helichrisi, Silobion aveneae and also through cell sap by implement used. Virus particles were found polyhedral of 40 m in diameter. Virus withstands dilution end point up to 1:5000 and is inactivated at 50-60C for 10 minutes. 
Control: Field sanitation and rouging of infected plants at regular intervals (2).

Foorkey
Symptoms: Affected clumps exhibit dwarfing of the clumps and tillers with the formation of minute tillers. Affected plants fail to produce flowers and as such yields are drastically reduced. 
Epidemiology: Disease is not transmitted through seed and soil. Aphids Micromyzes kalimpongensis is the insect vector identified, transmissible to small cardamom by P. nigronervosa. 
Control: Cutting and burring the affected plants meticulously. Frequent survey for disease incidences is advised. Rouging and distinction of affected clumps, destroy all the collateral host plants of aphid vector. Avoid planting of rhizome from diseased areas. Use seedlings raised from seeds as planting material. Spraying the plantation with insecticide such as Metasystox to eliminate vector population (including alternate hosts). Remove, as far as possible, are weeds, known to be alternate hosts of the vectors (such as wild cucurbits, colocasia, bananas etc.)

Nematode Induced Diseases
The root knot nematode Meloidogyne sp. affects the seedlings, making the crop stunted and with numerous grassy shoots. 
Control: Drenching nursery beds with 2% formaldehyde or spraying nematicides like carbofuran or aldicarb.
Other insects causing minor damages are: Shoot fly (Merochlorops dimorphus), Lacewing bug (Stephanitis typical), Leaf thrips (Heliothrips haemorrhoidatis) and Aphid (Pentalonia nigronervosa) (2).

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Due to the pleasant aromatic odour, large cardamom is used for flavouring various vegetables and meat preparations in Indian dishes, confectionery, hot or sweet pickles and in beverages. Seed and powder are essential ingredients in mixed preparations and spice masala mixtures. Sikkim and Darjeeling inhabitants eat raw ripened fruits during September and October months (Gyasto et al. 1980; Gupta et al. 1984) (1). Seed used as a substitute for cardamom and applied to sweeten food (3). In south India, large cardamom is used in the preparation of snuff and agarbatthies (10). 

Parts Used: Dried fruits


Harvesting Practices: 

Harvesting
It takes four months for the fruit to mature. Harvested during September-January, the peak period being late October-mid December. Panicles containing fruits are harvested with the help of a chisel-shaped narrow knife specially made for this purpose. Harvesting is done only once in a year. The individual capsules are separated by hand. Old sterile dried shoots, known locally as ‘Elaichi chhuri’ is also removed. During the third year (1st year of production), the yield is negligible, which becomes maximum in the sixth and seventh year. For the next 1-2 years the yield is steady and then gradually decreases up to the 12th year. Well managed plantations can yield even for up to 20 years (Pruthi 1993) (5). Capsules are fleshy while harvesting (72-85% moisture) and the outer layer of the capsules (echinated) can be removed by rubbing after curing. The normal conversion ratio of green to dry capsules is 4:1 to 5:1 (Roy 1998). Retention of maroon colour of the capsules is a positive index of quality (Karibasappa 1987; Rao et al. 1993 a) (1).  

Post Harvest Handling
Fruits are separated from harvested panicles for drying and curing. The capsules are dried on a mud-plastered threshing floor for 7-10 days and sold. They contain 50% moisture and are dried again by traders to avoid fungal contamination. Three types of curing systems are available. They are: 
a. Traditional ‘bhatti’ system: A load of capsules (200-250 kg) are heaped per m2 in a 25-70 cm thick bed and heated directly over a fire by firewood. It is dried for 2-3 days at 100˚C. The resultant capsules are dark and have smoky flavour. The volatile losses high as 35%. The original colour is lost and cannot be stored for long time (Roy 1998; Rao et al. 1993 a). The Central Food Technological Research Institute (CFTRI) has suggested a number of modifications upon this system (CFTRI 1994).
b. Flue pipe curing houses: Flue pipes laid in a room are connected to a furnace installed outside. Smoke does not reach the produce, resulting in early drying and better quality capsules with better colour (Annamalai et al. 1998; Karibasappa 1987; Rao et al. 1993 a).
c. CFTRI System: Low cost natural convection dryer, where flue ducts, arranged in double deck are connected to the furnace in a series. Thermal efficiency is much better, cost of drying is cheaper and superior quality product than in the case of the other systems. 
d. Portable curing chamber: This is a low cost portable drier developed by CPCRI and can be afforded by medium/rich farmers. Quality is same as flue pipe system.
e. Improved Batti system: There is an improved Bhatti system and it is made up of mud and stone walls provided with chimney and furnace. Poor and medium farmers can adopt.

Husk of capsules contained 0.49 to 1.16% anthocyanins indicating the loss of colour during drying. Treating the freshly harvested capsules with dil. HCl (0.025%) improved colour. 

Polishing: The dried capsules are rubbed with hands/coir mats/wire mesh to remove any foreign matter and also to impart shining to its outer coat. The capsule tail ends (ruminant perianth) are trimmed off using scissors before grading of the capsules. 
Grading: Grading involves separating the capsules situated at the top 1/3rd position of the spike as these capsules are usually of poor quality, smaller size and have comparatively less moisture level than the capsules at the bottom of spike. 
Packing: Usually the coal tar coated polythene lined gunny bags (40 kg capacity) are effective against the effect of the insects and pest damage during storage. 

Large cardamom is usually stored in bulk on bamboo matting spread on the ground or packed immediately into gunny bags which are then stored in plywood tea-chests. The moisture content of the capsules has to be brought down to 12-14% for longer shelf-life (CFTRI 1994). Storage for more than 6 months tends to lose 4-20% weight. Fumigation by methyl bromide (16 g/m3), phosphine (1.5 g/m3) and ethyl formate (300 g/m3) are recommended to reduce the insect pest infestation. A hessian cloth wrapping over the bags reduces the possibility of insecticide contact (CFTRI 1994) (1).  

Produce and Products: Essential oil, oleoresin, encapsulated flavour, cardamom cola, large cardamom flavoured biscuits and large cardamom flavoured liquors are some of the products developed for diversifying the uses of large cardamom (CFTRI 1994). 

Encapsulated flavour: Prepared by spray drying a blended solution of large cardamom oil and gum acacia solution. 
Cardamom cola: Prepared by blending caramel acid, large cardamom flavour and carbonating the mixture. 
Cardamom flavoured liquor: Volatile oil of large cardamom, with a mixture of lemon, lime and ginger flavours blended with distilled rectified spirit (1).


Chemistry: The chemical components vary with variety, region and age of the product. The fruit on average has 70% seeds and 30% skin (Govindarajan 1982; Pruthi 1993) and dry recovery of 30-35%.
Components – Moisture (8.49%), protein (6.0%), total ash (4.01%), starch (43.21%), crude fibre (22.0%), non-volatile ether extract (2.31%), volatile ether extract (3.0%), alcohol extract (7.02%), volatile extract (2.8%), water soluble ash (2.15%), alkalinity of water soluble ash (0.90%), ash insoluble in acid (0.42%), volatile oil (2.80%).

Volatile oil is the principal component responsible for the typical odour. Essential oil is obtained by steam distillation of crushed seeds and yields 2.5% dark brown coloured mobile liquid with cineole-like aroma having the following physical constants: specific gravity at 29˚C - 0.9142, refractive index at 29˚C - 1,460, optical rotation in chloroform - 18˚ (Pruthi 1993). The major content of essential oil is 1,8-cineole (65-80%) while the content of terpinyl acetate is low (5%). The monoterpene hydrocarbons ranges from 5 to 17% of which limonene, sabeinene, terpenes and pinenes are significant components. Terpenols comprise 5 to 7% of the oil (Pruthi 1993) (1). Other constituents include bisabolene: 2-4%, alpha-terpeneol: 6-8% and polymerised oil: 3-4%.

Quality 
Depends on 
 External appearance – Reddish brown to dark pinkish colour, uniformity of size, shape, consistency and texture.
 Flavour – composition of aromatic compounds; cineole for pungency and terpinyl acetate for pleasant aroma. 
The draft proposal for Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) by Spices Board with CFTRI, Mysore for large cardamom is as follows:
Capsules: extraneous matter (not > 5% by weight), insect damaged capsules (not > 5% by weight), moisture (not > 14% by weight), volatile oil (%) ml/100 g (not < 1.5%), colour should be natural and free from added colours.
Seeds: moisture (not > 13% by weight), volatile oil (not < 2% by weight), total ash (not > 5% by weight), acid insoluble ash (not > 2% by weight), extraneous matter (not > 2% by weight), seed should be free from moulds and insects, insect damaged seeds (not > 2% by weight), colour and flavour should be natural and characteristic (1).

Other uses: 

Medicinal uses
Credited with curative properties in Ayurvedic and Unani systems of medicine (Mukerjee 1972; Singh 1978; Anon. 1994). Seeds have stimulant, stomachic, alexipharmic and astringent properties and are prescribed for the treatment of indigestion, vomiting, biliousness, abdominal pains and rectal diseases. A decoction of the seeds is used as gargle in affections of the teeth and gums. With melon seeds they are used as a diuretic in cases of gravel of the kidneys. They promote elimination of bile and are useful in congestion of liver; also used in gonorrhoea. In large doses with quinine, useful in neuralgia. Pericarp is useful in headache and heals stomatitis. Aromatic oil from seeds is applied to eyes to allay inflammation (IPC, 14; John & Mathew, Cardamom, 1979, 11(10)., 13; Kritikar and Basu, IV, 2433; Chopra et al., 1958, 144) (10)..

Export Potential
The bulk of the production is consumed in the country and only 1/3rd is exported to Pakistan, UAE and Afghanistan (Anon. 1998) (1). Export: 1628 t (Source: Spices Statistics, Spices Board, 1997-98).

Miscellaneous Information

 Amomum subulatum is included in the section Euamomum (sect. iv) which is characterised by radial spikes, broad lip and crested anther. Twenty five species are included in this section. Further subdivision of the section in based on the anther crest. In A. subulatum anther crest is subquadrate or truncate, capsule echinate. In S. India five major species occur. They are A. involucratum Benth., A. hypoleceum Thw., A. microstephanum Baker, A. cannaecarpum Benth., A. muricatum Beddome. A. aromaticum Roxb. is the source of Bengal cardamom. A. kepulaga Sprague & Burkill is the Round cardamom or Chester cardamom or Siam cardamom. A. delbatum Roxb. from Java and A. krervanh Pierrie et Gagnipain (Camboidan Cardamom) are also some of inferior quality cardamom. Other wild relatives include A kinger, A. lingriformi, and A. corynostachium. 

Aframomum is another genus which is also regarded as large cardamom. It has 4 species – A. angustifolium (Sonn) K. Schum – Madagascar cardamom, A. hanburyi K. Schum – Cameroon cardamom, A. korarima (Pereira) Engler – Korarima cardamom and A. melegueta (Roscol) K. Schum – Grains of paradise or Guinea grains.

Cultivars: Three popular cultivars are there in Sikkim – Ramsey, Golsey and Sawney. Other cultivars are – Ramla, Chivey Ramsey, Garday Seto Ramsey, Ramnag, Madhusay, Barlanga, Seto Golsey, Slant Golsey, Red Sawney, Green Sawney and Mingney (Gupta and Borethakur 1986). Ramsey and Ramla are suited to higher altitudes, Golsey for lower altitudes and Sawney for different elevations (Biswas et al. 1986).

Ramsey – tall, vigorous with wide clump growth, grows on high altitude, 60% cultivation, maroonish stem with dense foliage. Flowers are yellowish, small, corolla tip with pink tinge at base, smaller capsules (16-30 seeds), 1.0-1.8% essential oil and requires deep shade. Susceptible to Chirkey and Foorkey at lower altitudes.

Golsey – less vigorous with erect leafy stem, bearing stout upright leaves with medium clumps, grows on low to middle altitude, 30% cultivation, greenish to maroonish stem. Flowers yellowish-orange, bold to round capsules (40-50 seeds), 2.3 to 5.0% essential oil and requires less shade. Tolerant to Chirkey and Foorkey but susceptible to leaf spots.

Sawney – tall, vigorous bent downwards with dark green foliage, and pinkish stem, grows on middle altitudes, 7% cultivation, greenish to maroonish stem. Flowers yellowish with pink tinge at base, medium bold capsules (30-40 seeds), 1.8 to 2.5% essential oil and requires moderate to deep shade. Susceptible to viral diseases (1).
