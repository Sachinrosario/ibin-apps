Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

The southern wax myrtle or bayberry is a shrub or slender tree up to 40 feet high. The bayberry is native in sandy swamps or wet woods The height varies, depending on variety and location. Bayberry needs a lime-free soil.

Distribution: Southern bayberry is found on a range of sites in the southeastern U.S. and is tolerant of shade. This species is planted as an ornamental. In India it is mostly seen in the Himalayan region, but the other species Myrica esculenta is more common.

(1, 3, 6, 7, 9, 12,14,16)

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES

The leaves are simple, alternate, evergreen, about 1 to 4 inches long, narrow, wedge-shaped, entire or with a few irregularly teeth, lanceolate, dark green and shiny above, paler and sometimes hairy beneath; leathery, evergreen, with waxy globules and are aromatic and with yellow glands below which gives a fragrant odour when crushed. Twigs are grey and glandular. Bark is grey and smooth with horizontal lenticels. The fruit is a blue-white, waxy drupe that is used in candle-making. (1,6, 8,11,15,16,21)

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

The flowers appear in early spring, before or with the new leaves. Male and female flowers are borne on separate trees (unisexual without calyx or corolla), the male flowers in cylindrical yellow clusters and the female flowers in green somewhat shorter clusters.

Fruits, borne against the stems, 1/8 inch across. The small groups of green globular berries having numerous black grains are covered, when mature, with a pale blue, lavender or grayish-white aromatic wax is microscopic rounded particles used in making candles which burn with a pleasing fragrance.

(8,11)

Breeding System :

Phenology :

Bayberry (or Wax Myrtle) is a large evergreen shrub or tree, widely distributed in temperate regions. Flowers appear in May before leaves are fully opened. (8,11)

Pollinators:

Seed Biology :

The fruit, which remains on the tree for several years, consists of clusters of round, 1-seeded, somewhat berrylike nuts covered with a whitish wax. (8,11)

Seedling Germination :

Germination is inhibited due to wax coated seeds, hence the wax has to be removed by mechanical process by gently rubbing the seeds around on a piece of sandstone, or by rubbing the seeds between sheets of sandpaper before it is sown.  Seed must be subjected to a period of cold, moist conditioning before it will sprout.  Sow in outdoor nursery bed or outdoor flats in the August to February and expect germination in the spring as the ground warms up.  If sown too late in the spring, germination will not occur until the following spring. (8,11)

Cultivation:

It is propagated by seed, layers, and suckers. Sow the plant during August-January, so that the seed gets warm soil temperature for it to sprout during summer.  Grow the plants out for a year in the nursery bed or in pots, and transplant to the landscape when the seedlings are substantial enough to thrive. (4,5,7,11,12,19)

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests: (10)

  • Bayberry Aphids: Acephate, insecticidal soap.

  • Bayberry Loopers: Carbaryl, Bacillus turengensis, pyrethrum.

  • Bayberry Scales: Superior oil.

  • Bayberry Crawlers: Copper, carbaryl, acephate, pyrethrum.

  • Bayberry Web worms: Carbaryl, Bacillus turengensis, pyrethrum.

Diseases: (10)
  • Anthracnose: Benomyl.
  • Leaf rust: No chemical control.
  • Leaf spot: Benomyl.
  • Mosaic: No chemical control.
  • Twig blight (bacterial): No chemical control.
  • Verticillium: No chemical control.

Economic Potential and Uses

Candles made from the wax extracted from the bayberry fruits are aromatic, smokeless after snuffing, and very brittle. It makes a useful body for surgeon's soap plasters, and an aromatic and softening shaving lather. It has also been used for making sealing-wax. (19)

Parts used and Uses  

Fruits: Candle wax (novelty candles because of its pleasant scent).

Bark, leaves, flowers: Medicinal value. (1,6,8,11)

Harvesting Practices

Harvesting:  Whole plants or leaves are collected during the growing season; bark and root bark in late autumn or early spring.  All parts are dried for decoctions, liquid extracts or powders.  Berries are gathered when ripe for wax extraction. (1,4,5,12) 

Produce and Products

Candles: Bayberry wax, also known as "bayberry tallow" or "myrtle wax," is the rarest and most prized of all candle waxes.

Soap: Baybery wax can also be used to make soap, using it in place of animal fat tallow.

Cosmetics: In hair care this wax softens, improves combing and is moderately substantive.

Paint: In emulsions Bayberry functions as an emollient.

Sticks: In sticks it is used for its hardness coupled with its low melting point.

(2,13,17,20,22,39)

Chemistry

Wax:

The wax (Myrtle Wax) consists of glycerides of stearic, palmitic and myristic acids, and a small quantity of oleaic acid. Thus, the large proportion of myristic acid explains the low melting point and the relative hardness of this wax.

Composition of Myrtle Wax

Nature of fatty acids

35-43 % Diglyceride (mainly of C16 acid)

Processing Techniques

This fruit wax, also known as Myrica Wax, is found on the surface of the berries of the shrub Myrica Cerifera. It is removed from the berries by boiling them in water, on the top of which it floats. The wax floats to the surface and can be removed when hardened. It melts at 47 to 490 C. (116.6 to 120.2 F.). A relatively hard, low melting, grey-green crude wax is obtained. It is harder and more brittle than beeswax. The crude wax is then bleached and refined to a light yellow wax with a melting point of about 44 °C. Four-fifths of this wax is soluble in hot alcohol, and boiling ether, dissolves more than a quarter of its weight. Four pounds of berries yield about one pound of wax. (1,13,15,20,22)

Other Uses

Medicinal Properties:

Properties: Astringent, tonic, Stimulant, emetic, antispasmodic, alterative, expectorant, diaphoretic. Leaves - aromatic, stimulant. (4,11)(4, 11)

Main Uses:

  • Diarrhea - Bayberry root bark contains an antibiotic chemical (myricitrin), which may fight a broad range of bacteria and protozoa. Myricitrin's antibiotic action supports Bayberry's traditional use against diarrhea and dysentery. In diarrhea and dysentery, use the tea as an enema.

  • Fever - The antibiotic myricitrin also helps reduce fever, thus lending credence to Bayberry's use among the Choctaw Indians.

  • Intriguing Possibility: Myricitrin promotes the flow of bile and might potentially be of value in liver and gallbladder ailments, but as yet no research demonstrates this.

  • For gangrenous sores, boils, or carbuncles, use as a wash and poultice, or apply the powdered bayberry to the infection. The tea is an excellent wash for spongy and bleeding gums.

  • Bayberry is excellent as an emetic after narcotic poisoning of any kind. It is good to follow the bayberry with an emetic, such as lobelia.

  • As a gargle it helps sore throats and as a douche it helps in leucorrhoea. It may be used in the treatment of colds.

  • Bayberry is also valuable when taken in the usual manner for all kinds of hemorrhages, whether from the stomach, lungs, or excessive menstruation, and when combined with capsicum it is an unfailing remedy for this.

  • Has an excellent general effect on the female organs, also has an excellent influence on the uterus during pregnancy.

  • Excellent results will be obtained from its use in goiter.The tea taken internally is useful in jaundice, scrofula, and canker sores in the throat and mouth. The tea taken warm promotes perspiration, improves the whole circulation and tones up the tissues. Taken in combination with yarrow, catnip, sage, or peppermint, it is unexcelled for colds.

  • Leaf tea: used for fevers, sore throats, bronchitis, cholera, typhoid, epilepsy, indigestion, hemorrhoids, externally as a wash for itching. Powdered root bark was an ingredient in "composition powder", once a widely used home remedy for colds, flu, laryngitis, sinusitis, asthma, and chills.

(5,6,7,8,12)

Miscellaneous Information

Warning: Bayberry contains a high amount of astringent tannins.  Tannins have been known to cause cancerous tumors in lab mice and rats. Wax is irritating. Constituents of the wax are reportedly carcinogenic. Avoid in very hot temperatures. Avoid if hypertensive.

Caution: In large doses, Bayberry root bark may cause stomach distress, nausea, and vomiting. Those with chronic gastrointestinal conditions, such as colitis, for example, should use it cautiously.
