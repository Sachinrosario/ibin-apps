Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:

Deciduous tree. Native of china. Found in Subtropical regions like Himalayan foothills and Central Indian hills, tropical medium rainfall areas of Indo-Gangetic Plain and the Peninsula (moist tropical), monoecious tree cultivated extensively throughout the India.

It is shade tolerant. Frost hardy, liable to wind damage, suffers from drought. Susceptible to fire and grazing damage. Grows well on variety of soils. Grows well mixed with sissoo (Dalbergia sissoo Roxb.) in irrigated plantations in the plains of India and Pakistan.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Deciduous, monoecious (occasionally dioecious) shrub or moderate-sized tree, may reach to the height of 15 m depending upon the climate and soil. Trunk is thick and strong, branching forms a thick crown. Bark dark greyish brown, rough with vertical fissures. Leaves green, simple, alternate, often three lobed, glabrous to scabrous, smooth with a channelled petiole, very variable, ovate or broadly ovate, serrate or crenate-serrate, often deeply lobed. 

Flowers are unisexual in catkins. Inconspicuous, greenish. Male spikes (catkins) lax-flowered, broadly cylindrical or ovoid. Female spikes ovoid, pedunculate. Fruit syncarp consisting of many drupes enclosed in fleshy perianth, ovoid or sub-globose, up to 5 cm long, white to pinkish white, purple or dark purple to black. Juicy, sweet and slightly scented. Round or oblong in shape but composite berry. Seed many small.

Pollinators:

Birds and jackals disperse the seeds.

Floral and seed biology:

Seedling Germination: Mulberry regenerates itself naturally from seeds which are dispersed by birds and to a limited extent by jackals and also human beings. It can be propagated artificially through seeds but cuttings are preferred since the plants raised through seeds may be different characters. When grown close in plantations, the tree develops a long clean bole. Stem cuttings about 20-25 cm long, pencil thick and having 3 or 4 buds are collected during winter and planted in January. They sprout in March and are ready for transplanting during the rainy season. Spacing 6X6 m.

 

MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:

Mildew, caused by Phyllactinia corylea (Pers.) Karst., affects mulberry leaves. In Kashmir, tree mulberry suffers from a disease caused by Thyrostroma mori (Nomura) Hoehnel syn. Coryneum mori Nomura, resulting in considerable reduction in leaf output. Nursery stock is also affected. Mulberry leaf spot caused by Phleospora mori (Lev.) Sacc. Syn Septagloeum mori (lev.) Briosi & Cav., manifests as angula r spots on young leaves. Mulberry trunk rot is caused by Polyporu hispidus (Bull.) Fr. Shownia disease, characterized by development of rusty brown patches on leaves, affect mulberry plants in certain parts of West Bengal. The casual organism has not been identified.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts Used: Whole plant, young leaves bark and fruits.

Fuel: Mulberry gives a medium grade fuelwood. 

For sports goods, mulberry timber is converted green, steam-bent and dried in the bent form. Wood chiefly used for hockey sticks, tennis and badminton rackets, racket presses, cricket bats and stumps and other sports goods. Wood suitable for boat and house building, agricultural implements, furniture and turnery, especially picker arms, bobbins and tool handles. Also useful for spokes, poles, shafts and bent parts of carriages and carts.

Mulberry wood is promising raw material for manufacture of white writing and printing paper.

Other uses:

Young leaves which have attained full size best suited for feeding silkworm larvae. Tree is cultivated for raring silk worm in Europe, U.S.A., western and central Asia, China, Japan, Nyasaland, South Africa and Tanzania,

Leaves sometimes eaten as vegetable. Also useful as cattle fodder; nutritious and palatable, and stated to improve milk yield when fed to dairy animals.

Fruits eaten fresh or made into juice, stews and tarts. They may be squashed and fermented to yield spirituous liquors.

Bark has been employed in China and Europe for paper making.

Twigs used for basket making.

Leaves, fruit, root, bark and stem medicinally useful.

Avenue, shade, roadside plantation and for wasteland development.

Miscellaneous Information

In vitro plantlets have been obtained.

Wood: Sapwood distinct from heartwood. Sapwood white to yellowish white; heartwood bright yellowish brown or golden brown, darkening after exposure, lustrous when first exposed. Wood moderately hard to hard, light to moderately heavy, average weight 675 kg/m3 (42 lb/ft3) at 12 per cent moisture content. Specific gravity 0.63, 36-46 lb./ c ft., straight grained, medium coarse- and somewhat uneven textured, moderately hard to hard and elastic. Not liable to split but warps.

A ring-porous wood. Growth rings distinct, prominent on all the three surfaces, delimited by a belt of large earlywood pores. Pores in the early wood large, clearly visible to the eye, forming a conspicuous belt or zone of 3-4 rows, mostly filled with tyloses; transition from early wood to late wood usually abrupt. Latewood pores small, usually in clusters sometimes arranged in zigzag fashion especially in the extreme late wood; vessel lines distinct on longitudinal surfaces. Soft tissues predominantly paratracheal, forming a conspicuous light-coloured zone embedding the large earlywood pores, but mostly vasicentric in the late wood occurring as thin sheaths round the pores or pore clusters, sometimes connecting adjacent pore groups, especially in the extreme late wood. Rays broad to moderately broad, distinctly visible to the eye, prominent under the lens, rather few and somewhat widely-spaced.

Warping can be reduced by quarter sawing and excluding the heart center from scantlings. Best results obtained by storing logs or quartered logs for some months before final conversion.
