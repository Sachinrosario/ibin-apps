HABIT & HABITAT :
This leguminous perennial shrub is well adapted to subtropical climates with cold winters and to very acid, low-fertility soils.

Distribution: Found in Himalayan foothills, but widely introduced and naturalized in S and SE Asia from India to the Pacific isl. and elsewhere in the tropics. Cultivated in many tropical countries, expecially so in S and SE Asia, less often in Central America and the West Indies, in W and E Africa etc.

Chemistry:
 The genus Tephrosia belongs to the family Fabaceae which is well-known to be a rich source of flavonoids besides the rotenoids among their secondary metabolites. Rotenoids an interesting class of compounds showing mainly activity against insects have also shown powerful ictiotoxic activity fish poison.  Volatile oil: L-camphor, L-borneol, Sesquiterpene lactones: parthenolide, 3-beta-hydroxyparthenolide, secotanapartholide A, canin and artecanin, Flavonoids: tanetin (6-hydroxykaempferol 3,7,4'-trimethyl ether), 6-hydroxykaempferol 3,7-dimethyl ether, quercetagetin 3,7-dimethyl ether and, quercetagetin 3,7,3'-trimethyl ether.

Other uses: 

  1. It is one of the best green manure plants and is used thus as cover crop for tea, coffee, cocos, rubber and oil palm plantations, as living fence and windbreak for citrus and cinnamomum orchards. In some Asiatic countries like Bangladesh and Myanmar and in E Africa the species is planted for poisoning fish, too. In India it is a common ornamental tree.

  2. Its a febrifuge. Displays antithrombotic potential in addition to its claimed benefit in fever, migraine and arthritis. Inhibits induction of human platelet aggregation by ADP, collagen, and thrombin in vitro. 

  3. It is used to control soil erosion.

TOXICITY

  • Acts as a potent inhibitor of aortic smooth muscle phospholipase(s) A2 in lab animal tissue.  

  • Extracts of fresh leaves cause inhibition of contraction of aortic rings from lab animals. The inhibitory effects are due to presence of -SH reactive parthenolide and other sesquiterpene alphamethylenebutyrolactones in these extracts. 

  • Extracts of dried powdered leaves are not inhibitory but themselves elicited potent and sustained contractions of aortic smooth muscle, probably because they do not contain parthenolide or butyrolactones according to a chemical-HPLC assay, “There are marked differences in the pharmacological potency and profiles between preparations from fresh and dried feverfew”; “this may relate to their lactone content.”  

  • Do not use during pregnancy and breast-feeding.

  • May interact with anti-coagulant, thrombolytic agents due to feverfew’s effects on platelet aggregation. 

  • Aphthous ulcers may result from chewing feverfew leaves. 

  • Allergic dermatitis on contact with leaves, in some sensitive patients. 

  • GI irritation, abdominal pain, and heartburn have been reported. 

  • Rebound feverfew syndrome in 10% of patients who stopped taking feverfew abruptly.

  • Rebound headaches, insomnia, muscle stiffness, arthralgia, fatigue, nervousness, tension.  

  • "Feverfew taken prophylactically prevents attacks of migraine.”  

  • Has demonstrated in randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trials a reduction in the mean number and severity of migraine attacks and in the degree of vomiting; duration of individual attacks was unaltered. There were no serious side-effects. Found to be beneficial in migraine prophylaxis as an additive drug during long-term clinical studies in migraine clinics.

    Miscellaneous Information

    It also tolerates long droughts. It is easily planted by dropping seed into shallow contour lines and covering with soil. The plants are usually cut back to about 40 cm above the ground when cassava is planted. The cut stems and leaves are incorporated into the surrounding soil or spread as mulch. North Vietnamese farmers like this method because the leaves add organic matter and N to the soil, the hedgerows are easily and cheaply established from seed, and, after 3-4 years, the stems of old plants can be used as firewood. At that stage, the hedgerows will need to be replanted or the terrace risers that have formed left with native weeds. Although T. candida seeds prolifically during winter, for good seed production the plants must be sprayed for protection against pod-boring insects.
