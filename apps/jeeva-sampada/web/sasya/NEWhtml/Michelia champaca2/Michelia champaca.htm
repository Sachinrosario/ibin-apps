Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:

A tall, handsome, evergreen tree found in subtropical regions like East Indian hills and parts of Western Ghats, tropical regions with high rainfall areas of Western Ghats, West Bengal and Assam (wet tropical), occurs in tropical medium rainfall areas of Indo-Gangetic Plain and the Peninsula (moist tropical). Eastern sub-Himalaya from Nepal to Arunachal Pradesh, Naga Hills, Myanmar and the Western Ghats at 500-1,500 m in evergreen forests. Suitable for growing in homesteads, marginal lands and village commons.

Conservation/ Threat Status : Wild/ naturalised/ cultivated

Lower risk - least concern

Availability: Large supplies available from eastern zone, mostly from Assam.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

A tall, handsome, evergreen tree with straight trunk and tapering crown; bark grey, thick, smooth, dull brown inside, the blaze turning reddish-brown inside. Branches ascending, spreading, forming a close head. Leaves lanceolate, acute or acuminate, entire, wavy margined, glabrous above (except when young), glabrous or more or less pubescent beneath.

Flowers very fragrant, star shaped, hidden in foliage, axillary, solitary, each enclosed in bud by a grayish-yellow pubescent, spathaceous, coriaceous, deciduous bract. Sepals and petals 15 or more, deep yellow or orange, double narcissus like; the outer oblong, acute; the inner linear; pedicels stout, wrinkled, marked with an annular scar round the middle. Fruit a long cluster of capsules on a spike. Capsules dark-brown, opening on the back by two valves; valves woody, orbicular. Seeds 1-12, polished, scarlet or brown, ultimately hanging on long cords.

Floral and seed biology:

Cultivation:

Natural regeneration is usually plentiful around mother trees. Seeds are sown during August soon after ripening, as they lose their viability early. Fresh seeds are sown in the nursery and 12-15 months old seedlings are transplanted. Root and shoot cuttings may also be used; stump planting has been successful in some places.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts Used: Bark, fruits, and Flowers.

Fuel: Wood good fuel. 

Heartwood is strong and durable, capable of taking a high polish, and is valued as a furniture wood. Wood used for posts, boards, veneers, furniture, decorative fittings, carving and carriage and ship building. Also used for bent-wood ribs, general joinery work, bobbins drums, toys and beads. Suitable for making pencils, particularly after impregnating the slats with paraffin wax. Suitable for aircraft construction, battery separators and tea chest plywood.


Harvesting Practices:

 

Produce and Products:

Champa oil, and yellow dye.

Chemistry:

Calorific values - sapwood: 5,042 cal., 9,076 Btu.; heartwood : 5,093 cal., 9,168 Btu.

Processing Techniques:

Timber moderately refractory but can be seasoned without difficulty or degrade, if properly handled. Logs should be converted green and then stacked in plank and scantling form in a sheltered, well-ventilated shade. Kiln seasoning proved successful but slight discolouration of timber. Liable to crack if proper care is not taken.

Other uses:

Champa oil obtained from the flowers in the form of concrete, by extraction with petroleum ether; the perfume may be also extracted by enfleurage. Champac flowers are used in the preparation of attars and perfumed hair oils.

Fruits reported to be eaten.

Flowers yield a yellow dye used for dying textiles.

Bark considered stimulant, diuretic and febrifuge. Dried roots and root bark purgative and emmenagogue. Juice of leaves used in colic. Flowers and fruits stimulant, antispasmodic, stomachic and diuretic and considered useful in dyspepsia, fever and in renal diseases. The flower oil used as an application in cephalagia, ophthalmia, gout and rheumatism. Fruits and seeds considered useful for healing cracks in feet.

Widely cultivated in gardens and temples. Flowers are used in religious ceremonies and are worn in the hair by women. Leaves - Leaves used in sericulture, as a food for silkworm.

Miscellaneous Information


Wood: Sapwood distinct from heartwood. Sapwood white to greyish-white, heartwood yellowish-olive-green to olive-brown to pale brown with a greenish tinge. Somewhat lustrous, smooth. Soft to moderately hard. Light to moderately heavy, average weight 495 kg/m3 (31 lb/ft3) at 12 per cent moisture content, lustrous. Specific gravity 0.39 – 0.60 air dry, 31-34 lb./ c ft. Usually straight grained, fine to medium textured.

A diffuse-porous wood. Growth rings distinct to naked eye, delimited by a fairly conspicuous continuous line of soft tissue. Pores usually small to very small, indistinct to the eye, sometimes medium-sized and visible to eye, prominent under the lens, moderately numerous, solitary or in short radial multiples of 2-3, open or occasionally filled with tyloses; vessel lines inconspicuous to fairly distinct on the longitudinal surfaces. Soft tissues in narrow concentric lines delimiting growth rings, prominent under the lens. Rays fine to moderately broad, just visible to eye, but distinct under the lens as prominent rather closely-spaced lines.
