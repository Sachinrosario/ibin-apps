Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

An erect, stout, tufted herb, 2-4 ft. high, resembling wheat in habitat. It is grown chiefly in the northern plains of the country, but its cultivation extends up to elevations of about 4,575 meters (15,000 feet) in the Himalayas. Its greatest concentration is in the states of Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan and Bihar, which account for approximately 52, 18 and 11 per cent, respectively, of the total area. It is also grown to a considerable extent in northern Madhya Pradesh, Haryana, Punjab, West Bengal, Himachal Pradesh and Jammu and Kashmir. It is of minor importance in the south of 20oN, although it can be grown successfully wherever wheat can be grown. In this region, it occupies small areas in Gujarat, Maharashtra, northern Karnataka and in the Nilgiris and the Palney Hills of Tamil Nadu. (20,25)

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Densely flowered; spikelets sessile, arranged in threes on one side of a flattened rachis, all fertile (6-rowed type), or lateral ones barren and occasionally rudimentary (2-rowed type); glumes 2, small, narrow, short awned, enclosing three spikelets; lemma lanceolate, five-ribbed, tapering into a long straight or recurved awn; palea a little smaller than lemma, with margins inflexed; lodicules 2; stamens 3; stigma 2; fruit a caryopsis, elliptic which is three by eight inch long, short-pointed, grooved on the inner face, smooth, free or adherent to palea or both to lemma and palea. (17,21,22,24,25)

Breeding System :

Barley is a self pollinator and the domestication of barley happened this way: Selection of non-brittle varieties, i.e. Those in which seeds were retained on the plant and not shed before harvest. Then wild and early crop varieties of barley had only two rows of spikelets where as in later cultivated forms there were six rows of spikelets.

Phenology :

An erect, glabrous, annual grass upto 120 cm. high, with flat leaves. (17,21,22,24,25)

Pollinators:

Barley is a self fertilising plant and natural crossing is very low- less than 0.5% in most types- being more common in necked types than in covered types. (13,25)

Seed Biology :

Grains ovoid, narrowly oblong, tightly enclosed and adhering the lemma and palea. (25)

Seedling Germination :

Germination Temperatures for Barley Crop: Barley seeds germinate between 3-20 degree celsius.

Cultivation: (5,15,16,25)

Climate: Like wheat, barley does best in areas where winter is cool and the growing period lasts five months. It is ideally suited to areas where rainfall is either low or uncertain.

Soils: It thrives well on well-drained and moderately rich loams.

Crop Rotations: It is generally rotated with crops, such as bajra, maize, paddy, johar, cotton, groundnut, smaller millets, moong and moth in different parts of the country. Double-cropping with barley is practiced under assured soil moisture or irrigation facilities. On soils infested with nematodes, the rotating of barley with non-host crops, e.g. sunflower, for at least one to two years would reduce the nematode population.

Sowing Time: The normal sowing season extends from the middle of October to the middle of November, depending upon the elevation, soil type, moisture status and irrigation facilities.

Culture: Best yields are obtained from a well-prepared soil which is in good tilth and completely free from weeds. However, a very thorough cultivation is not needed. Three or four ploughings with a desi plough or one ploughing with an improved iron plough and one harrowing are generally regarded as adequate. For raising a crop under dryland farming, soil-and water-conservation measures, such as deep ploughing and desking after each rainfall, levelling and bunding should be done during the monsoon season to ensure proper soil moisture for crop growth. At the last ploughing, BHC (10%) at the rate of 20 kg per ha or Aldrin dust (5%) at 10-15 kg per ha should be applied to the soil to protect the crop against white-ants and other soil pests. The saline-alkali land should be prepared, planked and left undisturbed for about a week before sowing, so that the salts move from the lower soil layers to the upper soil-zone and the root-zone becomes relatively free from salts. Unlike wheat barley needs a slightly loose seedbed. On soils infested with nematodes, two to three deep ploughings during summer at ten-day intervals are recommended to reduce nematode population.

Seed Treatment: With Agrosan GN or Ceresan at the rate of 2 kg per kg of seed before sowing and Vitavax at the rate of 2 kg per kg of seed will protect the seedlings against the attack of externally seed-borne diseases and from loose smut and covered smut respectively.

Seed-Rate: For irrigated areas, a seed-rate of 75-80 kg per ha may be used. Whereas, for the rainfed and saline areas, it should be higher to ensure a uniform and better stand. For rainfed areas, it is 80-100 kg per ha for saline soils.

Method of sowing: The seeds are sown either by broadcasting or with a single-tube drill (pora, magha or sadde), operated in shallow furrows behind a plough.

Manuring: 20-60 Kg of nitrogen. Excess of nitrogen affects the malting and brewing quality of grain adversely. Superphosphate, however, helps to neutralize its deleterious effects. For a better grain yield and quality, an application of a combination of 30 kg of N + 20 kg of P2O5 + 20 kg of K2O per hectare as a basal dose at sowing time has been found to be sufficient for the north-western plains.

Irrigation Schedule: Barley is grown either on conserved moisture or under restricted irrigation. If only one irrigation is available, its application near the tillering stage has proved very profitable. When two irrigations are available, one should be applied at the active tillering stage and the other at the flowering stage.

Interculture: The use of weed-free seed and a thoroughly prepared seedbed are essential for controlling the weeds effectively. Weeds can also be controlled with an application of 2, 4-D sodium or amine salt at the rate of 0.50 kg a.i./ha plus 3% urea in 700-800 liters of water, 30-35 days after sowing the crop. one hand-hoeing is useful.

'molya' disease

Heurodera avenae

Cereal cyst nematode

The nematode is a sedentary endoparasite, causes the 'moyla' disease of a number of ceral crops in Rajasthan, Harayan and Punjab. Affected crop shows patchy and stunted growth with pale grenish yellow plants, very little or no grain formation. Elongation of the main root with excessive branching at the tip gives a bunchy or twiggy appearance to the root system. First symptoms of the disease are visible within one month after sowing, very prominent by the end of January when white glistering female nematodes can be seen attached to roots; these females turn into brown cysts which survive in the soil for a few year, each cyst containing about 200 eggs. There are indications that two or more biotypes of this nematode exist in India.

Crop rotation for one or more seasons with non-host crops like cumin, fenugreek, carrot, mustard, etc.

Deep ploughing of infested fields during summer months of May and June, 2-3 times at 15 days intervals.

Maintainance of good soil fertility helps the plants to tolerate nematode damage.

Soil fumigation with DD 400 l/ha or DBCP at 30 l/ha three weeks prior to sowing; application of Aldicarb or Carbofuron at 2 Kg/ha at sowing time is also effective.

Some varieties like 'Morocco Moroccaine', 'BJ 191', 'B-191', 'C-164', etc are either resistant or tolerant to some of the biotypes and can be grown in infested fields.

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used and Uses

The major portion of the grain produced is consumed by the poor classes either as flour (pure or mixed with that of wheat or gram) for chapati-making or as parched grains to make sattu. It is also used to prepare malt for manufacturing beer and whiskey and other products, such as industrial alcohol and vinegar. Malt syrup is also utilized for making candies, breakfast cereals, medicines and in textile industry. Malt sprouts and by-products of brewing are used as a feed for dairy cattle. The grains are also used for manufacturing pearl and powder products, which generally form the diet of sick people. Surplus grain provides feed for cattle and horses. Straw is also fed to cattle

Use of barley is for malting and most of the malt produced is used for brewing beer. Much smaller amounts of malt are used for distilling, and very limited amounts used in making breakfast cereals, dark beers, and as food flavoring. It is used extensively in preparing infant foods, and to produce malt syrup for medicinal, textile, and baking use. Spent grains are an excellent high protein feed supplement. In Japan, barley is used as a rice extender and for making a barley tea. (3,9,25)



Harvesting Practices

The crop becomes ready for harvest in about four months after sowing. The plants are either pulled out or cut with sickles and the shaves stacked for about a week or more. The grains are threshed out by beating with sticks or trampling with oxen. (5,12,25)


Produce and Products (3,9,10,25,27)

Flour, livestock feed, and used as straw apart from uses as alcohol beverages.

Barley Malt: Made from sprouted barley ( a type of sugar). Barley malt is similar to rice syrup in that it is processed from grain, and retains about 40% of its complex carbohydrates. The remaining sugar composition is 42% maltose, 6% glucose, and about 1% fructose. It is used mainly in candies and soy milks and imparts a rich, distinct flavor. Avoid products that have been mixed with corn syrup, which will speed up the absorption of the sugars. (27)

Alcohol - is produced by yeast fermentation of various plants. Yeasts are single-celled fungi. The genus Saccharomyces is the one most commonly used due to its efficient alcohol production and tolerance of high alcohol levels. Yeasts can break down simple sugars only (monosaccharides) into CO2 and ethanol. So, enzymes that break down starch and dissacharides (sucrose = glucose + fructose is most common sugar in plants) must be added before adding yeast except for honey and some fruit juices.

Beer: Malt - dried sprouted grain of barley (Hordeum vulgare) - contains enzymes which convert starch into monosaccharides. Barley grains are germinated which converts starch to sugar and breaks down proteins to yield amino acids, grain is dried with heat which kills seedlings and microrganisms.

AIM BarleyLife: brings the juice of young barley plants—in easy-to-use powder form.

Barleygreen: brings the juice of young barley plants—in easy-to-use powder and caplet forms.

Processing Techniques

The cereal starches, such as maize, wheat, rice and sorghum, are recovered by several processes, of which the wet-milling is by far the most important. Other processes commercially employed in the manufacture of unmodified starches are the alka1i.

In all the above processes, starch is recovered in five stages: (i) softening or steeping of the grains, after thorough washing in water, (ii) glinding and sieving of the steeped grains, (iii) tabling or centrifugation of the ground mass, (iv) dewatering and drying of the final products, and (v) the recovery of important byproducts.

Wet-milling process-This process is universally adopted for the manufacture of all types of cereal starches. It makes possible a complete separation of the major components of the cereal kernels.

The raw material comes to the wet-milling plants in the form of shelled grains. The grains are screened to remove all large and small pieces of grains, chaff, sand and other foreign matter.

The screened grains are steeped in steeping conical staves in which water and sulphur dioxide are sent in. Sulphur dioxide helps in the prevention of the growth of putrefactive organisms, dispersion of proteins and in maximizing the recovery of starch. During steeping, washwater from a previous operation, concentrated to attain a soluble level of 0.10-0.20 per cent, is sent counter-currently through the steeping staves. The steep water coming out of the staves contains 40-80 g/l of total solubles and is sent to concentration plants for preparing animal feeds, mixed feeds, or fermentation nutrients.

After steeping, the grains are crushed in attrition mills and the pulverized grains are sent through a set of hydroclones under pressure. The heavier endosperm and fibre particles pass out of the bottom of the hydroclones, while the lighter germ is drawn off the top of the vortex.

The underflow from the hydroclones is sized in screens and ground in Buhrstone or Entroleter impact mills. The pulverized material is passed through screen pumps where fibres are removed as continuous mats which are washed with water, slurried, squeezed and dried. The defibred pulp, known as mill starch, is concentrated in centrifuges where protein is removed as a overflow and starch is obtained from the underflow. The starch stream is passed through a series of hydroclones to reduce the protein content to the minimum level of 0.3 per cent. In the last hydroclone, the starch is washed with softened fresh water. The starch milk from the cyclones is dewatered on moving belt filters or in basket centrifuges, and dried in flash driers.

Alkali process- In this process, the proteins in the cereal grains are dispersed by an aqueous solution of caustic soda or ammonium hydroxide. All cereal starches can be manufactured by this process. The cereal grains are crushed and sieved, the flour is slurried with an aqueous solution of ammonium hydroxide, or the grains themselves are directly soaked in a dilute aqueous solution of caustic soda in large vats. The alkaline slurry is screened in vibrating screens, the underflow is fed into a series of centrifuges where the separation of starch from the proteins occurs. The starch milk from the centrifuges is washed with water, filtered in vacuum filters and tunnel-dried. The protein solution from the centrifuges is dried in spray driers.


MODIFIED STARCHES

Natural starches are the basic source material for the manufacture of all types of modified starches. The modified starches may be classified into innumerable groups, such as thick boiling starches and thin boiling starches; or acetylated, esterified, etherified and hydrolyzed starches; or textile, laundry and paper starches and so on. The modified starches are categorized here into (i) dextrins, (ii) acid-modified starches, (iii) oxidized starches, (iv) pregelatinized starches, and (v) starch derivatives.

Dextrins

Known popularly as starch gum, white dextrins, canary dextrins, or the British gum, the dextrin is an intermediate colloidal substance formed during the hydrolysis of starch. It is marketed as a yellow or a ; white powder, or as granulated particles, or as thick brown coloured liquids, or as a white, viscous paste. Dextrins are prepared from unmodified starches and are named after the starch from which they are derived, e.g. Barley dextrin.

The dextrins are commercially prepared by two methods, viz. dry process and wet process. In the dry process, raw starch is directly heated to 200-250°C In steam, oil, or fuel heated roasters. During dextrinization, the colour of the original starch changes to yellow and brown and finally the entire mass is charred. At 150-80°C, a series of low viscosity canary dextrins are obtained, and at above 195°C a series of British gums are produced. In some manufacturing plants, small quantities of hydrochloric acid are added to the raw starch for bringing down the temperatures of dextrinization. In the wet process, dextrins are produced by acid hydrolysis, enzymic action, or by the action of Bacillus macerans on the raw starch. In actual operation, an aqueous suspension or a paste of starch is heated with a trace of acid or an enzyme and after sufficient degree of conversion is accomplished the excess acid is neutralized, the mixture filtered and the product dried. Only hydrochloric acid, nitric acid and formic acid are commercially used in this process. The roasted or the dried product is milled, sieved and packed.


Acid-modified starches

These starches are prepared by treating an aqueous suspension of starch with a dilute solution of an acid at subgelatinizing temperatures (50-55°C). The acids used for the hydrolysis of starch are hydrochloric, nitric, sulphuric, formic, acetic, propionic, and phosphoric acids. In modern industrial practices, it is usual to employ a mixture of hydrochloric and sulphuric acids. The raw Starch is treated with the acid until the desired viscosity change is obtained. The resulting slurry is then neutralized with sodium carbonate, and the acid modified starch is recovered by filtration, washing and drying.


Oxidized starches

These starches are produced by treating raw starch with oxidizing agents such as sodium hypochlorite, periodate, nitrogen dioxide, chlorine, potassium permanganate, potassium dichromate, and ozone. Commercial oxidized starches have been produced mostly from sodium hypochlorite-starch reaction, or to some extent by periodate or nitrogen dioxide-starch reaction. The effect of the other oxidizing agents on starch has been studied only at the laboratory levels.

Hypochlorite oxidized starches

A slurry of refined starch at a concentration of 20-24° Be is introduced into a reaction tank equipped with impellers for mixing the starch with chemicals. A dilute solution of sodium hypochlorite is let into the tank until the reacting solution has a pH of 8-10. As the reaction proceeds, the value of pH goes down and more quantities of the hypochlorite solution are added to maintain the original level of pH. The temperature of reaction is maintained in the range of 21-38°C. The reaction tanks are provided with a mechanical cooling device since the reaction is exothermic. When the desired amount of oxidation has been achieved, the oxidized starch is neutralized or made slightly acidic (pH 6-6.5). A solution of sodium bi-sulphite or sulphur dioxide gas is added to the treated starch to remove free chlorine present in the reaction system. The oxidized starch is finally washed in continuous vacuum filters and dried in hot air driers.


Periodate oxidized starches

These starches, known commercially as dialdehyde starches, are produced by the oxidation of the reducing and non-reducing endgroups of the starch by periodic acid. Periodic acid is manufactured by electrolysing an aqueous solution of iodine in caustic soda in electrolytic cells containing iron cathodes and lead dioxide coated lead anodes. The periodic acid is reacted with starch in polyethylene, or PVC, or glass-lined reactors. After the desired level of oxidation is achieved, the oxidized starch is washed with water, centrifuged and dried. The spent oxidants from the centrifuges are again recycled into the electrolytic cells, generating the periodic acid.


Nitrogen dioxide oxidized starches

These are prepared by reacting raw starches with liquid nitrogen dioxide in carbon tetrachloride at room temperature, or with the vapours of nitrogen dioxide, or with a mixture of sodium nitrite and nitric acid. Low temperature oxidation with a high concentration of nitric acid promotes carbonyl group formation, while the exactly opposite conditions give rise to non-uronyl carboxyl group formation.


Pregelatinized starches

This group of modified starches includes a variety of starches obtained by precooking natural, modified or derivated starches on hot rolls with or without the addition of chemical reagents. The starches have the common property of marked swelling on contact with cold water, and form colloidal dispersions reflecting the characteristics of the raw materials from which they are

derived.


Starch derivatives 

The term starch derivatives is applied to compounds evolved by the reaction of starch with other chemicals. The derivatives of starch outlined in this section include acetates, phosphates, carbamates and sulphonates of starch, hydroxyethyl starch, other types of esters and ethers, cationic starches, and cross-linked starches.


Other Uses

Medicinal uses: (17,24)

1)Barley is cooling, sweetish, acrid; aphrodisiac; causes constipa- lion;' useful in bronchitis, biliousness, asthma; appetiser; fattening; improves the voice; good for ulcers, burns, anemia, urinary discharges (Ayurveda).

2)It is tasteless; lowers the pulse; allays thirst; useful in biliousness, bronchitis, headache, pains in the chest, inflamed gums,fevers (Yunani).

3)It is demulcent, and easy for digestion, and is for these reasons used in the dietary of the sick. A powder of the parched grains is much employed in the form of a gruel in cases of painful and atonic dyspepsia.

4)In Patna, the ashes of the leaf are employed in the formation of cooling sherbets. The ashes of the stalks are prescribed for indigestion in the plains of the Punjab.

5)In European practice, barley water, a decoction of the grain, is principally prescribed, and is valuable in cases requiring demulcent treatment.

6)Preparations of malt have acquired some reputation of late years in Europe and America', since they are more demulcent and nutritious than those of the unmalted barley.

7)The germinated barley or malt with the radicle attached to it is used in China and Malaya as peptic, stomachic, lenitive, demulcent. and expectorant. It enters in a number of prescriptions given for infantile complaints.

Miscellaneous Information

Extracts of malted barley are produced as food ingredients, as a source of flavor, enzymes, sweetener, and color. Because pure malt extract, which is relatively expensive, can be adulterated with corn syrup.

A small amount of barley is pearled in Canada each year for use in soup and in the manufacture of baby food. Some barley flour is produced as a by-product of pearling, and is used in the preparation of breakfast cereals and baby foods. A market is emerging for the fractionated components of barley kernels for pharmaceuticals.

Other products: (3)

Beer

Beers have been made for at least 6000 years. High quality beer has three basic ingredients: barley malt, hops, and water. Adjuncts are used extensively in cheap beers.

Malt - dried sprouted grain of barley (Hordeum vulgare) - contains enzymes which convert starch into monosaccharides. Barley grains are germinated which converts starch to sugar and breaks down proteins to yield amino acids, grain is dried with heat which kills seedlings and microrganisms.

Hops - Humulus lupus - Cannabaceae, dioecious vines, bracts of female flowers contain volatile oils which flavor beer and enzymes which coagulate proteins making a clear liquid.

Adjuncts - alternative starch sources - unmalted grains, corn grits, corn syrup, potatoes, more commonly used in U.S. than in Europe.

BREWING - adjuncts + malt + water = mash, the liquid portion of the mash is wort, wort is boiled, hops are added and removed, wort is sent to fermentation tank and yeast are added. The type of beer produced can be controlled by the strain of yeast and temperature. "Green" beer is allowed to age for 2 to 3 weeks, then is normally pasteurized and filtered and then must be recarbonated. Brewing is much more automated and controlled than wine production.
