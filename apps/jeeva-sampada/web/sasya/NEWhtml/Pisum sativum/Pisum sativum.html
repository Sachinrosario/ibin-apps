HABIT & HABITAT

Field pea is an annual herbaceous plant and the stems grow to a length of 2 to 4 ft, Prefers mild climate, tolerates light frost. Field pea (Pisum sativum L.), a native of Southwest Asia and is grown in Europe, China, India and North America. (1, 2, 3, 9,10,13,14,15,18,19)

Availability :  

Status :Cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
The plant grows to a height of 2 to 4 ft. A leaf consists of one to three pairs of leaflets with a terminal, branched tendril. Leaves are pale green with a whitish bloom on the surface. At maturity, the plant is a prostrate vine. (1, 2, 3, 9,10,13,14,15,18,19)

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Field pea is of the indeterminate (climbing) type or determinate (bush or dwarf) type. Flowers are borne on racemes arising in the axils of the leaves and are highly self-pollinated. In most varieties, the blossoms are reddish-purple or white. (1, 2, 9,10,19) 

Breeding System : (1, 2, 8,9,13,18,23)

The cultivated peas probably originated from a wild type which is found from the Mediterranean to Tibet. This wild pea (Pisum elatius) has tough pods when immature which split open on maturity, and small black seeds, only 3-5 mm in diameter. First the field pea arose from the wild pea by mutation, and from this arose the white-flowered Garden pea. Peas were probably already used as cultivated plants around 4000 BC in Near Asia. Directed selection in the choice of seeds led to a continual increase in size of the fruit and seed. Garden peas are divided into green peas (round smooth seeds) and marrowfats (seeds with wrinkled skins). Marrowfats with high amylose content are grown for starch production.

Pea (Pisum sativum L.) - diploid chromosome number 2n=14, haploid n=7- belongs to the family Leguminosae.

Phenology:

A cool growing season is necessary for optimum field pea production (a mean temperature of 55° to 65°F). Hot weather during flowering causes the flowers to blast which results in reduced seed set. (1, 2)

Pollinators:

The crop is highly self pollinating, hence the pollinators do not have any role.  (1, 2)

Seed Biology :

Pods (seeds) are about three inch long and contain four to nine seeds. Seed may have a green, yellow or cream colored seed coat and are classified as such. The fruit of the snowpea is a legume, dehiscent and dry at maturity.  (1, 2,9,10,14,19)

Seedling Germination :

Pea seed germination rate increases with increasing temperature, but at temperatures greater than 64°F, the percentage of seeds germinating decreases. The percentage of seedlings that emerge is dependent not only on germination, but on the soil environment. Seed and soil-borne pathogens may have a major effect on emergence. Seed treatment with the fungicide, Baytan, can significantly improve emergence.  (1, 2)

Cultivation:  

Soil: Field pea can be grown on a wide range of soil types, from light sandy loams to heavy clays, but in any soil there must be good drainage as field pea does not tolerate soggy or water-soaked conditions. The soil pH optimum is 5.5 to 6.5.


Seedbed Preparation: Field pea grows best when planted into a seedbed with a minimum amount of residue on the soil surface. In order to obtain good soil contact with the seed, seedbeds should be firm and well worked. Avoid seedbeds with large clods and do not work the soil too fine, or subsequent soil crusting following rains could cause emergence problems. Cultivating the soil prior to planting aids in weed control and helps warm the soil.

 

Seeding Date: Being a cool season crop, field pea cannot tolerate hot weather or drought stress during flowering, thus seeding early is important.

 

Seed treatment with nitrogen fixing bacteria: The specific bacterial association for nitrogen fixation in field pea and lentils is with the bacterium Rhizobium leguminosarium. If field pea is to be grown in a field for the first time, or have not been grown recently, inoculation of the seed with the proper Rhizobium, prior to planting, may increase nodulation. Inoculation may also be beneficial if the soil pH is below 5.7. Once inoculated, seed should be kept out of direct sunlight and planted as soon as possible because the inoculant will die if subjected to sunlight or drying.

 

Method and Rate of Seeding: Seed field pea with a grain drill 1 to 2 1/2 inch deep in rows 6 to 7 inch apart. Care must be taken to properly adjust the grain drill to prevent cracking the seed (cracked seed will not germinate). Rate of seeding depends on the variety to be planted. Large-seeded varieties, such as Century should be seeded at a rate of 190 lb/acre. On average, a stand count of nine plants/sq ft is desirable. Field pea is not a strong competitor, therefore, poor germination or sowing at less than recommended rates may result in severe weed problems.

Fertility and Lime Requirements: The crop has a high requirement for mineral nutrients like P, K, Ca, Mg, and S. Being a leguminous crop, its nodulated roots meet the N requirement by fixing atmospheric N and converting it into available form.

Weed Control: Weed competition may severely reduce yield of field pea. Heavy weed infestations should be controlled by cultural or chemical measures prior to rotating into field pea, and prior to planting.

Mechanical control:

Harrowing immediately after seeding, will destroy newly emerged shallow seeded annual grasses and broadleaves. Cultivation should be avoided during pea emergence and for several days after emergence to permit rooting and stand establishment. If post-emergence harrowing is necessary, it should be done when field pea are in the 4 to 6-leaf stage and should be viewed as a method of last resort for weed control.

Chemical control:

  • Pre-plant incorporated (PPI): Trifluralin can be applied at a rate of 0.5 qt/acre, before planting, for the control of annual grasses and many broadleaf weeds. Clomazone can be applied at a rate of 1 pt/acre prior to planting.

  • Preemergence herbicides: Alachlor can be applied at a rate of 2 qt/acre. Propachlor can be applied at a rate of 4 qt/acre and is used to control many annual grasses.

  • Post-emergent herbicides: Bentazon can be applied posternergence at a rate of 0.75 to 1.0 qt/acre. Apply when annual broadleaves are small and actively growing, but only after three pairs of pea leaves (usually four nodes) are present. Do not include crop oil in spray mixture.
    MCPB can be applied at rates of 1 to 2 qt/acre.
    Paraquat 1.511 (Grarnoxone) can be applied at a rate of 3 to 5 pt/acre before or after seeding but before pea emergence. Paraquat is a non-selective herbicide and will injure newly emerging field pea if they come in control with this herbicide.
    Roundup 3E (glyphosate) can be applied at a rate of 2 qt/acre before or after seeding but before crop emergence. Roundup, like paraquat, is a non-selective herbicide so it is important to not contact the crop. Roundup provides excellent control of perennial weeds.

Cropping system: Field pea grown in a mixture with oat, barley or triticale yields more dry matter per acre than a straight pea culture and the field pea stand more erect which makes the crop easier to harvest. A mixture of 2/3 field pea and 1/3 oat can be seeded with alfalfa or clover as a companion crop. The highly populated stand reduces weed competition, allows for one or two alfalfa cuttings following the pealage harvest.  (1, 2,8,10,14)

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES

Pests: (1, 2,10) 

Stemfly

Ophiomyia phaseoli bores inside the stem causing wilting of plants. It was observed that crop sown during later part of November or early part of December was less damaged as compared to crop sown in October in North India. Soil application of phorate granules for its control is recommended. Effective control of stemfly with carbofuran and aldicarb, both applied at 2 kg a.i./ha. Seed treatment with 4% carbofuran granules has also been recommended for its control.

Leafminer

Phytomyza atricornis Meigen is one among the pests attacking peas and is predominant all over India. Good control of pea leafminer with sprays of methyl demeton, phosphamidon, fortnightly sprays of phosphamidon and monocrotophos, application of aldicarb followed by two sprays of monocrotophos, application of 0.5 kg a.i./ha either carbofuran or phorate at sowing and followed by another round of 0.5% kg a.i./ha applied 40 days after germination is effective in controling the pea leafminer.

Other Pests

Pea aphid, Acyrthosiphon pisum (Harris); whitefly, Bemisia tabaci (Genn); leafhoppers, Empoasca kerri Pruthi; thrips, Caliothrips indicus (Bag.) and stem borer, Grapholita (Laspeyresia) torodelta (Meyrick) have been reported on peas. Application of granular insecticides should be effective for the control of these pests.

Diseases: (1, 2,7,10,20) 

Fungal diseases of Pea:

  • Powdery Mildew (Erysiphe polygoni DC) :

Powdery Mildew can infect any part of the plant above the grown level including skin and pods. Infection first appears on the leaves as darkened areas which later become white powdery spots that enlarge and may coalesce to nearly cover the entire leaf area. Affected area retain a white powdery appearence. Severly diseased leaves may become chlorotic and distorted before falling. Pods may also be reduced in size, misshapen and plant growth reduces by defoliation. The fungus is capable of attacking different leguminous hosts and survives in conidia or perithicial form.

Destruction of diseased material and maintaining good sanitary conditions in field are important to minimise disease incidence. Effective control of the disease has been achieved by fungicical sprays. The recommended fungicides are bavistin (0.1%), benlate (0.01%), elosal (0.5%), karathane (0.1 %), bavistin (0.02%), thiovit (0.5%), calixin (0.05%), sulfex and wettable sulphur (3 kg/ha) and karathane (0.02%).

  • Rust (Uromyces fabae (Persoon) de bary):

This is an autoceious fungus and the symptoms are similar to bean rust). Under severe disease conditions, the plants dry up. Humid weather conditions favour disease development. The pathogen persists in crop debris in the soil. Some weed hosts also harbour the pathogen.

Cultural operations like crop rotation, destruction of weed hosts and affected plants and crop residues in the field are recommended. Rust disease has been successfully controlled with fungicidal applications using dikar (0.2%), bavistin (0.1%), benomyl (0.1%), calix in (0.1%), bayleton (0.025%), dithane M-45 (0.2%) and calixin (0.1%).

  • Root Rots

Root rots are caused by several specific soil fungus. Several fungi cause root rot on same plant. These root rot fungi persist in soil and residue from diseased plants. Excessive soil moisture favour root rots.

  • Fusarium root rot (Fusarium solani f. sp. pisi) : Disease appears on the stem below the soil line. Affected stem area turns reddish to dark brown. The stem inside sometime turns brick red. Crop Rotation and Seed treatment with bavistin (2 g/kg seed) before sowing can control disease.

  • Rhizoctonia root rot (Rhizoctonia solani Kuhn.): Symptoms are characterized by seed rot and water soaked stem rot. Crop Rotation and Seed Treatment with bavistin (3 g/kg seed) and vitavax (6 g/kg seed) showed good control.

  • Phythium root rot (Pythium aphanidermatum (Eds) Fitz; Pythium debaryanum Hesse; Pythium ultimum) : Symptoms are characterized by colourless to dark brown wet rot near the soil line. Young plants and some old plants are also killed. Control by good soil drainage and copper fungicides, bordeaux mixture (1 %), copper oxychloride (0.4 %) reduces disease incidence.

  • Fusarium wilt (Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. Pisi):

Wilt is detected by the appearance of yellowing of lower leaves and stunting of plants. Leaf margins curl downwards with incomplete filling of pods. Stem tissue is discoloured turning to orange brown. In some cases, the entire plant wilts and the stem shrivels. Wilt disease spreads through seed and soil. Optimum temperatures for the development of the disease is 25-28°C and is destructive in wet soils.

Since wilt is soil borne, a long crop rotation is recommended. Seed treatment with carbendazim (bavistin 2 g/kg seed) and spraying with bavistin (0.1%). Fair amount of disease control has been reported with soil amendments (millet straw) and ammonium nitrate. A low incidence of wilt disease has been established in late sown crop with irrigation at 21 days interval and application of phosphate.

  • Ascochyta Blight (Ascochyta pisi Lib.):


On leaves the fungus produces circular to oval spots that are dark brown with white to light brown centers. The infected lesions may dessicate and fall to produce a shot hole, but lesions may also coalesce and cause severe to complete defoliation. Stem lesions are also circular to elongate. Symptoms on pods are mostly circular and dark coloured and sunken. Severally affected pods shrivel and seeds may not either develop or markedly reduce in size and shrivel. The fungus is seed borne as well as soil borne in infected crop residue. The disease is favoured by high moisture level and relatively low temperature (20-22°C).

Effective cultural operations involving crop rotation and destruction of diseased plant refuse in the field are recommended. Since the disease is seed borne, seed treatment is also suggeted (thiram, dithane M-45 2 g/kg seed).


The other diseases reported on pea are white rot (Sclerotinia sclerotiorum (Lib) de Bary), grey Mould (Botrytis cinerea Pers ex. Fr.), scab (Cladosporium pisicolum), and blight (Alternaria J,ternata) (Fr.) Keissler.

Bacterial diseases of Pea:

  • Bacterial Blight of Pea:

Disease was observed for the first time in severe form during 1969-70 in Udaipur, Rajasthan. Recently, its outbreak has been reported in Kullu valley (Sood, 1989) where its incidence varied from 80 to 100%.

Symptoms: Disease affects all the aerial parts of plant. Spots on leaflets are round, oval or irregular, 2-5mm in diameter and reddish brown with translucent center and a darker brown margin. Several such spots may coalesce to produce a blighted appearance. Chocolate brown linear streaks are observed on the stem and petiole. Later on, the whole stem turn chocolate brown and shriveled. Sometimes, severely infected plants are killed. Tender pods are chocolate brown, thin, twisted and shriveled. Lesions are large on older pods and they become thin, twisted and dry. Seeds are discoloured and shriveled. Dried bacterial ooze makes pod surface glossy.

Pathogen: Causal pathogen, Pseudomonas syringe pv. pisi (Sackett) Young, Dye and Wilkie, is gram negative rod with rounded ends, 1.5-1.75 x 0.5-0.75 11m in size and motile with 1-4 polar flagella.

Disease cycle, epidemiology and control are not reported. It is suspected that the disease is introduced in Kullu valley through seed.

Another bacterium, P. syringae pv. syringae has been noticed on vegetable pea crop in Solan, Kullu, Mandi, Sirmaur districts of Himachal Pradesh during Feb.- March 1988. Disease symptoms produced by the bacterium is almost similar to bacterial blight.

  • Soft Rot of Pea:

A bacterial soft rot of green pea pods has been noticed for the first time in 1949 in the market of Delhi.

Symptoms: Pods show yellow discolouration while the seeds within are transformed into a mushy, maladorus bacterial mass.

Pathogen: Bacterium is described as Erwinia sp. Bacterium infects potato, carrot, cucumber, tomato, chilli, radish, beet root, egg plant, cauliflower and other vegetables under antificial inoculation.

Viral and Mycoplasmal diseases of Pea:

  • Pea Mosaic:

Symptoms:The diseased plants are pale, weak and dwarfed. The young leaves and stipules show a general mottling whereas the older ones display distinct irregular whitish areas. The tendrils are curled abnormally and the apical leaves remain folded. Invariably small specks of light brown discoloured areas are seen on petioles, stems and tendrils. The diseased plants flower later than the healthy ones and the flowers are smaller in size and fewer in number. Comparatively fewer pods are set on diseased plants and they are often smaller in size and contain a few seeds.

Transmission:The virus is mechanically sap transmissible. No seed transmission has been found.

Insect Vector:Six species of aphids, viz., A. craccivora Koch., A. gossypii Glov., A. evonymi Fabr., M. persicae Sulz., R. pseudobrassicae (Davis) and R. maidis transmit the virus.

Host Range:Pisum sativum L. var. arvense, Vicia faba L., V. arbonensis L., V. sativa L., V. binesis, Lathyrus odoratus L., L. ochrus, L. sativus, Trigonelfa foenum-graceum L., T. corricufata, Grotolaria juncea L., Lupinus,augustifolius L., L. a/bum L., Gicer arietinum L. and Trifolium subterraneum. G. amaranticolor Coste and Reyn produces local lesions on inoculated leaves.

Causal Virus:This disease is caused by marble pea mosaic strain of pea virus 2A.

Control:No information is available. Nevertheless, some of the cvs. Canners Perfection, Horal, Hundred Fold and Little Marval are immune to this virus. The varieties Dwarf and Kelvedon Wonder are found to be highly resistant.

  • Pea Seed-Borne Mosaic

Symptoms:The important symptoms are mild to severe mosaic; in chronic infection, plants are pale, have deformed and reduced leaf lamina. In most cases, leaves have deep detention followed by marginal rolling and leaf curling. Flowering is delayed/reduced and consequently pods are few, small and distorted.

Transmission:The virus is sap transmitted.

InsectVectors:Aphids,Acyrthosiphumpisu,M. persicae andA. craccivora, transmit the virus.

Seed Transmission: 20 -40 per cent.

Control: Selection of seeds from apparently virus-free plants.


  • Pea Top Necrosis:

Symptoms:The infected plants exhibit withering of the top (top necrosis and greyish brown discolouration of leaves, petioles and stems). Internally, the host tissues are highly necrosed. Severely infected pea plants die premature, whereas plants with mild infection develop malformed pods with wrinkles or even abortive seeds.

Transmission: The virus is transmitted by mechanical sap inoculation and also by wedge or cleft grafting.

Vector: Nematode, Trychodorus sp, transmit the virus.

Seed Transmission: The virus is transmitted through seed up to 13-14 per cent.

Some varieties are resistant to powder mildew, fusarium wilt, pea enation virus and other diseases.

Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used and Uses  

Seed (pod):

  • The smooth, green- and yellow-seeded varieties are used for human consumption as dry split field pea.

  • Field pea seed is used for starch extraction.

  • Field pea are used as protein concentrates for livestock and are popular pigeon feeds.

  • The use of Pea as functional ingredients in the food industry - green peas as vegetable (fresh, frozen, preserved) or mixed with potatoes or minced meat and the mature peas cooked for soup, broth . The viscosity of slurried pea flours makes them useful in aqueous food systems . The dried grains are used whole, split, or ground into flour.

Leaves and seeds:

  • Field pea may be grown as a forage crop, for hay, pasturage or silage. Protein content and feeding value of the forage is increased-by the addition of peas to the seeding mixture. The cream-colored varieties are used for feed or as seed for forage production .

  • As a green manure crop (whole crop), field pea returns approximately 25 lb/acre of nitrogen (N) to the soil.

Shoot:

  • The tender shoots of field pea may be cut and used as salad greens.   (1, 2,10,12,13,14) 

Harvesting Practices  

When the crop is planted for marketing as canned peas or fresh green pods, the pods should be harvested when they are fully filled but before physiological maturity. When the crop is grown for seed, the plants and pods should be left in the field to finish drying before harvesting. Seed moisture should be around 10%. The coloring may bleach out of the seeds if pods lay on the moist ground for long periods. Bleaching of seed is undesirable and will reduce seed quality.

Storage: Like lentil, grain infestation usually starts in the field and spreads rapidly in stores. All storage structures or bins should be treated with Malathion before grains are stored. The stored crop needs to be inspected periodically and fumigated when bruchid infestation is found. (1, 2,10) 

Produce and Products  

  • Pea flour: Field pea flour is valued not only as a vegetable protein source but also, in part, due to its unique functional properties.

  • Pea starch: Uses of pea starch:

(a) Use of this starch lowers the glycemic index of foods.

(b) The starch has unique gelatinization properties (no breakdown at elevated temperatures); consequently, the starch can be used at a reduced concentration to lower the caloric content in soups, gravies.

(c) The starch has been found by us to prevent textural staling (compressibility) and shrinkage in a variety of baked products.

(d) The starch has a potential use in non-food products (such as degradable plastics), due to its mechanical strength and low granule size.

  • Pea protein: Used as concentrates in animal feed. (1, 2,3)

Processing Techniques  

wet processing and dry processing dry peas into such products as - pea flour, pea starch, and pea protein concentrate (up to 50% protein).

The hulls (seed coats) are finely ground for use in high fiber bread. In the wet processing method the pea cotyledons were finely ground and slurried with five parts water. Lime was added to raise the pH to 9.8 and then the slurry was centrifuged to yield a high protein supernatant and starch solids. The supernatant was spray dried or drum dried to produce a protein concentrate (ca 60% protein). This pea protein concentrate had a fairly bland flavor since most of the volatile pea flavor components "flashed-off" during the drying process. The primary disadvantages of the wet process are the high cost of drying and the cost of coping with large volumes of effluent. The starch is dried and has various uses such as a desliming agent in the potash industry, starch adhesives, and in carbonless paper where pea starch has certain desirable characteristics.

A lower cost product can be produced by dry processing of the field pea. Fine grinding of the cotyledons and air classifying of the pea flour resulted in the separation of pea protein concentrate (ca. 50% protein) and pea starch (5 to 8% protein). Remilling of the pea starch can reduce protein content to 2 to 3%. (3,35)

Process:

Roller-milled pea flour (reduction rolls). The system is applicable to smooth peas (yellow and green) and to wrinkled peas. The roller milled powder was then separated by simple sieving into three fractions: (a) passing a 86 .mu.m sieve, (b) retained on a 86 .mu.m sieve, and (c) retained on a 230 .mu.m sieve. The sieved fractions differed in protein contents by about 5% (lowest in the below 86 .mu.m fraction). The fraction which passed through the 86 .mu.m sieve contained about 9% moisture, 17-20% protein, and <6% oil. Composition of typical products (hulls, brokens, flours, and flour fractions).

In wet fractionation of pea flours, especially from highly colored legumes, it is preferable to separate (after removing the water solubles) the two insoluble parts (prime starch on the bottom, and tailings which are just above the prime starch). The prime starch and tailings are then each purified, by additional washings and centrifugation.

The wet fractionation procedure was carried out as follows:

I. A slurry of 200 g of the below 86 .mu.m sieved fraction was prepared by mixing the powder with 500 ml water in a blender for 3 minutes; the slurry was then centrifuged at about 1500 g for 15 minutes;

II. The top solubles were decanted; the solid layer was resumed in a blender for 3 minutes with 500 ml water; the slurry was centrifuged again as above;

III. Repeat step II once more; IV. Decant solubles and remove clearly separated tailings starch (below the solubles) from the bottom pure starch. The proteins are contained in the soluble fraction(s).

Note: If desired, the amounts of water can be reduced in the second and third washings; the decanted solutions from second and third washings are low in solubles and can be recirculated for washing new lots of the original <86 .mu.m flour.
