Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:

A large, evergreen tree native to India, Burma, Sri Lanka and the Philippines, it has been widely planted and naturalised in many parts of the tropics and subtropics. It is common in southern Florida, is occasionally found in the West Indies and Central America, and in Australia is established in both New South Wales and Queensland. Occurs in tropical region of medium rainfall areas of Indo-Gangetic Plains and the Peninsula (moist tropical), in sub-tropical but also in valley areas where frost may not be a limiting condition. Suitable for growing on open lands, around village boundaries and along roadside. Fast grower, can tolerate prolonged flooding, grows well on well-drained soils, once established can tolerate drought. Shade bearer. Frost tender when young.

Tropical and warm subtropical conditions are required for the tree. In southern Asia, the tree is found growing in elevations up to 1,800 m, but in Hawaiian Islands, it has not grown well beyond 600 m. It grows vigorously in regions of heavy rainfall (from 1,500 m to as much as 10,000 m per year), prospers on riverbanks, and has been known to withstand prolonged flooding. The species also grows well in higher, well-drained land, and once established, is observed to tolerate drought. The trees are planted in West Bengal, Deccan and Konkan region, and all forest districts of south India - in the plains and in the hills, up to 1800 m, usually along river banks.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

A large evergreen tree. Mature trees are generally about 13 m tall, but may reach as high as 30 m, with trunk diameter up to 1 m. Bark light coloured, thick, rough, exfoliating. Branches terete, very pale when dry. Leaves coriaceous, variable, lanceolate, elliptic-oblong or broadly ovate-elliptic, acute, subobtuse or shortly acuminate, smooth and shining, pellucid-dotted, slightly narrowed at the base. Main nerves very many, slender, prominent on the lower surface, uniting into intramarginal nerve, reticulations numerous between the main nerves.

 

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Flowers dirty white, fragrant, sessile or shortly pedicelled, crowded in heads on the ends of laxly panicled cymes rising from the branches below the leaves (rarely axillary or terminal). Calyx rugulose externally, shortly turbinate; limb cup-shaped, yellow inside, truncate or with 4 very obscure segments. Petals calyptrate. Fruit in the shape of an olive, or subglobose, varying in size from a pea to pigeon’s egg, dark purple, smooth, juicy, crowned with the truncate calyx limb. One seeded. Seed shped like the fruit, 1-2 cm long or 2 to 5 seeds compressed together into a mass resembling a single seed, the whole enclosed in a coriaceous covering.

Floral and seed biology:

Seedling Germination: Natural reproduction is from self-sown seeds which germinate in the rainy season after dispersal by birds and flying foxes which eat the fruit. The germinating power of fresh seeds is high but is rapidly lost; the seedlings slowly develop during the first year, but the growth is rapid during the second and third years if conditions are favourable. Irrigation is essential for 1 or 2 years for good development, particularly if seedlings are exposed to sun. Young trees require 8-10 irrigations, 4-5 irrigations during may and June when fruits ripen; shade is highly beneficial in preventing the desiccation of soil and death of seedlings.

Artificial reproduction is by direct sowing of seeds in August. Seedlings are raised in baskets which are watered and kept in shade. They should be protected from frost during winter and transplanted when about one year old during August – September. Transplantation for the forests should be done, without exposing or injuring roots, under shade unless the soil is permanently kept moist.

Stump planting is found to be the best among artificial methods. Cuttings The tree is also propagated by modified Forkert method of budding, by inarching and grafting by approach.

MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:

Tree is subject to white spongy rot (Tramates personii Fr.), leaf spot [Pestalitiopsis versicolor (Spreg.) Stev.], tar spot like lesions on leaves (Neobarklaya natalensis Syd.) and sooty moulds (Diplodia variispora Died. and Meliola cladotricha Lev.).presence of Lophodermium agharkarri Tilak, Capnodium eugeniarum Cooke, and Hendersonia syzygii Hasija on the tree and Gyrothrix circinata (Berk & Curt) Hughes on dead portions of leaves has been recorded.

Caterpillars of Carea subtilis Wlk., Phlegetonia delatrix Guenee, Oenopsila flavifuscana Wlk., Metanastria hyrtaca Cran., and Euproctis fraterna M. feed on foliage. Catterpillars of Chrysocraspeda olearia Guenee often cause serious defoliation in nurseries, young plantation and even in mature trees. Batocera rubomaculata de Geer feeds on tree. Leaves are infested by a leaf miner, Acrocercops phaeospora Meyr. Tree is a host for Rhipiphorothrips cruentatus Hood.

Green bug of coffee sucks the sap of the plants which curl up; drooping of tender leaves and twigs has also been observed. Tree is subject to ‘lassi disease’, caused by Idiocerus atkinsoni Leth., in which adults and nymphs of this hopper suck the juice of flowering shoots, floral buds and inflorescences which dry up and fall off.

Freshly felled timber is damaged by 3 types of pests: powderspot beetles affect sapwood, pinhole-borers and ambrosia beetles cause stained pinholes and streaks and sapwood & heartwood borers.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts Used:

Wood considered to be excellent fuel. Calorific values : sapwood - 4,834 cal., 8,702 Btu.

Wood: Wood is reddish gray, rough and moderately hard, with no distinct heartwood. Its specific gravity is about 0.77. Average unit weight at 12 per cent moisture content 850 kg/m3.

Timber used for construction, boat building and for commercial and tea chest plywood. Also used for oars, masts, agricultural implements, tool-handles, cartwheels, well curbs and troughs, sleepers, furniture and as props for shafts and galleries in mines.

Other uses:

Food – The tree bears many oblong or round plum like fruits, generally astringent and with a flavour that varies from acid to fairly sweet. The astringency can be reduced by soaking in salt water. Ripe fruit eaten in India. Fruits used for making preserves, squashes and jellies. A jam can also be prepared from pitted fruits. Juice of unripe fruits used for preparing vinegar.

Hedges, windbreaks, and ornamental – Because of its good foliage, provides shade and when closely planted makes good wind-break. Also used as an ornamental. It is one of the most popular avenue trees in India. if topped regularly, forms a dense hedge.

Honey - Flowers are rich in nectar that yields good-quality honey. Leaves lopped for fodder.

Seeds as cattle fodder. Seeds are medicinally important.

Bark - Bark contains 13-19 percent tannin, much used for tanning and dyeing. Bark yields tannin.

Fruit, root, bark medicinally used.
Leaves - Leaves have antibacterial property. They are used to promote vomiting. The leaf ash is used for strengthening the teeth and gums.
