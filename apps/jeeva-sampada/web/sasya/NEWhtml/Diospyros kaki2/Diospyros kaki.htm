Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT:

Temperate region (1000-1500 m msl). A small deciduous, monoecious or dioecious tree. Native of north-eastern India, and extending to Japan.

Prefers mild climate. Plant is not very exacting in soil requirements; deep soils of uniform texture, preferably well drained loams are favourable for cultivation and growth. A high degree of atmospheric humidity is not required if the soil is well supplied with water. Distributed in peninsular India and eastern India.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

 

Deciduous. Trees grow to the height of 8-10 m. trunk is straight and strong. Bark is usually cracked and brown. Branching is strong and drooping. Canopy is conical to oval. Leaves are oval, green, leathery, entire, glossy on upper surface but dull on the lower side, with pointed ends. Stalk is strong and 12-15 cm long.

Back to Top

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:

Flowers are usually green with white corolla but are not visible from distance as they appear long with leaves, hermaphrodite or unisexual, solitary.

Fruits are green to orange, oval to conical, skin is smooth and glossy, 5X10 cm. Fruit stalk is short but strong and may remain with the fruit even after maturity. Fruits may be hollow in the centre but are sweet, juicy and smelling. Pulp is usually orange coloured.

Floral and seed biology:

Seed Germination & Propagation: Propagation is from seed, but grafting and budding on 2-year old seedling stock is the usual practice for propagating, horticultural types D. virginiana Linn., D. mollis Griff., D. lotus, D. discolor and D. peregrina are employed in other countries as root stocks for whip-grafting and budding and some of these species have been introduced at Coonoor.

Whip grafting is considered particularly suitable if the stock plants are small. Budding should be effected when the bark lifts easily from the wood, and bud sticks should be stored at 4-60 until required, as at higher temperatures they start to grow out.

Propagation from cuttings and layerings have proved unsuccessful at Coonoor.

Seedlings are raised from wild spp. They are sown in the nursery beds about 0.5 cm deep in November or December. These seedlings are grafted by Veneer method in September, then transplanted during winter at is spacing of 6X6 m.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:

Parts Used:

Fuel:

Good fuelwood.

Wood: Wood is of the decorative class with dense black ground colour and streaks of orange yellow, grey, brown or salmon imposed on it. It is close and even grained, moderately hard and heavy (wt. 49 lb./cu.ft.). it can be finished to an exceptionally smooth surface and with a marble like coldness tot he touch. It possesses a slightly disagreeable smell. Wood employed for carpentry work.

Other uses:

Fruits - Attractive fruit, yellow to tomato-red in colour, with a smooth glossy thin skin. Delicious plum-like taste. Good keeping quality. Colour of ripe kaki is reported due to the condensation and oxidation of tannin substances.

Tannin of unripe fruit is used for dyeing and as a preservative for wood.

Roasted seeds can be used as a substitute for coffee. As the production of seeds is very limited, their use as a beverage is not likely to assume any practical importance.

Medicine - The calyx and peduncle of the fruit are used in the treatment of cough and dyspnoea.

Miscellaneous Information

Several types of D. kaki are known. They may be classified into two groups - one comprising trees yielding sweet fruits suitable for immediate consumption and the other producing astringent fruits which become sweet and edible only after curing or after a period of storage. Seedless types are found in both groups, and the same type may be sweet when carrying seeds and astringent when it is seedless.

Four types have been tried at Coonoor - Dai Dai Maru, yielding medium-sized, orange-red, glossy fruits with a broad rounded apex, unarmed type giving large deep red, glossy fruits, Tanenashi, and Hyakume.
