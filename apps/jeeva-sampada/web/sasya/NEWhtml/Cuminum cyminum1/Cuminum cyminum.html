HABIT & HABITAT
Annual herb, slender, glabrous except the fruits. It is found to be widely cultivated throughout India.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:
 Leaves twice or thrice 3-partite, ultimate segments filiform. Umbell compound, rays few; bracts and bracteoles several, linear, ridged; calyx teeth small, subulate, unequal. Petals oblong or obovate, emerginate, white, often unequal. Fruits cylindric, tip narrowed; primary ridges filiform, distinct; secondary usually hispidulous; vittae large, solitary under each secondary ridges; carpophore 2-partile or 2-fid. Seeds somewhat dorsally compressed, convexo-concave.


REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:   
Inflorescence: Compound umbel, rays few ; Flower: Bracteate,bracteolate, linear, rigid; Calyx:  Teeth small, subulate and unequal; Corolla: Petals oblong, ovate, emarginated, white ; Androecium: Stamens 5, epigynous; Gynoecium: Ovary inferior, 2-celled, disk 3-lobed, styles 2, stigma capettilate ; Fruit:  Cylindrical, tip narrowed, primary ridges filiorm, secondary sually hispidulous; Seed: Dorsally compressed, convex-concave. 
Floral and seed biology:
Flowering & Fruiting : Febuarary-March. 
Mode of pollination and seed dispersal:
Flowers are pollinated by insects. Seeds are dispersed by wind, birds or animals.
 
Genetic variability:
The reported chromosome number 2n=14.

AGRO TECHNOLOGY:

Propagation: Plant is propagated through seeds.

Economic Potential and Uses
UTILIZATION:
Uses:
 In Ayurveda system of medicine fruits are used in aphrodisiac, anthelmintic, carminative, antidysenteric, stomachic, alexipharmic, astringent and eye disease. It is useful in leucoderma, fever, leprosy, biliousness and scorpion string. It also increases appetite and improve taste. In Unani system of medicine fruit has sharp, burning taste. It is used as carminative, astringent, vulnerary, tonic to the intestine, abortifacient, stop epistaxis. It is also useful in ulcer scabies, gonorrhoea, asthma, hiccough, enlargement of spleen and inflammation.


Chemistry:

·        Apigenin-7-O-glucoside and luteolin-7-O-glucoside isolated from fruits. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979)a.

·        Detection of a-pinene, a-phellandrene, a-terpinene, limonene, p-cymene and cuminaldehyde in seed oil by GLC. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984)a.

·        Isolation of apigenin-7-O-glucopyranoside, apigenin-5-O-glucopyranoside and luteolin-7-O-glucopyranoside. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984)b.

·        Cuminaldehyde (21.95) detected in fruit essential oil (1.4%). (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1985-1989).

·        a-, b-pinenes, camphene, myrcene, g-terpinene, p-cymene, terpinolene, linolool, pulegone, cuminaldehyde, myrtenal and 2,3-dihydrocuminal identified in volatile oil of Chinese plant by GC-MS. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994)a.

·        1,4-p-menthadien-7-al, 1,3-p-menthadien-7-al, p-cymene and g-terpinene identified in essential oil. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994)b.

·        Determination of cuminaldehyde (37.18), p-menthadienal (26.24), g-terpinene (12.17), b-pinene (11.49) and p-cymene (8.70%) in seed essential oil. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1990-1994)c.

·        Cuminaldehyde (p-isopropylbenzaldehyde) was identified as a potent mushroom tyrosinase inhibitor from cumin, a common food spice. This benzaldehyde derivative was found to inhibit the oxidation of L-3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine (L-DOPA) by mushroom tyrosinase with an LD50 of 7.7 m/mL (0.05 mM). Its oxidized analogue, cumic acid (p-isopropylbenzoic acid), was also characterized to inhibit this oxidation with an LD50 of 43 mg/mL (0.26 mM). These two inhibitors affect mushroom tyrosinase activity in different ways. (Kubo and Kinst, 1998).

·        Cumin seed (Cuminum cyminum), a commonly consumed spice was examined for a possible anti-diabetic influence in streptozotocin induced diabetic rats. An eight week dietary regimen containing cumin powder (1.25%) was found to be remarkably beneficial, as indicated by reduction in hyperglycaemia and glucosuria. This was also accompanied by improvement in body weights of diabetic animals on the cumin diet. Dietary cumin also countered other metabolic alterations as revealed by lowered blood urea level and reduced excretions of urea and creatinine by diabetic animals. (Willatgamuwa et al., 1998)

·        A pilot plant was designed and used to extract cumin seeds (Cumin cyminum L.) using supercritical carbon dioxide at 40oC and 100 bar. The extracts obtained were compared with cumin essential oil obtained by conventional steam distillation, using GC-MS. The analyses show that valuable components are extracted by the supercritical procedure, which would otherwise be thermally degraded by conventional steam distillation. (Eikani et al., 1999).

·        From the water-soluble portion of the methanol extract of cumin (fruit of Cuminum cyminum L.), which has been used as a spice and medicine since antiquity, sixteen monoterpenoid glucosides, including twelve new compounds, were isolated. (Ishikawa et al., 2002).

·        From the polar portion of the methanolic extract of cumin (fruit of Cuminum cyminum L.), two sesquiterpenoid glucosides, cuminoside A and B, and two alkyl glycosides were isolated together with five known compounds. Their structures were established as (1S,5S,6S,10S)-10-hydroxyguaia-3,7(11)-dien-12,6-olide beta-D-glucopyranoside, (1R,5R,6S,7S,9S,10R,11R)1,9-dihydroxyeudesm-3-en-12,6-olide 9-O-beta-D-glucopyranoside, methyl beta-D-apiofuranosyl-(1à6)-beta-D-glucopyranoside and ethane-1,2-diol 1-O-beta-D-apiofuranosyl-(1 à 6)-beta-D-glucopyranoside, respectively. (Takayanagi et al., 2003).

·        Three glycosides ,1-O-beta-D-glucopyranoside, 3-O-beta-D-glucopyranoside, 4-O-beta-D-glucopyranoside, were isolated from the fruit of cumin. (Kitajima et al., 2003)

Pharmacology:

·        Alcoholic extract of seeds at 150 mg/kg showed 100% antifertility effect in early pregnancy in female rats. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1970-1979)b.

·        The anticonvulsant effect of the fruit essential oil of Cuminum cyminum Linn. (Umbelliferae) was studied against seizures induced by maximal electroshock (MES) or pentylenetetrazole (PTZ) in mice. Administration of the essential oil protected mice against MES- and PTZ-induced tonic seizures. Furthermore, at some anticonvulsant doses, the essential oil produced sedation and motor impairment. (Sayyah et al., 2002).

·        Supplementation with C. cyminum to diabetic rats significantly reduced the fatty changes and inflammatory cell infiltrates. Moreover, C. cyminum supplementation was found to be more effective than glibenclamide in the treatment of diabetes mellitus. (Dhandapani et al., 2002).

·        Cuminum cyminum seed extract indicated the estrogenicity and the high dose was more potent in eliciting this response in the uterine tissue. (Sharma et al., 2003)

 

Ethnobotany:

Medicine for diarrhoea, dyspepsia, inflammation of breasts, testicles (Fruit, Bhalla et al, 1982); In child birth to accelerate delivery (Seed, Tarafder, 1984); reduce labour pain (Seed, Abraham, 1981); Stomachache (Fruit, Nagendra Prasad and Abraham); Edible as condiment (Fruit, Abraham)
