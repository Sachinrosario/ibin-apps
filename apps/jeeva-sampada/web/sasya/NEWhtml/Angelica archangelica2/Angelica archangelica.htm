Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT                                                                                                                                                     

Angelica is a perennial, aromatic herb.
Climate and soil: Grows in temperate regions at altitudes 1000-4000 m.

Availability :

Angelica archangelica is commercially grown in Belgium, Hungary, Germany and is a native of Europe and New Zealand. There are thirty or more varieties of angelica growing around the world. China alone boasts at least ten varieties (2).In India Angelica is found in a natural state in Kashmir (near water channels) at altitudes of 1000-3900 m, in Himachal Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, at altitude of 1800-3700 m and Sikkim at 3000-3300 m. Also reported from Rajasthan at an attitude of 1200 m and from Bihar. Both A. anchangelica and its related species A. glauca are aromatic and used as herbal spices.

Conservation/ Threat Status :  Wild and cultivated

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:Angelica is a perennial, glaborous, aromatic herb growing 2-3 m in height. Root tuberous, stems hollow, leaves ovate, 30-90 cm, 2-3 pinnate, ultimate pinna toothed, leaflets few, ovate or lanceolate. Root of Angelica is aromatic warm, pungent and of bitter-sweet taste. The root of A. gluaca is 20-50 mm thick, spongy, externally grayish brown with deeply longitudinal wrinkled and furrowed surface. The odour is characteristically aromatic and pungent with a sweet bitter.

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Angelica is a favourite flavouring herb in Western culinary art. Leaves are used dried or fresh as a tisane, which helps in reducing fever and cold. Because of its lovely colour and scent it is often used to decorate cakes and pastry and for flavouring jams. Angelica jams and jellies are favourites. Leaf stalks are employed in confectionery. Young leaves and shoots used to flavour wine and liquors, while the stout stems are candied as a cake decoration or cooked like rhubarb. Fresh or preserved roots have been added to snuff and used by Laplanders and North American Indians as tobacco (1).. Essential oil is used in the perfume and flavour industries. Angelica root is the main flavouring ingredient of gin. It is widely used in liqueurs like Benedictine, Chartreuse, Cointreau and Vermouth (2).

Parts Used: Leaves, leaf stalks and flower stalks.


Harvesting Practices: Flower stalks and leaf stalks are best when harvested in April-May while leaves are best for flavouring when harvested in June, just before flowering. Young leaves can be gathered any time during the growing season, the stems in the summer of the second year. Roots are dug up just before flowering and dried slowly (1).
Post Harvest Handling
Cut seed heads are dried and stored (1).


Produce and Products: Leaves, leaf and flower stalks and root oil. Oil is extracted from the root, fruit or seed of the plant. Fresher roots yield oils of lighter colour and a more pronounced terpene note. Oils distilled from older roots are darker, more viscous and have a characteristic musk-like odour. Generally expect to see a water-white or pale yellow to orange-brown coloured liquid. Oils from young roots (or from the seed) exhibit a light somewhat peppery top note missing in oils from older (2-3 years) roots (2). 


Chemistry: Angelica contains an essential oil, 0.1-0.4% in fresh roots and 0.5-1% in dried root. Fruits contain 1.2 to 1.3% oil. Oil is extracted by prolonged steam distillation. The major constituents of the root oil are a-pinene, b-pinene, p-cymene, dihydrocarvone, sesquiterpene ketones and alcohols. The roots contain 5 furanocoumarins. The medicinal properties are attributed to these compounds, namely archangelin, prangolarin, oxypeucedanin hydrate, ostsathol and osthol. These are reported to have effect in curing leucoderma. The main body note has a rich, earthy-herby, woody characteristic with a musky, animal-like undertone. Seed oil typically is quite a bit higher in beta-phellandrene (35-65%) and lower in the musk components (pentadecanolide and tridecanolide) than the root oil. Root oil can range between 10 and 30% beta-phellandrene. Seed oil is a water-white or very pale yellow oil with a strong, fresh, light peppery odour. It is sometimes used to adulterate the root oil and can be difficult to detect (2).

Other uses: 

Medicinal uses
It is carminative, expectorant, diaphoretic, cardioactive and stimulant. Powdered root is administered to children in warm water for stomach complaints and also to check vomiting. Also used in leucoderma. All parts promote perspiration, stimulate appetite, and are used to treat ailments of the chest and digestion (1). An alternative to artificial hormones during menopause, a remedy for menstrual problems, a tonic for anaemia, and a treatment for heart disease and high blood pressure. Medieval and Renaissance herbals noted the blood purifying powers of angelica. It was used as a remedy for "poisons, agues and all infectious maladies." The fleshy root, of the wild celery plant, was chewed and burnt to ward off infection during the 14th & 15th century plagues. It stimulates production of digestive juices, improves the flow of bile into the digestive tract, and combats digestive spasms. Properties of the herb (and extract) are: antispasmodic, aphrodisiac, anticoagulant, bactericidal, carminative, depurative, diaphoretic, digestive, diuretic, emmenagogue, expectorant, febrifuge, hepatic, nervine, stimulant, stomachic and tonic. The oil has been recommended for treating a weak stomach or digestive system, lack of appetite, anorexia, flatulence, chronic gastritis and chronic enteritis. It is also used to reduce accumulation of toxins, arthritis, gout, rheumatism and water retention. In the traditional Chinese model, Angelica is used for damp, cold intestinal conditions with underlying Spleen Qi deficiency, as well as chronic lung, phlegm, cold syndromes with painful wheezing. In aromatherapy, it is a germ killer, excellent for coughs and colds, flu, muscular aches, fatigue, migraine, nervous tension, stress and rheumatism. It has a calming effect on the digestion and is relaxing (2).

Miscellaneous Information

 According to Western legend angelica was a gift of mother angel to man for curing the plague. But now it is cultivated not for its medicinal properties but for its flavouring ability. 
It is best known for its green candied or crystallized stems.
