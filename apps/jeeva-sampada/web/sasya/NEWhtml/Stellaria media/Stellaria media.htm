HABIT & HABITAT
Is a small, erect or prostrate, leafy herb, found in the temperate and alpine Himalayas from Kashmir to Sikkim, and in Meghalaya, the Nilgiris and Kerala. 

It is generally found in the Cultivated fields, Cultivated fields and Plains, Fallow ground, Fallow, arable lands, Fruit gardens, Gardens and Roadsides, high ranges, Lawns and gardens, Moist localities, Moist situations, Mustard fields, On hill tops, Open forest, Pea fields, Potato fields, Road sides, Shady Places, Shola, Tea gardens, Vegetable fields and Waste land.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A common weed, found throughout the Himalayas up to altitudes of 4,300 m. and as a weed elsewhere. Stems procumbent, 15-60 cm. long, with a line of hairs running on alternate sides in each succeeding internode's, much-branched; lower leaves petiole, upper sessile; flowers white, small, star-shaped, in leafy cymes; capsules ovoid; seeds brown, acutely tubercle.

Chemistry
wild plant from Lebanon gave the following proximate composition : moisture, 91.7; protein, 1.2; ether extr., 0.2; crude fibre, 1.7; N-free extr., 3.6; and ash, 1.6 g./lOO g.; and vitamin A value, 1,000 I.U./100 g. of edible matter. The leaves are rich in vitamins A and C; a sample of the wild vegetable from Belgrade contained 32.5 mg./lOO g. carotene, on dry basis. Vitamin contents of the leafy material, recorded from different sources, are : thiamine, 0.016; riboflavin, 0.136; and niacin, 0.51 mg.; and total ascorbic acid (dry basis), 200-550 mg./lOO g. The ascorbic acid content was reported to be the maximum in young, green leaves and decreased after flowering. The leaves of cultivated chickweed contain a carbonic anhydrase, which differs from animal carbonic anhydrase in some respects (Cowan et al., J. Sci. Fd Agric., 1963, 14, 484; Chem. Abstr., 1964, 60, 2249; French et al., Bull. Fla agric. Exp. Sta., No. 482, 1951, 9; Baird & Lane, Canad. J. Res., 1947, 25C, 95; Morley, loc. cit. Bradfield, Nature, Land., 1947, 159, 467). The plant contains mucilage, and saponin (according to some workers, saponin is not present), and traces of an alkaloid. It is rich in potassium and silicon. Analysis of the plant ash (13.2%) gave : potassium (KaO), 36.6; calcium (CaO), 14.0; magnesium (MgO), 21.8; sodium (NaaO), 6.9; iron (FeA)), 2.6; phosphorus (PgOg), 7.0; sulphur (803), 2.9; silicon (SiOa), 3.6; and chlorine, 9.8%. The aerial parts of the plant in post-flowering period contain 44 mg./lOO g. of vitamin E. The seeds yield 4'8 per cent of a fatty oil (Morley, loc. cit.; Hoppe, 861; Wehmer, I, 306; Simes el al.. Bull. sci. indusir. Res. Org. Aust., No. 281, 1959, 19; Hultin& Torssell, Phytochemistry, 1965, 4,425; Materiae Rudes Plantarum II, 408). The plant is said to be eaten by cattle, but is fatal to lambs and horses if eaten in large quantities. In large doses, the plant may temporarily cause a mild form of paralysis in human beings also. The plant contains toxic concentrates of nitrates (Forsyth, Bull. Minist. Agric. Lond., No. 161, 1954, 32; Steinmetz, 11, 436; Kingsbury, 43). Chickweed may sometimes be grown as a green manure. It may also become a troublesome weed in the fallow land, cultivated fields, lawns and gardens. Spraying 2, 4-D or ferrous sulphate or CMU has given control [Hort. Abstr., 1953, 23, 584; Ram Gopal, Indian Fmg, N.S., 1954-55, 4(10), 23; For. Abstr., 1951-52, 13, 278; Field Crop Abstr., 1953, 6, 273]. --

Other Uses
  1. The chickweed is said to be very useful in inflammations of the digestive, renal, respiratory and reproductive tracts.  It has a strengthening and soothing action on mucus and desmoid's surfaces and as such can be applied direct if necessary.  It is also useful in severe inflammations of skin such as erysipelas, scalds and burns, crural ulcers, hemorrhoids, eczema and inflammations of eyes.
  2. The herb can be employed dried or fresh or in the form of powder, extract, decoction, fermentation, poultice or ointment.
  3. The plant is employed in plasters used for broken bones and swelling.
