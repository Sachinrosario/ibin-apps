Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT
Hydnocarpus laurifolia is a large evergreen tree common in tropical forests along the westren ghats from the Konkan southwards and below the ghats in Malabar and Kanara in moist situations, especially near the water. In its natural habitat the obsolute maximum shade temperature varies from 94-990 F., the obsolute minimum temperature is about 600 and the normal rainfall varies from 90-180 inch or more (3, 4, 5).

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
A tree reaching 40-50 ft. in height. Leaves spiral, simple, leathery, very asymmetric at the base (usual), or symmetric at the base, smooth margined. Leaves 4-10 inch., long, oblong, ovate or lanceolate, acuminate, entire or serrate. Venation pinnate. Secondary veins visible. Intramarginal vein absent. Stalk of blade less than a quarter the length of the blade to a quarter to one half the length of the blade, attached to base of leaf blade. Stipules present; free from one another; laterally placed; not encircling the twig; scale-like, small, not persistent. Basal glands absent. Bark is brown and somewhat rough (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY
Inflorescences represented by solitary flowers only (rare), or present; axillary or superaxillary; the flowers from a single point on the stem, or on an unbranched axis, or on a branched axis; specialized inflorescence units absent; numerous and persistent inflorescence bracts absent. Some flowers unisexual; with male and female flowers on different plants; stalked; about 0.5 cm long or across; round; with male or female parts raised on a column, or not raised on a column; with the female parts of the flower raised on a stalk. Hypanthium absent. Perianth present; with distinct calyx and corolla whorls. Sepals 4-5; free; not persistent in fruit; not growing any further after flowering. Petals present; 4-5; all joined; not fleshy; deciduous; not stalked; entire. Stamens 5, filaments present; free of each other; all equal in size; isomerous, or polystemonous; opposite the sepals (when isomerous). Anthers 2 loculed; attached by the base; dehiscing by short slits, or dehiscing by long slits. Ovary superior. Carpels joined; 3 or 5. Locules 3-5. Styles absent. Stigmas 3-5 lobed; distinctive, or not distinctive; disk-like (1, 2, 4, 5, 6).

Phenology :
The tree flowers in February-March and the fruit, which is a large rinded berry ripens in May-June (4).

Seed Biology :
Fruit globose, 2-4 inches in diam., tomentose, mammilate; seeds 15-20, 0.8-1.0 in. long (weight of the seed is 1-1.4 g each), subovoid, obtusely angular, striate. The seeds are oily and do not retain their germinative power for long (3, 4).

Seedling Germination :
Germination is epigeous. The horny testa cracks with the swelling of the seed, exposing the soft whitish albumen which encloses the cotyledons. Primary root is long, moderately thick, lateral roots numerous and fibrous in nature (3).

Cultivation:

H. laurifolia is often planted on road sides in hilly areas. It may be raised from seeds sown soon after separation from fruits. Under natural conditions, seeds germinate during the rains shortly after falling to the ground.

Economic Potential and Uses

The seeds of several species of Hydnocarpus yield fatty oils generally known as Chaulmoogra oil. Oil is yellowish or brownish yellow in colour or soft cream-coloured fat. It has a slight characteristic odour and a somewhat acrid taste. It is soluble in most organic solvents. Hydnocarpus oil is mainly used in the treatment of lepromatous leprosy and is effective in early cases in decreasing the size of nodules, anaesthetic patches and skin lesions. It is administered internally, the dosage being increased gradually to prevent gastric irritation. Intramuscular injections give better results, iodised hydnocarpus oil is said to be less painful. Sodium salts of the fatty acids have also been used. The seed oil has been recommended as a local application in rheumatism, sprains and bruises, sciatica and chest affections. The oil is also used for burning. The press cake obtained after the expression of oil from the seeds is used as manure.

Hydnocarpus oil and its derivatives are being gradually replaced by sulphone drugs, like Promin, Diasone and Promizole, which have given promising results in the treatment of leprosy. Regression of lepromatous lesions, even of advanced nodules, has been reported by the use of sulphones (2, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10).

Uses

The wood is whitish, but generally turns brownish grey due to fungal sap-stain and it is not durable. It saws easily, working to a good finish and smooth surface. The timber is used for packing cases. It is a good board wood if carefully seasoned. It is used as fuel wood (4).

Harvesting Practices
There is no organised way of seed collection. Fallen fruits are manually collected from the forest floor

Produce and Products

The seeds constitute the source of hydnocarpus oil which is by far the most important oil of the chaulmoogra group. Timber is used in packaging industries and is also used as fuel wood.

The oils of the chaulmoogra group are characterised by the presence, in predominating amounts of unsaturated cyclic fatty acids mainly chaulmoogric and hydnocarpic; lower homologues are also reported to be present in some oils. The acids contain a cyclopentene ring at the end of the carbon chain and an asymmetric carbon and are dextrorotatory. They possess marked therapeutic value, especially in the treatment of leprosy.

Major fatty acid composition of seed oil: Palmitic acid 1.8%, Oleic acid 6.5%, lower homologues of Choulmoogric acid 3.4%, Hydnocarpic acid 48.7% and Choulmoogric acid 27.0 % (4).

Processing Techniques

Kernels contains about 60 % of oil. The oil is obtained from the chaulmoogra or hydnocarpus seeds by hot or cold extraction. The seeds yield 30-40 per cent of the oil, according to the method used. By hydrulic pressure about 30.9 per cent of the oil is obtained, while by ether extraction the yield amounts to 38.1 per cent. On hydrolysis the fatty oil yields fatty acids which are mixtures of several constituents and these can be seperated by fractionation (4, 5).

Other Uses
The fruits of the tree are used as fish poison, but fish so killed are unfit for human consumption. Hydnocarpus seeds have long been used in South India as a remedy for leprosy, chronic skin affections and ophthalmia and as a dressing for wounds and ulcers. Both seeds and oil act as gastro-intestinal irritants when taken internally, producing vomiting and purging (4, 5).

Economic potential to the country

Economic Potential To The Country

Chaulmoogra belongs to the Hydnocarpus family, quite common in southern Asia, particularly in India. In southern India, chaulmoogra oil has been used for a long time to heal chronic skin diseases, wounds and ulcers. Applied locally, it also relieves rheumatism and other pains. The ripe and fresh seeds give a medicinal oil reputed to be a specific remedy against leprosy quite efficient when the disease has just started to develop.

Export Potential

Small quantities of seed oil of Hydnocapus kurzii and Hydnocapus laurifolia was exported during 1950's to Belgium, France and Borneo. The total quantitiy exported was 7, 057 gal., valued at Rs. 57,852. At present, there are no available records of seed oil exports from India (4).
