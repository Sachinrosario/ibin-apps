Other Biological Details

HABIT & HABITAT

An erect annual herb. Black cumin is the dried seed like fruit of a small herb about 45 cm in height and native of Mediterranean region. It is cultivated occasionally and often found as a weed of cultivation in Punjab, Himachal Pradesh, Bihar and Assam (1) .

Climate and soil:
A hardy crop can grow under wide range of temperatures from 5-25°C. Normally grows well in warm weather during sowing with a temperature of 20-25°C. Cold weather is congenial for the early growth period and crop requires warm summary weather during seed formation. Nigella thrives on wide range of soils which are rich in organic matter and free from water losing and an altitude range of 1500-2500 m. However loamy medium to heavy soil with good fertility level is most suitable with pH 5-8 (1).

Availability :

Probably indigenous to Mediterranean region and the Middle East up to India. It is cultivated in the subtropical belt extending from Morocco to northern India (Punjab and Bihar) and Bangladesh, in East Africa and in the former Soviet Union. In Europe, North America and South-East Asia it is cultivated in minor scale (1).
Conservation/ Threat Status : Cultivated and wild.

DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:

Pretty, small, erect herb up to 70 cm tall, with a well-developed yellow-brown taproot and numerous feeder roots. Stem profusely branched, subterete, ribbed, sometimes hollow when oil, puberulous, light to dark green. Leaves alternate, stipules absent; petioles strongly broadened at base, light green, only present in basal leaves, 1-6 cm long, ribbed, puberulous; blade up to 7 cm x 5 cm in outline, bi-tri- or even multi-pinnately dissected into short, thin sublinear, divergent, slightly pilose lobes, normally green but sometimes turn reddish brown (1).

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY/ BREEDING BEHAVIOR:
Flowers terminal, solitary; pedicel 4-8 mm long, puberulous, ribbed; all flower parts inserted on a pale yellow or blue, fleshy, depressed-conical receptacle, about 2 mm in diameter, visible as an orange-brown ring below the carpels in fruit; sepals 5, petaloid, ovate, 13-17 mm x 6-12 mm, apex obtuse, tapering basally into a 2-3 mm long claw, papillose to pilose, pale green when young, sometimes partly reddish, inside pale blue-white when older; petals 8 with short claw, blade 2-lobed, lobes enclosing nectar-pocket, dorsal lobe 3.5-5.5 mm x 2.5-4.5 mm, apex bifid, greenish-white with violet lines, ventral lobe oblong, 2.5-4 mm long, violet at base, white at apex; stamens in 6-10 groups of 3-7 stamens each, each group forming a spiraloid line on the receptacle, filaments linear, 3-9 mm long, violet blue to pale blue, anthers yellow; pistil lobed, composed of 3-7 white granular carpels, almost connate at base, forming a compound ovary 4-9 mm long with free stigmas (1).

Fruit:
Fruit ribbed oblongoid, tuberculate capsule, 6-16 mm x 5-12 mm, greyish-green to brown at maturity, many-seeded, with persistent stigmas (1).


Breeding system and varietal improvement:
Flowering is protoandrous. In older flowers stigmas bend down and self-pollination may occur.

Phenology:

In temperate climates flowering starts about 100 days after sowing. In warmer climates flowering starts in 8-10 weeks after seed germination (1).

Floral and seed biology:
Seed Biology:
Seed 3-4 sided, obpyramidal, 3 mm x 1.5-2 mm, rugose-tuberculate, dark black, with a carrot-like smell; embryo minute, embedded in copious, fatty endosperm (1).


CUTIVATION ASPECT:
Seed germination:
Epigeal germination, promoted by darkness and high temperatures.

Local cultivars :
Easily differentiated from the other 13 species. sometimes subdivisions are followed into forms or varieties based on seed colour, branching habit and hairiness, but have no practical value. Sometimes botanical division between wild and cultivated taxa is proposed, in which the wild plants are more hairy with smaller flowers and more tuberculate fruits (1).

Released varieties:
Pant Krishna.

Chromosome number:
2n=12

Selection of planting material:
Seeds are the planting material.

Planting:
Seeds are broadcasted at a seed rate of 8 kg/ha, as they are very fragile for transplanting, but may also be sown in peat blocks. A row spacing of 25-40 cm. Even intercropped in Ethiopia with barley and wheat. Planted as a rabi crop in the winter season in the northern plains. Sown during the month of October in North Indian condition. Soil prepared to fine tilth and levelled to make convenient size of beds. Pre-sowing irrigation should be given for better germination. Plant to plant spacing is 15-20 cm.

Management after planting:
Irrigation: Light irrigation should be given immediately after sowing and subsequent irrigation should be given initially at 5-6 days interval and thereafter at 10-15 days depending up on the weather conditions and soil types. Irrigation is very much required during flowering and seed formation.
Fertilizer: At the time of preparation 10-15 t/ha well rotten FYM, 30 kg N and 60 kg P2O5/ha should be applied as basal dose. Apply 30 kg N in two splits at 40 and 60 DAS as top dressing.
Weeding: First hand weeding is given in about 30 days, depending up on the weed growth, 2-3 weedings are to be done to make the field weed-free. Thinning of plants also to be done.

MAJOR PEST AND DISEASES:
Pests:
Aphids (insect pest) attack black cumin can be controlled by spraying by 0.03% Dimethoate (30 EC) or 0.1% Malathion (50 EC) at 15 days interval.
Larvae of Spodoptera litura may bring about 15-20% economic damage and controlled by applying 0.05% Monocrotophos followed 10 days later by 0.2% carbaryl.

Diseases:

No serious disease problem has been reported except a minor leaf-spot caused by Cercospora nigellae (1).

Economic Potential and Uses

UTILIZATION:
Seeds are used as spice. Whole or crushed seeds are mixed through dough or sprinkled on bread in Sri Lanka, India, Middle East, Turkey, etc. crushed seeds impart a black colour to the bread. It is an essential component of semi-sweet breakfast rolls and coffee cakes known as 'choereg' in the Middle East. Seeds are used to flavour a variety of dishes like sauces, curries, pickles, vegetable and meat dishes, fruit pies and other confections and in flavouring vinegar and alcoholic beverages. In Arab countries a few seeds are mixed with honey and taken as a sweet. Essential oils are extracted from Nigella seed, which is used in food, flavour and pharmaceutical industries. The carboxyl compound, nigellone is the important constituent possessing medicinal and preservations quality. Oil obtained by the extraction of the seeds to be used for eatable purposes. The dried seeds of Nigella are the commercial products being used in food, flavour and pharmaceutical and perfuming industry. Seeds are used as a stabilizing agent for edible fats (1). It is one of the five spices in Bengali five spices (panch phoron). Seeds are scattered on naan bread, giving peppery flavour. Also scattered in breads and cakes or sometimes mixed with sesame (4).

Parts Used:

Seeds

Harvesting Practices:
Ready for harvest in 140-160 days after sowing, when seeds have been attained full maturity (i.e., when the capsules have turned to full black colour). Delay in harvesting may cause shattering of seeds. An average of 800-1000 kg/ha yield can be obtained (1).

Post Harvest Handling:
Seeds are removed by light threshing. Stored under dry conditions to maintain spicy flavour. Under normal room conditions, they remain viable for 2-3 years. Broken seeds deteriorate rapidly, due to the activity of lipases (1).

Produce and Products:

Whole and crushed seeds.

Medicinal uses
Seeds are acrid, bitter, thermogenic, aromatic, carminative, diuretic, anodyne, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, deodorant, appetising, digestive, anthelmintic, constipating, sudorific, febrifuge, stimulant and expectorant. Useful in skin diseases, haemorrhoids, cephalalgia, rheumatic pain, headache, asthma, jaundice, inflammation, fever, paralysis, ophthalmia, halitosis, anorexia, dyspepsia, flatulence, diarrhoea, dysentery, cough, amenorrhoea, dysmenorrhoea, helminthiasis especially tapeworm, strangury, intermittent fevers, agalactia and vitiated conditions of vata and kapha (2). Seed and oil possesses pharmacological properties such as detergent, seductive, anti inflammatory, expectation antibacterial. Crushed seeds in vinegar are applied in skin disorders such as ringworm, eczema and baldness. Applied as a galactagogue, emmenagogue, vermifuge and aid discharge of pus. Pounded seeds are used against nausea and in parts of north India to induce abortion. A powdered mixture of seeds with fenugreek, Lepidium sativum L., Commiphora sp. and the dried leaves of Cleome droserifolia (Forssk.) Delile, Ambrosia maritime L. and Centaurium pulchellum (Sw.) Druce, administered as tea, is used in Egypt to treat diabetes (1).

Chemistry:
Odour of crushed black cumin seeds is reminiscent of lemon with a suggestion of carrot; taste is strong, pungent and peppery, but also aromatic and nutty. Somewhat resembles oregano. Seeds contain numerous esters of unsaturated fatty acids and terpene alcohols (7%) and about 0.5% essential oil. Essential oil is a yellow liquid with unpleasant smell that tastes like juniper berries. Main components of essential oil are thymoquinone (25-50%), p-cymene and a-pinene, further traces of carvacrol, anethole and a-terpineol.

Analysis of black cumin reveals the following constituents:
Total ash: 3.8 - 5.3, ash insoluble in acid: 0.0 - 0.5, volatile oil: 0.5 - 1.6, ether extract (crude oil): 35.6 - 41.6, alcoholic acidity as oleic acid (FFA): 3.4 - 6.3.
In US, regulatory status 'generally regarded as safe' (GRAS) has been accorded to black cumin (GRAS 2342) and black cumin oil (GRAS 2237).

Per 100 g edible portion seeds contain approx.: water - 4 g, protein - 22 g, fat - 41 g, carbohydrates - 17 g, fibre - 8 g, ash - 4.5 g (Na - 0.5 g, K - 0.5 g, Ca - 0.2 g, P - 0.5 g, Fe - 10 mg), thiamine - 1.5 mg, pyridoxine - 0.7 mg, tocopherol - 34 mg and niacin - 6 mg. Fatty oil is composed of eicosenoic acid (0.5%), linoleic acid (about 60%), linolenic acid (0.3%), myristic acid (0.3%), oleic acid (about 25%), palmitic acid (about 12%) and stearic acid (3%). Seed also contains tannins, alkaloids damascenine, nigellone, nigellimine, nigellimine-N-oxide and nigellicene, sterols cholesterol, campestrol, stigmasterol, ß-sitosterol and a-spinasterol. Further it contains 1.5% of the glucoside melanthin, which on hydrolysis yields the toxic melanthogenin (1). Black cumin oil also contains: 31.7% para-cymene, 24.5% thymoquinone, 9.4% ethyl linoleate, 9.3% a-pinene, 2.8% ethyl hexadecanoate, 2.7% ethyl oleate, 2.2% ß-pinene, 1.6% limonene, 1.1% carvacrol, 0.8% sabinene, 0.6% thymohydroquinone, 0.6% terpinen-4-ol, 0.5% ?-terpinene, 0.5% myrcene, 0.4% bornyl acetate, 0.4% longifolene, 0.3% butyl propyl disulfide, 0.2% ethyl tetradecanoate, 0.2% ethyl octadecanoate, 0.2% methyl linoleate, 0.2% carvone, 0.2% a-longipinene, 0.1% artemisia ketone, 0.1% p-cymen-8-ol, 0.1% 2-heptenal, 0.1% 1,8-cineole, 0.1% trans-sabinene hydrate, 0.1% ethyl octanoate, 0.1% camphene, 0.1% a-terpinene, 0.1% terpinolene, 0.1% linalool, 0.1% (E)-anethole, 0.1% 2-undecanone, trace ß-phellandrene, trace borneol, trace ß-thujone, trace cis-sabinene hydrate, trace ethyl hexanoate, trace ethyl heptanoate, trace ethyl nonanoate, trace dipropyl disulfide, trace dibutyl disulfide (Source: Aboutabl et al., 1986) (3).

Economic potential to the country

Major growing states are Punjab, Himachal Pradesh, Bihar and Assam and in small scale at Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Tamil Nadu and West Bengal.

Export potential:
Producing centres: India, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, Nepal, Egypt and Iraq.
