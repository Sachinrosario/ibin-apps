DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES:
 A coarse scandent vine with shout prickly stem as thick as the thumb found almost all over the tropical parts of India. Leaves large, ovate-oval or sub- orbicular, up to 25 cm long, thick, with 5-7 costae; umbels 1-3 on a common peduncle 1 cm or more in diameter, spherical.

Economic Potential and Uses
UTILIZATION:
Uses:
 In some parts of India, the roots are used as a substitute for Sarsaparilla in the treatment of venereal disease. Among the santals, they are applied for rheumatism and pains in the lower extremities. The inhabitants of Nepal give them in doses of three mashas, for the treatment of gonorrhoea and other discharges from mucous membrane. The mundas of Chota Nagpur use the root in bloodless dysentery.


Chemistry:
 Diosgenin  is reported from root and leaf as well as from in vitro callus. Diosgenin identified in root, leaf and cultured callus tissues. (Rastogi and Mehrotra, 1980-1984).

Pharmacology:


Ethnobotany:

Medicine for anaemia (Root, Tarafder and Chaudhuri, 1981); Antifertility (Root, Sharma, 1981); Blood dysentery (Root, Chaudhuri et al, 1982; Janardhan, 1963); Fever (Root, Jain and De, 1966; Tarafder, 1986); Gum trouble and toothache (Stem, Chaudhuri et al, 1982; Tarafder and Rai Chaudhuri, 1981); Leucorrhoea, spermatorrhoea (Root, Srivastava and Varma, 1981); Rheumatism (Root, Jain and De, 1966; Janardhan, 1963; Stem, Chaudhuri et al, 1982); Urinary complaints (Root, Jain, 1965); Venereal disease (Root, Jain and De, 1966); Veterinary amaebic (Root, Pal, 1981); Gastric complaints (Leaf, Tarafder, 1983); Edible (Fruit, Vartak, 1981); Vegetable (Leaf + Shoot, Vartak, 1959); Symbolic keeping in bed stops bad dreams (Root, Jain, 1971)
