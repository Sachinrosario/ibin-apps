DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES
An annual and is a frost tender. A erect much branched glabrous herb 30-60 cm high; branches grooved, glabrous, often purplish. Leaves ovate, usually notched at the apex. The flowers are monoecious (individual flowers are either male or female, but both sexes can be found on the same plant) and shortly pedicellate, pale green. Seed is i mm diam., lenticular, smooth, shining and black.

REPRODUCTIVE BIOLOGY

Breeding System :
The plant is self-fertile & pollinated by wind..

Phenology :
The flowering generally occurs during July-February and also there is a report of flowering throughout the year.

Pollinators:
It is known to be pollinated by wind.

Seed Biology :

Seed Germination:
Seed - sow late spring in situ. An earlier sowing can be made in a greenhouse and the plants put out after the last expected frosts. Germination is usually rapid and good if the soil is warm. A drop in temperature overnight aids germination. Cuttings of growing plants root easily.

Cultivation:
Prefers a well-drained fertile soil in a sunny position. Requires a hot sheltered position if it is to do well. Plants should not be given inorganic fertilizers, see notes above on toxicity. Cultivated as a food plant in the tropics. Most if not all members of this genus photosynthesize by a more efficient method than most plants. Called the 'C4 carbon-fixation pathway', this process is particularly efficient at high temperatures, in bright sunlight and under dry conditions.
Amaranths are cultivated in environments ranging from the true tropics to semi-arid lands from sea levels to high Himalayas. Generally cultivated within 30° latitude of the equator. Most grain amaranths have been concentrated in high land valleys, such as Sierra Madre, Andes and Himalayas. Ecotypes have also evolved that tolerate alkaline sandy soils with pH as high as 8.5 as well as the acidic clays of the hills.
Day length: Some of the grain species of amaranths are sensitive to length of day. Some strains of A. hypochondriacus will not set flower in summer, however, they do mature in the green house during short day conditions of winter. Some strains of A. cruentus remain vegetative for a long period in its equatorial house. However, it goes to seed very early when introduced into the long-day- conditions. A. caudatus thrives well in high hills and is known to be a short-day species. It usually flowers and sets seed only when day - length is less than 8 hours.
Plants were grown in the open under 5 levels of day light (Eze, 1987); full light; with a single layer or wire mesh screening (70% light); with 2 layers of screening (50%); with 4 layers (20%); and with 6 layers (6%). Growth data are presented for plants grown during the dry season (December January) when the average day light intensity above the screens was 6500Ix (with a maximum of about 9500 Ix under a cloudless sky.). The total plant fresh weight was greatest in full light (as were both the fresh and dry weight of the roots). Leaf area was greatest with 50% light, diminishing with increasing shade. The % flowering and number of branches were greatest, and senescence was most rapid, in. full light. Stem weight ratio varied only slightly between treatment, but as light intensity decreased so did the root weight ratio whereas the leaf weight ratio increased. The contents of total carbohydrate, ascorbic acid and chlorophyll - a and b per unit dry weight of leaf tissue, and the chlorophyll stability index, were higher at higher light intensities. Protein accumulation was best at 70% light.
Temperature:It grows best when the daily high temperature is at least 21°C. Various accessions have shown optimal germination at temperature varying between 16°C and 35°C. The speed of emergence is increased at upper end of the range. The grain species A. hypochondriacus and A. cruentus tolerate high temperature and they are not frost hardy. Growth of the plant cease at about 8°C and the plants are injured below 4°C. However, A. caudatus being native to Andes and high Himalayas is more resistant to chilling than the other two grain species.
Two seed lots, 1 white seeded and 1 black seeded, from the same population of A. hypochondriacus were assessed by Weil et al. (1987) for emergence index and percentage emergence in a peat sand medium over a range of temperature and sowing depth using a modified thermo-gradient plate. Seed colour produced no difference in emergence, but differences in emergence were caused by temperature, sowing depth and temperature x depth interaction. A predictive response surface shows expected seedling emergence index at temperature 15 and 42°C and sowing depths between 13 and 51 mm. Highest emergence index and % emergence on the thermo-gradient plate occurred from the 13mm sowing depth and at 24-34°C; the data suggest field emergence may be satisfactory with soil temperature between 18.5 and 24°C.
Soil and fertilizer: Field observations indicate that amaranth grows well on soil containing widely varying levels of soil nutrients. Initial studies in Pennsylvania (Rodale Research Centre) shows that young grain amaranth plants grow taller with fertilizer, but this grain yield has thus shown far little improvement. Vegetable amaranth, on the other hand, requires high soil fertility, particularly potassium and nitrogen. Grain amaranth requires well-drained sites and appears to prefer neutral or basic soils (pH values above 6). However, this has not been studied carefully, and with the wealth of amaranth germplasm that exists, it is likely that types that tolerate acid conditions can be found, especially as vegetable amaranth is often cultivated in tropical lowlands where acid soils are common. Although the genus is not known for high salt tolerance ability to withstand mild salinity and alkalinity is apparent in some species of amaranth} Moreover, A. tricolor has demonstrated (Foy and Campbell, 1981) tolerance for soil with high alluminium levels. Bressani et al. (1987) reported that A. cruentus ecotypes from USA and Guatemala, A. hypochondriacus from USA and A. caudatus from Peru were grown with 12-24-12 NPK fertilizer at 0, 30, 60 or 90 kg/ha. Fertilizer had no significant effect on yield, but increased the seed protein content in the 2 lowest yielding ecotypes, while effect on seed fat content were inconsistent. Donald R. Schmidt (1987) in a letter to the editor Amaranth News letter mentioned that amaranth, especially the grain type, has been his hobby for about 20 years. Along with official duties in international agronomy assignments, he had opportunity to conduct field trials with grain amaranth in Ethiopia, Nepal and the uplands of Java. One feature of his experience, which contrasts with implications from some populations from some popular articles, is that amaranth is not well adapted to soils markedly deficient in available phosphorus; amaranth seedlings have repeatedly failed to establish. In the same trials, cereals such as corn, sorghum, wheat, triticale and even small seeded millets established satisfactorily. In these situations, satisfactory stands of amaranth were established only where readily soluble phosphorus fertilizer was placed directly below the seeds. Under hilly condition in India NPK at the rate of 20:30:20 kg/ha meet the full requirements of the crop.
Rainfall:For seeds to germinate and establish roots, amaranths require well-moistened soil, but once seedlings are established, grain amarnaths do well with limited water; intact, they grow best under dry, warm conditions. Vegetable amaranths, on the other hand require moisture throughout the growing season. Grain amaranths have been grown in dry-land agriculture in areas receiving as little as 200 mm of annual precipitation, and, at the other extreme, vegetable amaranths are routinely grown in areas receiving 3,000 mm of annual rainfall. Indeed, in West Africa, vegetable amaranth production continues even during the torrential rainy season.
Preparation of fields: The field must be well leveled and two to three ploughing are sufficient for sowing of amaranth seed. Before sowing application of 50 quintals of farmyard manure per hectare has given very good result in yield.
Sowing time and seed rate:The crop is generally sown in the first or second week of June- just after the first monsoon shower. Traditionally, the seeds are broadcast but better crop stand is achieved if seeding is done in rows. The depth of sowing should be less than 2cm in view of very small grain size, with 50cm spacing between rows and 20cm between plants. A seed rate of l-l/2kg to 2kg/ha is used for good grain yield.
Inter-culture: Seed germinates within 4 to 5 days after sowing and needs. maximum care till it attains a height of about 25-30 cm. In fa<^8 this is the most critical stage for obtaining maximum yield, potential of the crop. During this phase, it must be properly, spaced, made free from weeds and must receive adequate moisture. One more weeding is necessary after 30 days of sowing. At the seedling stage one spray of some fungicides to check the attack of damping off is necessary at the seedling stage. Once the stand is established, maintenance is relatively easy. The broad leaves and erect habit quickly create a close canopy, making under strong weeds only a minor problem.

MAJOR PESTS & DISEASES
Hymenia recurvalis and other cater pillars are serious pests of amaranth. Cletus sp., Asparia sp. and Lygus lineolarus bug (Cabbage looper), Trichoplusiani, (European comborer), Ostrinia nubilalis (Corn earworm), Heliothis zea (Cowpea aphid), Aphis craccavora, (Striped blister beetle), Epicauta uittata, a weevil Conotrachelus seniculus and Spinach flea beetle, Disonycha xanthomelas, Lygus bugs, and more specifically the Tarnished plant bug, are presently considered to be the most important insect pest of grain amaranth. Lygus bugs are cosmopolitan pests. They are very familiar pests throughout the world. They feed on hundred of plant species. In fact, the tarnished plant bug has been recorded on 32 hosts. Lygus bugs damage grain amaranth by feeding on the meristematic tissue, developing floral buds, immature blossoms, and developing embryos. This feeding causes localized wilting and tissue necrosis, abscission of fruits, morphological deformation of fruit and seed, and altered vegetative growth have severely damaged grain amaranth yields by piercing the developing plant/leaves/seed and sucking out the juices. Leaf miners have also been found on grain amaranth. Serious damage has been caused by Spider mites. Stem weevil is a major pest of amaranth; its grubs damage foliage and roots and cause the plant to wilt. Stem borer (Lixus truncatucus) is another problems in Africa and Asia in early sown plants, which causes high degree of lodging in plants. Leaf rotters also cause considerable damage during rainy seasons. Seedbeds should be guarded against ants and termites. The other pests damaging the crop are Hypolixus nubilous (Egypt), Rhachi creagra (Costarica), Chrotogonus (Pakistan), Hyphurus (India), Geocoris (California), Thysanoptera (Hawaii), Haplothrips longisetosus (India), Diabnotica barberi and Spodoptera exigna. Peggy Hass (1983) noted that in South Mexico a weedy type of amaranth is used as a guard crop for corn; some pest insects that normally attack corn prefer the amaranth, so they will eat the weedy amaranth and leave the corn alone (Rodale Research Centre 1983). Meloidogyne incognita, (Chitwood) root- knot nematode, a serious pest of Celosia also slightly affect Amaranthus species. Soil fungus, damping off, leaf blight, white rust and mycoplasma and virus have been identified as the serious diseases of grain amaranth in India, Pandy et al. (1985) while studying the leaf surface mycoflora of Amaranthus paniculatus grown in Almora hills, observed that of 24 fungi spp. found on leaves of this crop, Fusarium oxysporum and Nectria cinnabarina were of restricted occurance while Alternaria alternata, A. amaranthi, Phoma glomerata, P. hibumica, Phyllostica spp, (leaf spot), Aspergillus luchunsis and Rhizoctonia spp were commonly isolated. The fungal population was higher in the pre flowering stage of the host. Sealy et al (1988) screened a total of 126 accessions of species and cultivars of Amaranthus against Pythium myriotylum and suggested that accessions that exhibited weighted percent mortality of <20% be selected for use in breeding programme against damping off disease. Bialoskorski et al (1982) also observed Pythium aphamidermatum damaging amaranth crop in Brazil. McLean and Roy (1988) reported Colletotrichum domatium damaging Amaranthus hybridus plant in Mississippi, USA. The other species damaging the crop are C. trancatum and C. capsici, Choanphora cucurbitarum, causing Wetrot is the most troublesome disease of amaranth in Africa. Albugo bliti, causing white rust is a serious disease in Southeast Asia.
Prof. Sumer reported that in coastal areas of Peru amaranth exhibited some evidence of viral disease-perhaps because of higher humidity in these locations. No disease problems have been noticed in Guatemala. Reddy et al (1980) reported casual organism Xanthomonas amaranthicola a bacterial leaf spot disease of amaranths in India).
Sharma et al. (1981) and Naseema et al. (1983) reported that Aspergillus flauus, A. niger and Rhizopus stolonifer were externally as well as internally seed borne in most of the seeds of Amaranthus gangeticus and A. caudatus and are the major storage fungi of amaranth crop in India.
Sammons and Barnett (1987) reported that tobacco ring spot virus damaging Amaranthus hybridus in California, USA. A severe mosaic disease caused by a str. of Cucumber mosaic virus in Amaranthus caudatus was reported in Himachal Pradesh by Sharma and Chowfla (1987) damaging the crop. The other viruses affecting the crop are Ivy Vein clearing virus, alfalfa mosaic virus and beet western yellow virus.

 Economic Potential and Uses

Parts used
Leaves, Shoots and Seeds.

Uses

  1. The shoots and leaves are consumed as pot herb. 
  2. Seed - cooked. Very small but easy to harvest and very nutritious. The seed can be cooked whole, and becomes very gelatinous like this, but it is rather difficult to crush all of the small seeds in the mouth and thus some of the seed will pass right through the digestive system without being assimilated.

Harvesting Practices
Amaranth grains mature much earlier and the plant dries up quite late. If the heads are allowed to remain till the plant dry up, heavy shattering of grains is noticed which leads to heavy grain loss. The heads are cut when the plant is still somewhat green and start weathering and kept for sun drying for 6-7 days in the threshing yard. Threshing is done by beating. The produce is threshed and winnowed like other cereals. Unusually the harvesting is done early in the morning when the plants are somewhat wet due to night dew to avoid grain shattering in cutting heads.

Chemistry :
On a zero moisture basis, 100 g of leaves contains 283 calories, 34.2 g protein, 5.3 g fat, 44.1 g carbohydrate, 6.6 g fibre, 16.4 g ash, 2243 mg calcium, 500 mg phosphorus, 27 mg iron, 336 mg sodium, 2910 mg potassium, 50 mg vitamin A, 0.07 mg thiamine, 2.43 mg riboflavin, 11.8 mg niacin and 790 mg ascorbic acid. The seed contains 14 - 16% protein and 4.7 - 7% fat.

Other Uses:

  1. It is being used as disclaimer, astringent, vermifuge.
  2. A decoction of the entire plant is used to stop dysentery and inflammation. 
  3. The plant is emollient and vermifuge. 
  4. Yellow and green dyes can be obtained from the whole plant.
  5. The leaves are administered internally for scorpion-sting, but are not antidote to snake venom.
