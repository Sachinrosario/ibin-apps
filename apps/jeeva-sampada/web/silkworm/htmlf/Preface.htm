The biological resources are vital to humanity’s economic and social development, hence are rightly considered as a common heritage of mankind.  There is a growing recognition that biological resources are global assets of tremendous value for present and future generations. The Indian-sub continent has been known for a long time as a major place of origin for diversity of several sericigenous insects besides being one of the mega centers of biodiversity with two of the 18 identified hot spots, the Eastern Himalayas and Western Ghats. Thus it has to face the biggest task of its conservation since the rate of gene erosion is at the highest and the list of endangered species is increasing every day.

 

India has the distinction of being the only country producing all commercially known varieties of silk viz., Mulberry, Tasar (both tropical and temperate), Eri and Muga. Many indigenous races of mulberry silkworm, Bombyx mori are still available in different parts of India in addition to hundreds of newly evolved breeds that are commercially exploited in the hybrid form.      

 

Some of the traditional silkworm races are lost due to rapid replacement by high yielding silkworm breeds / hybrids. The economically and ecologically important non-mulberry biodiversity is wild and forest based. The wild silkworms have several ecotypes distributed in the different eco-climatic conditions of the country. Due to the loss or large scale disturbances of habitat, wild silkworm species are on the verge of extinction. Thus, for   sustained utilization of silkworm bio-resources, it is necessary to identify, characterize and evaluate indigenous silkworm races, newly developed high yielding silkworm breeds/hybrids from different states as well as a large number of non-mulberry silkworms in order to adopt appropriate strategies for in-situ  and  ex-situ conservation of endangered  seri- bioresources.

 

          Of the five varieties of natural silk, Mulberry silk is the chief variety comprising 90% of the total silk production in India. The next in commercial importance is Tasar silk followed by Eri and Muga silk. Hence the Vanya (Non-mulberry) silk which is about 10% of the total silk output is of considerable importance in the Indian context. Collection of wild silk cocoons and drawing handspun yarn from them had been a profitable occupation of our tribal folks from time immemorial. Besides earning a livelihood for thousands of rural poor, silk cultivation and silk weaving has been the part of our cultural heritage. Hence, it is essential that these traditional cottage industries of native India, along with associated traditional knowledge, sustain at any cost. Vanya  silks especially Tasar silk has great export potential, but the erratic supply of tasar yarns is the major constraint in the development of tasar silk export.

 

          India has the distinction for its rich fauna of sericigenous insects and attempts have been made for their conservation, preferably in respective ecological zones as many of these have shown different behaviours under changed conditions. Many of the fauna are threatened with extinction as a result of large scale deforestation.  Thus, for the sustained utilization of these bio-resources, especially for product diversification, it is important that these bio-resources are properly surveyed, characterized and conserved. 

 

          Steps to catalogue, characterize and conserve different silkworm breeds in their eco-climatic niches were initiated in 1980s.  Centralized germplasm station for mulberry and silkworm established in Hosur (Tamil Nadu) maintains a large number of indigenous and exotic breeds and Central Tasar Research and Training Institute at Ranchi (Jharkhand) conserves the tasar eco-races in different regional niches, to preserve their genetic and morphological characteristics. Similarly, Central Muga Eri Research and Training Institute at Jorhat (Assam) is looking after the maintenance of the muga and eri silkworms.

 

The present document is a treatise of information on different sericigenous fauna of the country being commercially exploited. Detailed information on the potential breeds / hybrids of different types of silkworms together with their niches, food plants and other features of ecological importance have been given, besides information on post cocoon activities. Attempts have also been made to collect information on the bye-product utilization in different silk types. Future lines of work have also been suggested based on the existing gap areas.
