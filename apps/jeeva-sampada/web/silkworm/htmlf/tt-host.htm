Varieties:  The superiority of T. arjuna in some and T. tomentosa in other aspects demand a mixing of desirable characters of both species for tasar culture. However, cycle of selection following experimental hybridization is not practicable immediately in the genus Terminalia. Therefore, efforts were made to identify natural hybrids of both species after extensive survey. Presently, there is no any variety as such for exploitation in tasar culture.

Distribution:   The food plants of tasar grow luxuriantly at low altitude 0-600 MASL between about 40° North and south latitude. Tasar culture is mainly practiced on plants of Terminalia arjuna (Arjun) and T.tomentosa. These plants are commonly distributed in mixed deciduous tropical forests of Indian sub continent. Indian communities have traditionally regulated the use of forest resources on the basis of religious beliefs and social customs. Cultural practices

 

          Traditionally tasar culture is a forest based industry. The rearing of tasar culture is mainly conducted on tall and irregularly distributed trees. The scattered distribution and tall nature of food plants cause management difficulties, leading to considerable loss of worms. Moreover, as time marches pressure on land increased and the available flora became more and more scarce. Thus to make tasar culture viable it is necessary to adopt  scientific methods of plant maintenance. Cultural practices enable better establishment and quick growth thereby reducing the gestation period. The package of practices have been worked out with regard to agronomical practices of T. arjuna and T. tomentosa. Efforts have been made by CSTRI, Ranchi to standardize the different cultural practices for better management of Terminalia spp.  The aspects are given below:

 

a) Spacing for Arjun and Asan bush plantation

 

Experiments conducted with different spacings for Arjun  reveal that spacing of 4’x 4’ is ideal for maximum yield. This accommodates 6724 plants/ha and yield about 18 metric tons of leaf at the end of 4th year of plantation. Wider spacing i.e. 6’x6’, 8’x8’ or more has been found uneconomical as leaf yield per unit area is greatly reduced due to accommodation of very less number of plants.  For Asan 6’ x 6’ spacing has been considered as ideal for economic plantation. This accommodates a total of 3025 plants/ha and yield about 10 MT tones of leaf at the end of fourth year of plantation under Ranchi condition.

b) Maintenance of plantation

 

For optimum output of quality leaves from economic plantation, the plants need to be maintained on scientific lines. The maintenance essentially involves the following steps:

 

1.   Cultural practices:  Dry farming under rainfed conditions are well suited to economic plantation. Bunding of  the fields on contour lines and division of land into compartments should be done to ensure water harvest and preserve moisture. Hoeing should be done at least twice in June and August depending on rain reception. Time to time loosening of soil and weeding facilitate proper growth of the plants.

 

2.   Manure application:  very alternate year the land should receive 600 cft/ha FYM compost. FYM is applied during November to December. In practice 6-9” deep circular trench is dug around the base of the plant and refilled with 1.5-2.0 kg of FYM compost. The application of manure improves the physical condition of soil by way of adding organic matter.

 

3.   Fertilizer application:  Nitrogen, phosphorous and potash (NPK) are inorganic elements which are essential for proper growth and yield of the plants. The requirement of NPK to Arjun and Asan depends on nutrient status of the soil and age of the plant. The fertilizer is applied through deep placement method by making 3-4 holes of 9-12” deep. Phosphate and potash are applied as a single dose on the onset of monsoon while urea is applied in 2 split doses on the onset of monsoon and after first crop rearing during August. It has been observed that addition of NPK yields about 30% more leaf in Arjun and Asan besides improving the nutrient status. Further growth of the plant is faster due to application of NPK.

 

4.   Training and pruning:  To improve the leaf quality and keep the plants within manageable height of 10’-15’, training and pruning are affected. After four years of plantation, plants are heavily pruned at 6’ height once in 2 years during February, while light pruning of 1” diameter shoot is also affected during February in the absence of heavy pruning. For chawki garden, Arjun/ Asan plants should be pruned at 3’ height during February. This will help in maintaining the plants at 6’-8’ height which is desirable for net rearing of chawki worms. However, the grown up virgin plants should not be directly pruned at 3’ height. They are required to be slowly brought down at 3’ height otherwise mortality may occur.
