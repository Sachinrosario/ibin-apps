Introduction

 

          The Tasar culture is essentially forest and agro-based activity that covers agricultural and industrial activity. The activity covers cultivation of host plants, production of silkworm eggs, silkworm rearing, cocoon production, reeling, spinning, weaving and utilization of silk waste and other by-products. The end product of industry is Tasar Fabric.  Tropical tasar is one of the important non wood forest produce found in the tropical forest of the country. It is collected in the form of silk cocoons spun by polyphagous sericigenous insect Antheraea mylitta Drury feeding on the leaves of variety of food plants.  It thrives as part of traditional livelihood among the aborigins of Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Orissa, Andhra Pradesh, Maharashtra, West Bengal and Uttar Pradesh. Of late with the advent of modern research findings, it is being reared on the bushes of Arjun (Terminalia arjuna Bedd.) and Asan (T. tomentosa W. & A.) in an organized manner. Though production of tasar silk is far lower compared to mulberry, the biodiversity reflected by the tropical tasar, its role in forest regeneration and its ability of employment generation at all levels speaks of the importance of tasar culture.  The tropical tasar culture has a long heritage in India and the traditional areas have shown that given proper direction and infrastructural support   tasar culture can provide better scope for rural development and economic transformation has been brought where an integrated approach has been adopted for the development of tasar culture.

 

          The rich biological diversity of A. mylitta is largely due to its wide range of distribution and foraging of silkworm on variety of food plants. Owing to its wide range of distribution the species has encountered diverse geoclimatic conditions like annual precipitation, temperature, day length, plant succession interalia. Factors like latitude, longitude, altitude etc. of distinct areas lead to marked differences expressing wide variations in phenotypic, physiological, behavioural, commercial and technological traits. Various populations thus, isolated geographically over centuries have adapted to a particular ecological niche and referred to as eco-races. These eco-races constitute unique genetic resources of tropical tasar for today and tomorrow. The existing genetic resources of A. mylitta can be utilized effectively only, if sufficient information on the species is available. The Inventory of Tropical Tasar will serve as ready reckoner of the silkworm, Antheraea mylitta Drury for applied researchers, taxonomists, geneticists, entomologists, physiologists, technologists, foresters, fashion designers, traders, exporters, farmers, reelers, spinners, weavers, dieticians, cosmeticians, pharmacologists and silk users for reasons of their own.

Physiographic/Climatic requirements:

 

          Tropical Tasar occurs mostly in the forests of India bounded by the Ganges in the North and the Godavari in the South West. The geographic range of population distribution of A. mylitta is limited mainly to five types of soil namely red loamy, sandy red, black clayey, laterite and forest hill. (Table 1). However, majority of the population are restricted to red loamy and black clayey soil. Dense humid forest and moderate temperature by virtue of either altitude or higher latitude are conducive for development of tasar silkworm.

The tasar silkworm being poikilothermic cannot regulate its body temperature therefore, agro-climatic conditions play a crucial role in its development, survival, pupation, emergence and fecundity.

 

Temperature and Humidity:  Tasar silkworms tolerate wide range of temperature and humidity because of their outdoor multiplication. They survive at temperatures and relative humidity as high as 35°C and 90-100% and as low as 9°-10°C and 30-40 per cent respectively.  However, their life-cycle activities slow down below 20°C and above 35°C.  Temperature and humidity intimately influence the number of life-cycle in a year and the larval span. In the first season (July-August) the temperature ranges from  24° - 35°C and relative humidity ranges from 75-95 per cent, whereas in the second season (October-November) the temperature varies from 20 to 30°C and relative humidity from 60 to 80 percent. The larval period lasts for 30-60 days, depending upon the season.

 

Sunlight:  Newly hatched early instar larvae avoid bright sunlight and therefore usually rest on the underside of the leaves. Late instar larvae prefer bright sunlight but move to shade when the sun is very strong. During moulting and cocoon formation they prefer shade.

 

Rain and Storm:  In breeze and drizzle, the larvae continue to feed normally. Gusty wind, heavy rains and storm are disturbing, especially for first instar larvae and are easily blown away.

 

Grainage:  In sericulture parlance, insectory is known as grainage. During summer, inside grainage temperature should not go beyond 35°C. The abiotic conditions prevailing and desirable during summer and their effects on cocoon preservation are cited below:

Seasons of natural occurrence

 

          Silkworms of naturally occurring adults of A. mylitta can be grouped in to three categories: uni-, bi- and tri-voltines.  However, a true univoltine stock is lacking, as only small part of nature grown cocoons behave as univoltine. In captive conditions, the tropical tasar behave as bi- and tri-voltine depending upon the environmental condition. The moth of A. mylitta is a non-feeding stage and in all the diapausing stock adult moth emerges from its cocoon during June-July. Again, in non-diapausing generations, the season of natural occurrence for bi-voltines is September whereas November is the season of occurrence of adult moth for tri-voltines.
