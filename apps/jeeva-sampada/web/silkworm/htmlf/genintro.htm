The earliest reference of cultivated silk production dates back to roughly 2640 BC when the Chinese Empress Hsi Ling Shi patronized the silk industry. For several hundred years, the art of producing silk was kept as a secret and guarded safely within the country.  Hoshomin, the Queen of China, introduced sericulture for the first time. For a long time, sericulture was considered to  be a national  secret by the Chinese government. Later, it was introduced to Europe and Japan.  Sericulture was introduced to India about 400 years back and since then silk industry is flourishing as an agro-industry par excellence.  India is known for a rich fauna of sericigenous insects.  The list of different sericigenous fauna and their food plants are listed below (Table 1).

According to Mukherjee (1919), the Aryans discovered silkworm in sub-Himalayas beyond Kashmir. Indian epics  like Rigveda, Ramayana and Mahabharata (Nanavati,  1990) also mention the use of ‘Pitambara’  (silk clothes of Indian origin) and ‘Chidambara’ (Chinese origin). Despite the long history of Indian sericulture, it is only in the independent India that concerted efforts were initiated for characterization and conservation of silkworm breeds in different agro-climatic regions.

 

India has the unique distinction of producing all the five known varieties of silk viz., mulberry, oak tasar, tropical tasar, eri and muga. Among five types of silkworms reared in India, Bombyx mori, feeds on the leaves of Morus to produce the best quality of silk. As regards mulberry silk production, more than 98% is produced from five traditional sericultural states viz., Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, West Bengal, Tamil Nadu and Jammu & Kashmir.  Limited amount of mulberry silk is produced by 15 non-traditional  states viz., Assam, Arunachal Pradesh, Bihar, Chattisgarh, Himachal Pradesh, Jharkhand, Kerala, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Manipur, Orissa, Punjab, Tripura, Uttaranchal and Uttar Pradesh.  The famous Muga silk is  produced only in India and is confined to Brahmaputra valley. The muga silkworm (Antheraea assama)  feeds on Som and Solom  plants (Machilus sp.). Tropical tasar silk is a product of Antheraea mylitta, which feeds on Terminalia tomentosa and T. arjuna grown in the thick forests of Jharkhand, Bihar, Madhya Pradesh and Orissa. Oak tasar silk is a product of Antheraea frithi, A. compta, A. pernyi, A. yamamai and A. proylei, which  feed on several species of Quercus. It is confined  only to sub-Himalayan oak belt comprising of Jammu & Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh and Uttaranchal in North western sector and Manipur, Mizoram, Nagaland, Arunachal Pradesh and Meghalaya in North eastern sector.   Eri silkworm, Phylosamia ricini which feeds on common castor plant (Ricinus communis) is mainly raised in Assam and Orissa, producing eri silk. The country has made a phenomenal progress in silk production since independence. During the fifties, silk production was only 600 MT per year and sericulture was considered as subsidiary occupation of the poor farmers. The establishment of Central Silk Board by the Government of India and consequent establishment of new research institutions in 1960s paved way for reaching higher production level in subsequent decades.  Owing to the R & D contributions by the CSB institutions during the subsequent period, India recorded a quantum jump in silk production. With the development of improved mulberry varieties and  the cultivation practices, high yielding silkworm hybrids with the rearing technology together with silkworm and mulberry pest and disease control measures, significant improvement in mulberry acreage, cocoon production and  raw silk production was increased.

          India, blessed with its wide range of sericigenous flora and fauna, occupies second position among the silk producing countries in the world. During 2002, the total production of raw silk in the country was 16,319 MT(ISA, 2003), of which mulberry silk accounted for 14,617 MT (89.57 %), Tasar-284 MT  (1.74 %), Eri–1316 MT  (8.06 %) and  Muga–102 MT (0.63 %).  The trend in global & National raw silk production over the years (2000 to 2002) is given in Tables 3 & 4. Of the five types of silk produced in the country, mulberry sericulture is the dominant one, accounting for about 90% of the entire silk produced in the country.

          Indian silk industry has grown manifold in terms of both production and  productivity, which is attributed mainly to the R & D efforts of the institutions of the Central Silk Board and extension support of Departments of Sericulture of different states.   Sericulture is a labour-intensive industry in all its phases, viz., food plant cultivation, silkworm rearing, reeling and other off-farm activities such as twisting, dyeing, weaving, printing, etc.  The industry  spread over several states, caters to the needs of  both the domestic as well as foreign markets.  With the shift in weaving sector, from handlooms to powerlooms, the mulberry silk industry is now gearing up for production of quality silk yarn and in-turn quality fabrics to suit the needs of the international market.  Improved silkworm hybrids yielding international grade silk (2A- 4A) together with improved reeling technology, has now enabled the industry to produce the required quality silk, thereby curbing the imports.  However, there is need for professional approach in extension management for quick and efficient transformation of the existing on-farm and off-farm  practices to enable the industry to face the challenges of the present day market scenario in the light of WTO/ GATT regulations.
