Taxonomic Details

Scientific Name: Cinachyra australiensis (Carter, 1886)

  Common Name: golf ball sponge 

 Accepted Name: Cinachyrella tarentina (Pulitzer-Finali, 1983) 

Taxon author(s): Carter, 1886                                                                                  

Synonym: Cinachyrella tarentina (Pulitzer-Finali, 1983),Cinachyra cavernosa (Lamarck, 1815 sensu Tsurnamal, 1969),Cinachyra australiensis Carter, 1881, Cinachyra isis Lendenfeld, 1907,Cinachyra malaccensis Sollas, 1902,Cinachyra providentiae Dendy, 1922,Spiretta porosa Lendenfeld, 1888, Tethya armata Baer, 1906,Tethya australiensis Carter, 1886,Tetilla cinachyroides Hentschel, 1911,Tetilla lindgreni Lendenfeld, 1903

Habitat and Ecology

Ecosystem : filter-feeder, marine, sessile.                                            

 Depth: intertidal 91m

Ecotoxicology: This sponge was suspected to be symbiotic with sulphur-oxidising chemi-autotrophic bacteria.   

 

Back to top

External Morphology

 

Shape: color: Globular sponges

 

Max size and age:8 cm.

 

Surface characteristics: hispid-bristly surface provided with numerous scattered large and deep porocalices which are flask shaped. It has numerous craters. It is often covered with sand or algae.

 

Texture: bristly surface

 

Mucus production and smell:Mucus production is particularly common in intertidal tropical species and may serve a physiological role in protecting (e.g. cooling) the sponge when exposed to the sun and air.

 Identifying Characteristics:
ball-shaped, squat and yellow colour obscured by sediment; craterlike pits smaller and more numerous : In cross section, there is a clear cortical region with special cortical megascleres (short oxeas) and collagenous reinforcement, Megascleres choanosomal large oxeas, cortical small oxeas, protriaenes, and anatriaenes. Microscleres sigmaspires. Identified with porocalices (inhalant pores grouped in special pore areas) without unusual accessory spicules.

Miscellaneous Information

Their main predators are the Nudibranchs, cowries and sea urchins. Nudibranchs also use the toxin for its own benefit. They absorb the toxin into their body and recycle it to make themselves distasteful to their predators.
