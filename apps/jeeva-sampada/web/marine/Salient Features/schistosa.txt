Morphological:  Head moderate, slighty distinct from neck, shields entire; nostril with prominent, downward prolongation giving a beaklike profile, which accounts for the common names 'Beaked Sea Snake' 'Hook-nosed Sea snake'; one pre and 1 (rarely 2), postoculars; two, rarely one, anterior temporals and three or four posterior temporals; 7-8 supralabials, the 3rd and 4th or 4th only in contact with the eye; mental narrow, elongatel partly hidden between infralabials, five in number, in contact with the genials (chin shields), which are not well differentiated and separated by scale (See figs. 3A-C) Scales keeled and overlapping, each with a short central keel; scale rows 51-59 at midbody in adult males, 53-59 in adult females.  Ventrals 303-359, very small, only a little broader than the adjacent scales and each one with two keels; preanals feebly enlarged.

Diagnostic characteritics:  The common sea snake has a stout and stocky body, recognised easily by the end of the snout, which projects in a hook beyond the lower jaw and the presence of a deep groove under the chin.  Most adults and young are dirty white to pale greenish grey, with olive to blackish crossbands which are widest in the middle and tapering are often almost uniform grey above.  New born young are mild white, with 42-50 jet black crossbands almost encircling the body posteriorly and tapering to a point on sides anteriorly.  The head is greenish, without markings, the chin and throat being white in most of young and adults.

Additional remarks:  The beaked sea snake is generally found in shallow waters with a muddy bottom.  It is also frequently noticed in the streams to about the limits of tidal flow.  On days when the sea is calm, this snake is seen hiding under the surface.  It enters the tidal creeks during the monson.  Although this species is common in the tidal waters of the Bay of Bengal, Smith (1943) has made no mention of its possible occurance in the estuaries.  Murthy (1983) has recorded this snake from the estuaries in and around Chennai, and also from the Chilka Lake, Orissa.  Though the Beaked sea snake, like most other sea snakes, is an inoffensive reptile,  its disposition is said to be aggressive as it bites fiercely and effectively taking advantage of its wide gape.  Despite the varying accounts, it is proved that this species is the most frfequent cause of human fatalities from the sea snake bites.  The venom of this snake is considered four to eight times as deadly as that of the cobra.  Fishermen catch and throw this snake back in the sea with impunity.  However, the beaked sea snake should be approached with caution and must be treated with respect.
