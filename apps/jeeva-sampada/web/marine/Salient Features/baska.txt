SALIENT FEATURES:

Morphological:  Head small, snout upturned, and stronglyh projecting; skin of hindeer part of head divided into small shields; alveolar surfaces of jaws very broad with two strong denticulated rideges.  Cara[ace smooth, moderately depressed, with an interrupted vertebral keel in the young, which disappears in the adult.  Plastron considerably smaller, strongly angulate laterally in the young, truncate anteriorly and notched posteriorly.  Limbs with very narrow transversely enlarged scales, digits fully webbed, with four claws only.  Tail short, very short in the young.

Diagnostic characteritics:  Shell olive - brownish or greenish above, head and under surface of neck brown; plastron yellow; soft parts dull olive above, paler below.

Sex attributes:

Descriptive characters:  The male, during the breeding season, assumes a brilliant colouration as follows; the area around the nostrils turn pale blue while the surface around the rest of head and under surface of neck become deep black, passing into deep cirmson to black at the base of the neck and on the forelimbs.  The hind parts turn dull reddish purple.  The eye is coloured greenish yellow throughout life.

Meristic characteristics:

Feeding habit:  Herbivorous / carnivorous

Main food:  Leaves, stems and fruits of river - side plants; also molluscs, crustaceans and fish.

Feeding type

Additional remarks:  The Batagur turtle inhabits eestuaries, deep slow-flowing rivers and canals but its most preferred habitat is the shallow, muddy, tidal regions in the vicinity of wide river mouths, dotted with mangrove or other river- side vegetation.  They are shy animals and fond of basking of in the open for which purpose they emerge from the tidal waters, assembling in herds from one to five hundred.  The female lays about 60 eggs, mostly at night, in three batches in the sand banks near tidal estuaries.  The hatchlings, measuring from 70-75 mm in length emerge after a peirod of incubation which lasts from 70-100 days.  The eggs fall prey to otters, dogs, monitor lizards, giant fishes, and crocodiles, while the adult batagur is preyed upon by the sharks, the saltwater crocodile, and of course by humans.

The Batagur is heavily exploited for its eggs and meat and is now severely depleted and scarce.  It was said to be very common in Calcutta in 1900s and much sought after as food, sometimes substituting sea turtle meat.  Some islands in the Sunderbans of West Bengal are probably the turtle's unspoilt breeding grounds in India.  This species is listed as undangered and protected under Schedule I of the Indian Wildlife Protction Act (1972, amended 1991).
