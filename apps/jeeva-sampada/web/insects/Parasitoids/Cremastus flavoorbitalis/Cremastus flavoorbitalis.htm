 Biology/ Ecology / Behaviour

 The life cycle of Cremastus flavoorbitalis:

Larva:

First instar: The primary larva of Cremastus flavoorbitalis is of the caudate or tailed type. It has a heavily sclerotized, testaceous head. The body is divided into 13 segments. The last abdominal segment is developed into a long, narrow, tapering tail. The body is creamy white and has no spines or hairs. The first instar lasts 5 days. During this period the body changes markedly in appearance and in size. The head, however, remains the same throughout these changes.

One day after hatching the larva has an average length, including the tail, of 1.3 mm and an average width at the widest part of the body of 0.15 mm. The length of tail is approximately half the entire length of the body and presents a ruffled or uneven appearance. Each of the 12 remaining body segments has a double row of "scollops" running around the body in the middle of each segment. Apparently this structure permits the body to expand more readily.

The 2 day old larva measures 2.5 mm in length and 0.19 mm in width. The tail is still approximately half the entire length of the body. The remaining body segments are smooth, having only vestigeous of their ruffles.

At the time of molting into the second instar, five days after hatching of the egg, the larva  averages 3.6 mm in length and .31 mm in diameter. The tail is about 1/3 rd entire length of body. The body segments have become expanded to their maximum size, leaving no trace whatsover of the scallaped appearance that was in evidence earlier.

In the first instar the larval head is testaceous heavily scerotised, somewhat thimble shaped and has on the dorsal surface two grooves oringinating at the posterior boundary of head and the running entirely about 3/4 th of its length. A slight ridge on each side of the head extended posteriorly from the insertion of mandibles. On the dorsal portion of the head between the grooves there are no spines or sensary hairs. Laterally there are seven pairs of small spines on the head. One spine at the anterior end of the head about one half the distance between the mouth parts and the end of dorsal groove. One spine slightly ventrad and slightly cauded of the anterior end of the dorsal groove. A group of 3 spines, which might be connected by a curve, midway between mandible and the dorsal groove. One spine in a cauded centre position near the lateral ridge and one spine in a central posterior position. the ventral surface supports 3 pairs of small spines and one pair of large spine. One pair of spines is situated on the anterior portion cauded of the mouthparts and is separated by about the width of two mandibles. Another pair is midway on the head and is separated by width of one mandible. The third pair is on the posterior part of head and is separated by a distance twice as great as between the preceding pair. The large spines are situated immediately cauded of the third pair of small spines and as separated by the same distance. These large spines are set on tubercles.

The first-instar larvae of Cremastus flavoorbitalis possess distinctly defined mouth parts. The entire mouth-part structure is circular in shape. The labral region is semicircular and has within its borders several areas that are but thinly sclerotized. One large area within the labral region is located in a dorsal position. Laterad and slightly ventrad of this are two smaller areas within the labral structure. Supplementary to these are eight minute similarly constructed areas. There are no spines or hairs. In the oral cavity there are two large spines. The maxillary and labial region consists of two pairs of triangular structures, their apices directed toward the center of the oral cavity. The mandibles are simple, strongly curved, and rather sharply pointed. the mandibular condyle is well developed.

Second instar: the larval period of second instar lasts but 1 day. The parasite, however, is markedly different in appearance from what it was during the first instar. It measures on the average 3.8 mm in length and 0.7 mm in width. The head has become rounded and is very thinly sclerotized. The tail has been reduced to a very short robust process. the mandibles are very lightly sclerotized and are less strongly curved; they are nearly triangular and stout.

Third instar; The mature or third instar larva of Cremastus flavoorbitalis  measures 8.2 mm in length and 2.05 mm in width and is dirty white, the body consist of 13 segments. The spiracles are situated on the first thorasic and first eight abdominal segments. The wing buds visible on the mesothorasic and metathorasic segments, and the leg buds are visible through the wall of three thorasic segments. The cuticle is void of spines or other large processes, but is composed of many fine tubercles. the tail is further reduced. The third larval instar is 2 days in duration.

Cocoon: The cocoon of Cremastus flavoorbitalis is oblong oval in shape and an average measures 8.85 mm in length and 3.34 mm in width. There is, however, a marked variation in the dimensions of cocoons, the range being from 7 to 10 mm in length and from 2.5 to 4 mm in width. The coloration of the cocoon is not constant, as it varies from tan to dark brown. In most cases there is a lighter narrow band about the middle. Some cocoons, however, are without faint marking. The cocoon is enveloped in a loose but finely woven silk sheath. the average length of the period spent in the cocoon is 10 days.

Pre pupa and pupa

Cremastus flavoorbitalis spends the first 3 days of its cocoon existence as a pre pupa of the usual hymenopterous type. At the time of moulting, the pupa is nearly white, with the exception of the darkened eye spots. In the course of several days the pupa darkens and a few days before eclosion it takes on the markings and general appearance of the adult insect. The male pupa is generally smaller than female. The ovipositor of the female extends beyond the tip of abdomen and lies flat against the dorsal surface.

Biology;

Hatching, growth and Development: The Cremastus flavoorbitalis  deposit five eggs in a single host. The egg floats freely within the body cavity of the host and hatches after 31/2 days. The presence of the larval parasite in the body of the host seemingly has no effect on the parasite is small. For 5 days the larva remains in this instar, changing in body form. The second instar lasts but 1 day. One day after the parasite has molted into the third instar, the host larva spins a loose web, attaching itself to any convenient surface and reclines in a characteristic hammocklike position and remains quiscent. It then begins to darken at both ends and this blackening steadily progresses toward the center of the larva. When the host has become completely darkened, usually 2 days after the molt of the parasite into the third instar, the Cremastus larva makes its first appearance on the outside, emerging through a hole in the middle of the host. There is no external feeding.

European corn borer larvae parasitized by Cremastus flavoorbitalis are usually smaller than the nonparasitized larvae, at least a few days prior to the issuance of the parasite larva from the host. Head measurements of larvae after the parasite had left the body would seem to indicate that the host larvae were in the fourth instar. The host larvae are fifth instar at the time that the parasite issue from the hosts. The larva immediately upon issuance begins to spin a cocoon, cementing it to any nearby surface. The head capsule and the dried-up body of the host larva are usually attached by a few loose threads. When the operation of cocoon spinning is completed, the parasite is in the pre pupal stage and stays as such for three days; then it expels the larval meconium and molts into a typical white hymenopterous pupa. The pupa gradually develops and darkens for 7 days, at which time the adult sheds the pupal skin, chews an irregular hole at one end of the cocoon and emerges.

Habits of the adult: The Cremastus flavoorbitalis is quite positively responsive to light, is very active and exhibits a restless temperment. When several adults are placed in a cage, especially if it is small, their activities in trying to escape are very pronounced. In several instances in mating studies, adults were introduced into a specially prepared cellophane cages, although noticeably irritable, they calm down in a few minutes and content themselves with incessant ramblings over the end of the cage nearest the light. At temperatures below 700 F they are quiescent and do not become active until the temperature rises above that point. The adults will feed on sweetened water, honey or invert sugar such as glucose.

Mating

In the rearing of Cremastus flavoorbitalis in the laboratory, the problem of mating in captivity was the most difficult one Encountered. Many attempts were made to overcome this so that it might be possible to rear this parasite in large numbers. Matings were secured in abundance on only one occasion on July. At that time 75 males and 65 females were introduced into a cloth mating cage. The cage is 9 inches in height, 18 inches in width at the open end, which is 9 by 9 inches. The open back is equipped with a 9 by 18 inch celluloid-covered frame, which is held in place by elastic bands (3).

 Appearance

Egg: The egg is elongate-ovoid or kidney shaped, with the both ends bluntly rounded. The chorion is smooth, bearing no spines or external processes. It is pearly white. The average length is 0.513 mm, and the average width through the center of the egg is 0.167 mm. At the time of hatching the mature embryo is folded, jacknife fashion, within the chorion of the egg, i.e. the head and tail are bent forward on the reminder of the body with in the shell. The duration of the egg stage is 84 hours or 31/2 days.

Larva (Male/Female): The newly hatched caudate larvae of Cremastus flavoorbitalis Cam. bear a double row of scallops transversely on each body segment; these disappear before the first molt and are believed to be an adaptation to permit of rapid increase in body size (2).

Pupa (Male/Female):                                                                                                                                                                    

Adult (Male/Female): It has a slender, testaceous body with broad and rather abbreviated wings. The face is distinctly punctate, with the epistoma elevated and the clypeus distinctly discrete and apically margined (1).
