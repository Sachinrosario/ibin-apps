 Biology/ Ecology / Behaviour

Trichogramma japonicum  adults are extremely small. The female adult lays her eggs on other moths' eggs. First, she examines the eggs by antennal drumming, then drills into the eggs with her ovipositor, and lays one or more eggs inside the moth's eggs. She usually stays on or near the host eggs until all or most of them are parasitized. When the parasitized moth's eggs turn black, the larvae parasites develop within the host eggs. The larva eats the contents of the moth's eggs. Adults emerge about 5-10 days later depending on the temperature. Adults can live up to 14 days after emergence. Female adults can lay up to 300 eggs (2).

Conservation

Trichogramma  species differ in their searching behavior, host preferences, response to environmental conditions, and suitability in biological control uses. The timing of Trichogramma japonicum releases in the field is important. Non-parasitism could be due to the use of less suitable Trichogramma japonicum strains to the host pests, environmental conditions, and untimely release of parasitoids. It is best to release of parasitoids at the beginning of a pest infestation (when moths are first seen in the field), followed by regular releases until a natural breeding population of Trichogramma is established.

An example of this approach is the corn borer control. The first release should be during the first appearance of moths and corn borers' eggs in the cornfields. Weekly releases thereafter are to ensure the Trichogramma japonicum  population build-up and parasitism occurrence. The build-up of the parasitoids depends on the presence of the pest or alternative hosts and food for adults. It is important to regulary monitor pest population, egg parasitism (parasitized eggs are black in color), and the larval infestation. Trichogramma japonicum  is released as pupae in parasitized host eggs. The pupae can be pasted on cards or put in various containers. To be successful in the field, food, host eggs and shelter must be available.

Modify cropping practices by practicing crop rotation and by planting cultivars which are favorable to Trichogramma japonicum  population build-up such as wild carrots, dill, golden rod, leguminous plants, and flowering vegetables. Adults feed only on nectar, pollen, and honeydew. Many of these species are found naturally occurring in agricultural and garden habitats. Many adult parasitoids and predators benefit from sources of nectar and the protection provided by refuges such as hedgerows, cover crops, and weedy borders. Avoid indiscriminate use of hazardous pesticides to ensure their presence in agricultural fields (2).

Miscellaneous Information

  • The inundative release of T. japonicum during the panicle initiation resulted in significant increase in egg parasitism from 12% before release to 32% egg parasitism at 4 days after release (DAR).

  • Consequently, this resulted in lower WSB damage, 4.62% whiteheads, and yield of 5.7 t/ha.

  • In the January–June 2004 cropping using PSB Rc 82, fields released with T. japonicum yielded 9.75% whiteheads and 4.19 t/ha while fields not released with T. japonicum obtained 21.50% whiteheads and yield of 2.68 t/ha.

  • A net benefit of P13,000/ha was obtained when T. japonicum was used in WSB management.

  • Field release of T. japonicum Ashmead reduced WSB, S. innotata Walker population, hence, such egg parasitoidcan be utilized as an integral component in WSB management.

  • T. japonicum was environment friendly, economical and sustainable, and an efficient biological control agent against WSB (3).
