 Biology/ Ecology / Behaviour

Head transverse as wide as or wider than thorax. Antennae 11-12 segmented in both sexes. Mesonotum in the confluent point of notaulices with distinctly deep fovea; first abdominal tergite robust, only little longer than its width. Parasidal furrows always at least partially distinct. Venation of wings nearly complete. Fore wing: Pterostigma triangular and more or less prolongated at either end, pterostigmal cell complete, radial vein reaching wing margin, median vein complete, three radial cells developed, cubital vein from greatest part distinct, median cell complete; basal cell of hind wing distinctly complete. Propodeum either distinctly areolated, with areolae smooth or slightly rugose along carinae, or rugose, with carinae more or less distinct. Abdomen lanceolate, compressed apically in females and ovate in males; first abdominal segment either slender and long or nearly square, with various sculpture ( corsely rugose to nearly smooth); ovipositor sheaths either slender and long, with single hairs and nearly edged or rounded at apex, or wide, relatively quickly narrowing towards apex, densely pubescent on all surface and bluntly pointed at apex.

Female: Body length 1.9 mm, fore wing 1.9 mm, antennae 1 mm. Head transverse, slightly dilated behind eyes and strongly narrowing postriorly, smooth, shiny, sparsely haired dilated behind eyes and strongly narrowing posteriorly, smooth, shiny, sparsely haired, wider than thorax at tegulae, as wide as 2.2 times of transfacial line; transfacial line as wide as twice of clypeuo-antennal line; occipital carinae distinctly margined; the mutual distance of ocelli about diameter of antennal socket; clypeus slightly oval, convex, with 6-9 long hairs, completely separated from long hairs, completely separated from face by narrow groove; tentorial pits deep; eyes slightly prominent, longitudinal eye-diameter. Antennae 11 segmented, rather short, only reaching apex of thorax, 1st flagellar segment 1.4 as long as 2nd and 4 times as long as wide, apical segment acute, preceeding segment subequal to apical one.

Thorax smooth, shiny, sparely haired; mesonotum prrominent above pronotum, notaulices completely reching 3/4 apical mesoscutum, with deep and well marked fovea in confluing point; scutellum convex, prolongatly triangular, margined laterally, very rising the level of mesonotum; mesopleuron smooth, shining. Fore wing transperent; pterostigma triangular, 4 times as long as wide; 1st abscissa of radial vein slightly shorter than 2nd interradial vein, as long as 0.4 of 2 nd radial abscissa; 1st interradial vein in its insertion point on median vein and 2nd interradial vein colourless. Hind metatarsus as long as three following segments combined. Propdeum areolated, with wide pentagonal areolate.

Abdomen lanceolate, slightly longer than head and thorax combined; tergite 1 robust, only a little longer than width, slightly roundley prominent, rugose, with more or less distinctly central carinae and two lateral longitudinal carinae reaching about 5/6 apex, without lateral depression.

Colouration: Brownish black to black except ventral scape and pedicel brownish and legs brownish yellow (4).

Miscellaneous Information

Morphological Characterization of Ephedrus persicae Biotypes in the Palaearctic: Ephedrus persicae represents a biologically complex parasitoid species group that parasitizes >150 aphid species worldwide, including many pests in different agroecosystems. Evidence from the host range indicated some taxonomic problems within this species-complex. Using classical and geometric morphometric approaches, we assessed the morphological differences among various E. persicae biotypes in the Palaearctic reared from 20 aphid hosts. We found that morphological variability of E. persicae biotypes was influenced by the host. Classical morphometric analysis indicated a conservative position of E. persicae biotypes in the Palaearctic. Our analysis showed that E. persicae biotypes reared from nonleaf-curling Aphis and leaf-curling Dysaphis aphid hosts have significant differences, but all other analyzed E. persicae biotypes have an intermediate position, making it impracticable to split the E. persicae group this way. However, geometric morphometrics used to analyze variation of wing shape showed better predictive ability. The high ratio of correctly assigned individuals based on the wing shape of E. persicae/Aphis viticis Ferrari, E. persicae/Brachycaudus klugkisti (Börner), and E. persicae/Tuberocephalus momonis (Matsumura) biotypes indicates that some host-specific adaptations occurred. The E. persicae/ Hyadaphis foeniculi (Passerini) biotype is clearly separated along the CV2 canonical axis, exhibiting shorter and wider wings. On the basis of this result, we describe a new taxon—Ephedrus lonicerae n. sp (2).

Parasitation of the parasitic wasp Ephedrus Persicae (Frogatt) on the rosy apple aphid Dysaphis Plantaginea (Passerini): The rosy apple aphid Dysaphis plantaginea is one of the most important leaf sucking pests in pome fruit. As damage, caused by an infestation of a relatively small number of fundatrices in spring, easily exceeds the economic threshold level, pest management is crucial. Besides the use of IPM-compatible pesticides, natural enemies can play an additional role in controlling aphids. The solitary endoparasitoid Ephedrus persicae is the dominant parasitic wasp attacking rosy apple aphid. As this parasitoid develops later than its host, control is determined by the population density and the parasitising efficiency of the wasp. The population increase within a season is determined by generation turnover and parasitizing capacity, a factor poorly understood in E. persicae. To be able to estimate the number of wasps required for successful control the parasitic behaviour was studied in semi-field circumstances. Artificially infested colonies of rosy apple aphid on apple trees grown in a greenhouse, were covered with cages of gauze in which young, mated female parasitic wasps were released. The number of aphids (alatae, apterae and mummies) as well as the number of adult parasitic wasps were recorded weekly until the end of infestation or parasitation. This test method allowed a comparison of the parasitizing efficacy of Ephedrus with that of the well-studied parasitoid Aphelinus mali (Haldeman), that efficiently controls the woolly aphid Eriosoma lanigerum (Hausmann) (3).
