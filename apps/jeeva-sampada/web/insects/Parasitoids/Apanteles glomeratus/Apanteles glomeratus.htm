 Biology/ Ecology / Behaviour

Adults mate and the females lay eggs, in most cases, immediately after emerging from their cocoons. Eggs are deposited into larvae of caterpillars about 25-55 per larva soon after mating. A female lays about 120-210 eggs during her life. Apanteles  larvae emerge after about 15-20 days and spin their cocoons on or near the host which dies when the wasps emerge. The life cycle, from egg to adult, is approximately 25-30 days, depending on the temperature.

Pupa (Male/Female): The pupae are in an irregular mass of yellow silken cocoons attached to the host larva or to plant leaves. 

Adult (Male/Female):

Apanteles glomeratus adults are small (about 6 mm), dark wasps and similar to flying ants or tiny flies. They have two pairs of wings, the hindwings being smaller than the forewings, and chewing-lapping type of mouth parts. The antennae are about 1.65 mm long, and curved upward. The abdomen of the female narrows to a downward curving extension called the ovipositor with which female lays eggs.

Miscellaneous Information

Effects of neem seed extracts on Apanteles glomeratus L.  a parasitoid of Pieris brassicae L.: Neem seed extract was applied orally and topically to 4th and 5th instar  Pieris brassicae L. larvae which contained larvae of the parasitoid, Apanteles glomeratus L., and also topically to Apanteles glomeratus L. cocoons. As well as heavy parasitoid mortality due to premature host death, Apanteles glomeratus L. suffered increased mortality at all subsequent life stages and deformities in emerged adults. Neem seed extract is capable of having direct negative effects on Apanteles glomeratus populations. It is suggested that care should be taken in the use of such extracts and that assessment of potential impact on parasitoid populations should take place beforehand (2).

Ovipositional Experience of the Gregarious Parasitoid, Apanteles glomeratus, Influencing Her Discrimination of the Host Larvae, Pieris rapae crucivora:The gregarious parasitoid, Apanteles glomeratus, oviposits in the larvae of Pieris rapae crucivora. Scientist investigated to find how the previous experiences of the wasp in oviposition influence the degree of host discrimination. The oviposition time and the number of eggs laid in a host had a positive correlationship. Therefore oviposition time was used as an index of the number of eggs laid. The wasp could innately discriminate between the parasitized and unparasitied hosts and laid fewer eggs into the former than into the latter. The more the wasps had oviposited in unparasitized hosts just before encountering the parasitized host, the less number of eggs they laid into the latter; moreover they tended to reject the parasitized hosts. The wasp also reduced the number of eggs laid in an unparasitized host when she oviposited in them successively at short intervals. These results indicate that the wasp could estimate the density of unparasitized hosts by the frequency of oviposition and could control the number of eggs laid. These phenomena were explained by the assumption that the wasp, based on the presumed density of the unparasitized hosts, behaves to maximize her own inclusive fitness throughout her life (3).
