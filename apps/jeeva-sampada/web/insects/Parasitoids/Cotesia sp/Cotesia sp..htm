 Biology/ Ecology / Behaviour

Female wasps lay eggs inside a caterpillar where the larvae of the wasp will then develop. This caterpillar may continue to live for some time, but eventually it dies. When the larvae finally emerge from the caterpillar they pupate in small silken cocoons that are usually found on or near the dead caterpillar (2).

This species is a small (2.5-2.6 mm,) wasp in the family Braconidae. It is most often observed in the field as a tiny white cocoon (3-3.5 mm) attached to leaves of crucifer crops. Sometimes it is found next to the dead body of its DBM caterpillar host as in. Its life cycle lasts about 13 days from egg to adult emergence. The adults live much longer (28-35 days) if flowering plants and nectar are available. Cotesia sp is the dominant larval parasitoid of DBM. Parasitism has been reported as high as 88% but usually ranges around 20-40% of DBM larvae. It has been found during every month of the year in parts of northern Thailand where crucifer crops are grown year-round. It has even been found in some fields in the North where harsh organophosphate pesticides were being used. This suggests that some populations of Cotesia sp may have become resistant to pesticides used in certain areas. Cotesia sp is also more abundant in areas with overlapping plantings of crucifer crops. It is not unusual to find one or more Cotesia sp cocoons per plant in abandoned crucifer plantings in these areas. Unfortunately, in most cases successful biological control of DBM in lowland Southeast Asia has not been accomplished by naturally-occurring or introduced Cotesia sp alone. Although field collections indicating Cotesia sp parasitism at or above 50% are fairly common, this usually occurs after DBM populations have already reached damaging levels. Good control is possible, however, when Cotesia is present and safe control products like Bt (i.e., Florbac, Xentari, Bactospeine, etc.) are used. Successful biological control has also been achieved in lowland crucifer fields with this species when multiple releases were made during the growing season. Cotesia sp releases within nylon nethouses have also successfully controlled DBM . Biological control with Cotesia sp or other parasitoids is easier with crops like broccoli or cauliflower compared with cabbage or Chinese kale. Broccoli, cauliflower, or crucifer seed crops can tolerate more leaf damage than cabbage or Chinese kale (3).

Adult (Male/Female): Cotesia adults are small (about 7 mm), dark wasps and resemble flying ants or tiny flies. They have two pairs of wings, the hind wings being smaller than the forewings, and chewing-lapping mouth parts. The antennae are about 1.5 mm long, and curved (not elbowed) upward. The abdomen of the female narrows to a downward curving extension called the ovipositor with which she lays eggs. The pupae are in an irregular mass of yellow silken cocoons attached to the host larva or to plant leaves (4).

Conservation

Cotesia species are very sensitive to pesticides and will quickly disappear when chemical insecticides are used. To conserve Cotesia it is therefore important to avoid using insecticides when possible. If insecticides are used, this could even result in an increase of pest insects because their natural enemies are killed (2).
