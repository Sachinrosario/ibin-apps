 Biology/ Ecology / Behaviour

Trichogramma chilotraeae adults are extremely small. The female adult lays her eggs on other moths' eggs. First, she examines the eggs by antennal drumming, then drills into the eggs with her ovipositor, and lays one or more eggs inside the moth's eggs. She usually stays on or near the host eggs until all or most of them are parasitized. When the parasitized moth's eggs turn black, the larvae parasites develop within the host eggs. The larva eats the contents of the moth's eggs. Adults emerge about 5-10 days later depending on the temperature. Adults can live up to 14 days after emergence. Female adults can lay up to 300 eggs (6).

Conservation

Trichogramma  species differ in their searching behavior, host preferences, response to environmental conditions, and suitability in biological control uses. The timing of Trichogramma chilotraeae  releases in the field is important. Non-parasitism could be due to the use of less suitable Trichogramma chilotraeae  strains to the host pests, environmental conditions, and untimely release of parasitoids. It is best to release of parasitoids at the beginning of a pest infestation (when moths are first seen in the field), followed by regular releases until a natural breeding population of Trichogramma is established.

An example of this approach is the corn borer control. The first release should be during the first appearance of moths and corn borers' eggs in the cornfields. Weekly releases thereafter are to ensure the Trichogramma chilotraeae  population build-up and parasitism occurrence. The build-up of the parasitoids depends on the presence of the pest or alternative hosts and food for adults. It is important to regulary monitor pest population, egg parasitism (parasitized eggs are black in color), and the larval infestation. Trichogramma chilotraeae  is released as pupae in parasitized host eggs. The pupae can be pasted on cards or put in various containers. To be successful in the field, food, host eggs and shelter must be available.

Modify cropping practices by practicing crop rotation and by planting cultivars which are favorable to Trichogramma chilotraeae  population build-up such as wild carrots, dill, golden rod, leguminous plants, and flowering vegetables. Adults feed only on nectar, pollen, and honeydew. Many of these species are found naturally occurring in agricultural and garden habitats. Many adult parasitoids and predators benefit from sources of nectar and the protection provided by refuges such as hedgerows, cover crops, and weedy borders. Avoid indiscriminate use of hazardous pesticides to ensure their presence in agricultural fields (6).

Miscellaneous Information

Method of application: Prior to field application, allow the adult parasites (Trichogramma) to emerge from the container (any glass container with cover). Place the container under ambient temperature and free from ants. In releasing the adult parasites, open and slightly tap the container while going around the field. At the center of the field, randomly distribute the strips by clipping these on the plants.

Cautions;

  1. Do not place the Trichogramma strips in an open container and keep away from hot places and ants.

  2. Refrigerate the strips (in the vegetable bin/crisper only) if not applied as scheduled.

  3. Remove the Trichogramma strips from the refrigerator a day before field application.

  4. Ask the distributing center for the date of application (5).
