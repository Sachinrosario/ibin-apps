 Biology/ Ecology / Behaviour

The egg incubation period of Paederus fuscipes Curtis was 5.3 days with an 85.4% hatching of eggs. The average duration of larvae, prepupae and pupae was 7.5, 1.7 and 2.5 days, respectively. Sex ratio in the laboratory reared insects was 0.9:1 favouring females, almost similar to the field collected population (0.92:1). The longevity of male ranged from 37 to 72 days and that of females from 45 to 95 days. The maximum fecundity was 27 with an average of 21.3 eggs (2).

Miscellaneous Information

Paederus fuscipes dermatitis is a self-healing blistering disorder of the skin caused by a small insect belonging to genus Paederus, family Staphylinidae, order Coleoptera. Crushing Paederus fuscipes on the skin causes acute dermatitis within 24 hours, corresponding in shape and dimensions to the area affected by the substance released (pederin). The acute vesicular lesions become crusted and scaly within a few days and heal completely in 10-12 days, with a transitory postinflammatory hypercromic patch. Twenty consecutive cases of Paederus fuscipes dermatitis at different stages were studied histologically by routine light microscopy. The pederin causes a spectrum of histopathologic changes ranging from acute epidermal necrosis and blistering in acute stages, to marked acanthosis with mitotic figures in the late stages. Paederus fuscipes dermatitis is an entomological model of irritant contact dermatitis, having histopathologic features of intraepidermal and subepidermal blistering, epidermal necrosis and acantholysis. The presence of some acantholytic foci, relatively far from the foci of clinically involved skin, in four of the cases considered suggests a possible role of pederin in inducing acantholysis indirectly. Acantholysis is probably caused by the release of epidermal proteases (3).

 The predator was active throughout the year and maintained sizeable population on these crops. Highest activity of the predator was observed on sunnhemp (Crotalaria juncea L.) and groundnut (Arachis hypogea L.) during June (40.5 and 59.9 beetles per sq. rn, respectively) on okra (Abelmoschus esculentus Moench) during July (41 beetles/sq. m) and on paddy (Oryza sativa L.) during October (40 beetles/sq. m). Moist conditions and crop phenology appeared to be conducive for the development of the predator (4).
